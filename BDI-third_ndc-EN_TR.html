Third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) of Burundi

Executive Summary

Burundi, a signatory to the Paris Agreement since 2015, adopts its NDC 3.0 in 2025 in order to consolidate achievements, correct weaknesses and raise ambition for greenhouse gas emission reductions, in both mitigation and adaptation. The process is led by the Ministry of the Environment, Agriculture and Livestock (MINEAGRIE), in a context marked by high vulnerability to the effects of climate change, strong agricultural dependence (90% of the active population), high land pressure, a still-emerging economy and dynamic demographics.

Burundi's NDC 3.0 is a comprehensive strategic framework for the transition to low-carbon, resilient and inclusive development. It articulates on emission reductions, resilience strengthening, mobilisation of innovative financing and social integration, in order to contribute to building a sustainable and prosperous Burundi in line with the Vision 2040–2060, the revised National Development Plan (NDP) 2018–2027, the initial NAP (2023) and the SDGs.

Heightened Climate Ambition

On mitigation, Burundi strengthens its commitment to climate change through its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), targeting a 23% reduction in emissions by 2035 compared to the reference scenario (BAU). This objective combines an unconditional reduction of 3% and a conditional reduction of 20%, supported by international financing and solid partnerships.

Although the country transitioned to net positive emissions in 2023, it counts on the restoration of the forest sink through increased forest cover, coordinated policies, consistent actions — in particular the promotion of green energy, low-carbon technologies, strengthened financing and a high-performing MRV system — to return to a low-carbon and resilient trajectory.

Beyond mitigation, Burundi also reaffirms its commitment to more strongly integrating adaptation and resilience at the heart of development planning and budgeting at national, sectoral and local levels. This is consistent with the NAP process launched in 2014 which led to the initial NAP in 2023; this will be continued with the elaboration of the next NAP by 2027.

Vulnerability and Adaptation Priorities

Burundi is highly exposed to climate hazards affecting all vital sectors: agriculture-livestock-fisheries, forestry, water, health, energy, ecosystems, infrastructure and waste.

Adaptation rests on five major pillars:

  • Anti-erosion efforts, sustainable land, forest and water resource management;
  • Early warning and prevention of climate risks (hazards, disasters and epidemics);
  • Climate-resilient technologies and agricultural varieties;
  • Infrastructure adapted to climate hazards;
  • Social protection and inclusion (indigenous peoples, gender, youth, disability, the link between human mobility, environment and climate change).

Water is placed at the heart of adaptation, through integrated watershed management, the preservation of buffer zones, irrigation, transboundary governance and hill reservoirs.

Mitigation

The mitigation measures aim to:

  • Increase the country's forest cover;
  • Promote clean energy (hydroelectricity, solar, biogas); Energy: biogas, solar, rehabilitated hydroelectric plants, improved cookstoves, improvement of energy efficiency in buildings and transport systems;
  • Reduce the use of wood fuel through improved cookstoves;
  • Modernise energy and transport infrastructure;
  • Encourage sustainable forestry practices (REDD+);
  • Strengthen the management of protected areas and restore others;
  • Promote electric mobility;
  • Improve livestock feed composition;
  • Increase the production of organo-mineral fertilisers.

Adaptation

The adaptation measures concern the following main sectors:

  • Agriculture and livestock: climate information, early warning systems, irrigation, agricultural insurance, resilient practices, agroecology, crop diversification, fisheries and fish production, training of youth and IDPs;
  • Natural resources: anti-erosion efforts, reforestation, management of aquatic ecosystems, conservation financing, restoration of wetlands;
  • Integrated water management: collection and storage, integrated management (anti-erosion, watershed development), participatory governance, hydraulic infrastructure, rainwater harvesting and wastewater reuse;
  • Ecosystems and biodiversity: habitat protection, restoration of degraded ecosystems, including fishery areas;
  • Health: control of vector-borne diseases (epidemic prevention and treatment), integration of SRH/GBV, mobile services for displaced persons, adaptation of health infrastructure to changing climate conditions;
  • Infrastructure: climate-resilient standards, dredging, road and port maintenance, urban planning, construction of dykes to protect against erosion and flooding;
  • Waste: sorting, composting, circular economy, producer responsibility, pollution control;
  • Disaster risk management at national, provincial, communal and local levels.

Financing and Budget

The overall cost of implementing NDC 3.0 is estimated at USD 8,363,913,000, of which USD 3,943,911,000 for adaptation and USD 4,420,002,000 for mitigation.

Financing will come from a mixed mechanism: existing UNFCCC mechanisms, Green Climate Fund, Adaptation Fund, Loss and Damage Fund, international mechanisms (GEF, AfDB, EU and others), public-private partnerships (PPPs), carbon markets, environmental taxes, bilateral cooperation, national resources including in-kind contributions from populations and mobilisation of South-South cooperation.

Governance and Monitoring and Evaluation

An integrated institutional mechanism is planned, with:

  • Monitoring and evaluation actions with sectoral indicators (water, food security, ecosystems, resilience);
  • MRV mechanism and MEL for transparency and accountability;
  • Community participation and learning loops.

The approach rests on participatory planning, multi-sectoral coordination and alignment with national policies (Burundi Vision 2040–2060, NDP 2018–2027, SDGs, initial NAP 2023). The next NAP (2027) will align with NDC 3.0 and contribute to NDC 4.0.

Inclusion and Equity

NDC 3.0 places the human being at the centre of its design and implementation:

  • Women: economic empowerment, access to clean energy, reproductive health, leadership and protection against GBV;
  • Indigenous peoples: economic empowerment, accessibility and integration in the climate response;
  • Children and Youth: climate education, green training, keeping girls in school;
  • Persons with disabilities: accessibility and integration in the climate response;
  • Displaced populations: planned mobility management, safe relocation, green employment and basic services.

In connection with the increased engagement of youth in climate actions, a National School Feeding Programme (NSFP) will be developed.

The NSFP acts as a triple lever: Adaptation (food security, household resilience), Mitigation (reduction of wood waste and degradation of forest ecosystems, improved/clean cookstoves), Human Capital (nutrition, education, gender equality).

International and National Alignment

NDC 3.0 aligns with: The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs): SDG 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13 and 15. The national policies: initial NAP (2023); Burundi Vision 2040–2060 and the National Development Plan (NDP) revised. The global climate agreements: UNFCCC, Paris Agreement, REDD+, Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Management.

The methodology followed for emission calculations: IPCC Guidelines, IPCC 2006 Software and LEAP for emission projections in the Energy sector. The IPCC 2006 emission estimation tool was used to assess greenhouse gas emissions from anthropogenic activities planned in the NDC. In addition, the global warming potential (GWP) from the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report was used to convert emissions to CO₂ equivalent where CO₂=1; CH₄=28; N₂O=265; HFC-134a=1430; HFC-404A=3921.6; HFC-407C=1773.85; HFC-507A=3985; HFC-410A=2087.5; HFC-32=675.

Consideration of national context, monitoring and evaluation, and mobilisation

The national circumstances specify that NDC 3.0 was formulated on the basis of the new administrative division of Burundi, which currently comprises 5 provinces and 42 communes, and the ecological context characterised by 5 eco-climatic regions. Socio-economic indicators taken from an INSBU publication entitled "Indicators of Sustainable Human Development" were used. Given the impact of climate change on health, the epidemiological and health profile of the country was addressed; it is characterised by the recurrence of epidemics such as malaria, cholera, tuberculosis and measles, as well as the prevalence of HIV/AIDS and malnutrition in certain communes. The risk profile highlights the impact of disasters on populations and infrastructure with figures from 2018 to 2025 and specifies the main vulnerability factors. The political, legal and regulatory orientation frameworks show several legal and regulatory frameworks put in place while specifying the institutional mechanisms for addressing the challenges related to adaptation to climate change.

Projection assumptions

For the Energy sector, the projection assumptions are based on the population growth rate, economic growth (GDP), and the country's policy and strategic orientation measures relating to socio-economic and environmental development. For the Agriculture sector, the key parameters include biomass expansion factors, carbon-to-biomass ratios, annual forest biomass growth rates and sector-specific emission factors for different forest types, with land-use mapping data providing the activity data needed for calculations. For the LULUCF sector, the key parameters include biomass expansion factors, carbon-to-biomass ratios and reference-year activity data. For Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU), the projection assumptions are based on economic growth and the rate of industrialisation. For the Waste sector, the assumptions and parameters of the reference scenario of the Third National Communication (TNC) for 2025 and 2030 were retained. The projection assumptions are based on the rate of population, national economic and urbanisation growth.

Monitoring, evaluation and learning

The national MRV (Measurement, Reporting, Verification) system is strengthened to monitor the implementation of mitigation measures (GHG) and adaptation measures (resilience indicators). Strengthening institutional capacities to ensure an effective transition to a low-emission and resilient economy is essential. The implementation of Burundi's NDC 3.0 requires mobilising national resources, external financing, domestic innovation and adequate capacity building. The First Biennial Transparency Report (2025) is strengthened and the national MRV system contributes to the transparency of climate action.

In what way is NDC 3.0 ambitious and innovative?

NDC 3.0 is ambitious and innovative: despite the progressive loss of Burundi's absorption capacity, it aims to strengthen mitigation measures, increase the carbon sink and proposes many adaptation actions that are among national priorities. It has highlighted several cross-cutting aspects such as gender, women, youth, children and persons with disabilities, and the link between human mobility, environment and climate change. The impacts of disasters (torrential rains, floods, hail, landslides, violent winds) on populations and infrastructure (roads, buildings, dwellings and schools) have been identified and measures to address them have been proposed.

1. Introduction

During the 21st Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change held in Paris on 12 December 2015, the "Party" countries signed an agreement aimed at establishing a framework agreement to ensure a low-carbon future. In application of Article 3 of the Paris Agreement, countries resolved to submit their commitments through Nationally Determined Contributions every five years.

In this context, like most countries, Burundi submitted to the Secretariat of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change the Intended Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC) in 2015 (NDC 1.0), which became an NDC in 2018, and the second generation NDC in 2021 (NDC 2.0).

Through the presentation of NDC 3.0, Burundi commits to consolidating the achievements and lessons learned from the implementation of commitments made in previous NDCs, while increasing the targets and actions aimed at addressing the causes of emissions (through mitigation) as well as the consequences and impacts of climate change (through adaptation). Particular attention will be given to low-carbon actions.

  • The first part concerns the introduction and presentation of the document;
  • The second part concerns the national circumstances with a particular focus on the profiles, risks, vulnerabilities and formal frameworks of NDC 3.0;
  • The third part concerns the lessons from NDC 1.0 and 2.0 and the process of elaborating NDC 3.0;

  • The fifth part concerns the contribution to adaptation to climate change;

  • The ninth part concerns the conclusions.

2. National Circumstances

2.1 Geographic Context

Figure 2. Administrative map of Burundi

Burundi is a landlocked country in Central Africa located between 2°20' and 4°27' South latitude and between 28°50' and 30°53' East longitude. The country shares its borders with the Democratic Republic of Congo to the West, the Republic of Rwanda to the North and the United Republic of Tanzania to the East and South. The country covers an area of approximately 27,834 km². The relief is characterised by a Congo-Nile divide ridge that splits the country into two slopes: to the West, the Lake Tanganyika slope with its coastal plains and the north-western depressions, and to the East, the Nile basin slope with the central plateaux and the eastern depressions. The new administrative division of Burundi currently comprises 5 provinces and 42 communes.

Figure 3. Distribution of average annual rainfall (IGEBU)

The ecological context is characterised by 5 eco-climatic regions. Average annual rainfall varies from less than 750 mm in the Lake Tanganyika depression and the eastern depressions to more than 1,700 mm on the Congo-Nile divide ridge. Average annual temperatures vary between 17°C and 23°C depending on altitude.

2.2 Ecological Context

Burundi has significant ecological diversity comprising different types of land use. Agriculture constitutes the main land use with 2,017,281 ha.1 The country also has forests, wetlands, grasslands, lakes and watercourses, mining and quarrying areas, urban areas and human settlements (34,786 ha).2

The country is crossed by numerous watercourses, the main ones being the Rusizi, the Malagarazi, the Ruvubu and the Kagera. Lake Tanganyika, one of the deepest in the world at 773 m, constitutes a major natural resource for the country. Forest ecosystems play a crucial role in climate regulation, biodiversity conservation and watershed protection.

2.3 Demographic Context

The demographic balance shows that the Burundian population grew from 9.4 million in 2013 to 13.1 million in 2023. In 2024, the General Census of population, housing and agriculture counted 12,332,788 inhabitants, of whom 5,901,069 were male and 6,431,719 female.

Demographic analysis is important because it provides useful information that can be used to make sound decisions in business, government and social services, among others. It helps to understand the characteristics of a population and how it might evolve in the future, which is important for decision-making.

The total fertility rate (TFR), also known as the total fertility index (TFI), is a key demographic indicator that reflects the average number of children a woman would have during her reproductive life, taking into account current age-specific fertility rates. It fell from 5.3 in 2013 to 3.6 in 2023. High fertility (above 2.5) can lead to rapid population growth, with implications for resources and social services.

The crude death rate (CDR) is the number of deaths in a population relative to the total population, often expressed per 1,000 inhabitants over a given period (generally one year). Analysing and interpreting the crude death rate is essential for understanding the health of a population and demographic dynamics. A low CDR (below 8 per 1,000) generally indicates good public health, effective health systems and high life expectancy, while a high CDR (above 10 per 1,000) may signal public health problems, epidemics, conflicts or other crises. Analysing the crude death rate is therefore crucial for understanding the health of a population and guiding health policies.

Figure 4. Demographic balance (INSBU 2023)

The proportion of young people generally refers to the proportion of the total population that belongs to a specific age group, often defined as those aged 15 to 24 years, though this may vary by context. Analysing the proportion of young people in a population makes it possible to understand demographic challenges and plan adapted policies.

2.4 Socio-Economic Context

Figure 6. Trends in socio-economic indicators

Burundi is one of the poorest countries in the world. The economy is essentially agricultural, with approximately 90% of the active population in this sector. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita remains very low. Principal exports include coffee, tea, cotton and minerals. The country faces heavy land pressure, high youth unemployment and dependence on imports for petroleum and industrial products.

2.5 Epidemiological and Health Profile

The epidemiological and health profile of the country is characterised by the recurrence of cholera and measles epidemics as well as the prevalence of malaria, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS and malnutrition in certain communes.

In 2024, the national incidence rate of malaria was 458.3 cases per 1,000 inhabitants, and pregnant women and children under 5 years of age constitute the most affected segment of the population. From January 2023 to October 2025, the country has been in a cholera epidemic with a cumulative total of 2,800 cases and 17 deaths. The SMART 2022 survey estimates the global acute malnutrition rate among children under 5 at 4.8%. The areas most exposed to malnutrition are those in the Northern depression, the central plateaux, the Congo-Nile divide ridge and the Imbo plain.

The various risk assessments conducted at national level have highlighted the fact that the occurrence of cholera and malaria outbreaks, as well as malnutrition in high-risk communes, is strongly linked to the effects of climate change facing the country, which manifests in certain communes as heavy rains causing stagnant water — a source of disease — or prolonged sunshine causing water deficits, which are a source of declining agricultural productivity in the central plateaux and the Kumoso and Bugeresera depressions (localities in the North and North-East of Burundi).

2.6 Greenhouse Gas Emissions Profile

Figure 7. Net emissions (BTR 2025)

Burundi's greenhouse gas emissions profile is dominated by the Agriculture and LULUCF (Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry) sectors, which account for the largest share of national emissions. The Energy sector is experiencing rapid growth linked to economic development and demographic growth. Burundi's net emissions transitioned from negative to positive in 2023, reflecting a reduction in forest absorption capacity.

2.7 Climate Risk and Vulnerability Profile

2.7.1 Disaster Risks Linked to Climate Change

Burundi has a national multi-hazard mapping system that provides an integrated assessment of the main natural and climate hazards causing disasters on its territory. Implemented at national scale and covering all provinces, this mapping analyses in combination the dimensions of hazard, exposure, vulnerability and probabilistic risk in order to identify the areas most exposed to disasters. It accounts for the main climatic and geophysical hazards, including floods, torrential rains, landslides, violent winds and earthquakes. The results — comprising more than a hundred provincial maps, a national multi-hazard map and a risk atlas — constitute a reference tool for development planning, land-use planning and the systematic integration of disaster exposure risk into public policies.

An interactive digital platform, hosted by the University of Burundi and accessible at https://georisques.ub.edu.bi/#/, allows users to consult, visualise and download these data, thereby facilitating evidence-based decision-making and the prioritisation of adaptation and disaster-risk-reduction actions at national and local levels.

Figure 8. Multi-hazard mapping (IOM)

According to publications from the national disaster risk management platform, the impacts of climate change and disasters are enormous. These highlight the typology of disasters (torrential rains, floods, hail, landslides, violent winds), people affected from 2018 to 2025 (more than 1,103,773 with 201,396 displaced, 166 deaths) and various destruction (more than 20,225 houses and 895 classrooms totally destroyed).

Figure 9. Climate change impacts and disasters in Burundi

Burundi is exposed to multiple climate risks: torrential rains, floods, hail, landslides and violent winds. These phenomena have significant impacts on populations and infrastructure (roads, buildings, dwellings and schools). Data from 2018 to 2025 document the growing magnitude of these disasters and their humanitarian consequences.

2.7.2 Main Vulnerability Factors

The main vulnerability factors identified are the following:

  1. Dependence on Agriculture: a large part of the population depends on agriculture for its livelihood, making it particularly vulnerable to climate impacts. The agricultural sector contributes on average 39.4% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and provides more than 86.1% of total employment (mostly informal and seasonal jobs), compared with 10.4% for services and 3.5% for industry (revised NDP Burundi 2018–2027 and its PAP 2023–2027).
  2. Poverty: the high level of poverty in Burundi limits communities' capacity to adapt to and be resilient to climate change, making it difficult to access appropriate resources and technologies.
  3. Limited Infrastructure: insufficient infrastructure, particularly in rural areas, makes it difficult to manage risks associated with extreme climate events.
  4. Awareness and Education: a lack of awareness, knowledge and training on climate adaptation practices can limit communities' ability to respond effectively to climate change.
  5. Increasing Vulnerability of Specific Groups: women (especially female-headed households), persons with disabilities, Batwa, youth, children (in school or not), as well as displaced, migrant or relocated/repatriated persons, are particularly vulnerable, and their vulnerability increases with climate change. These groups are supported by the community, administration and NGOs, and must be made aware of climate change impacts and integrated into adaptation planning processes.

2.8 Political, Legal and Regulatory Orientation Frameworks

Burundi has developed several legal and regulatory frameworks to address climate challenges. These frameworks include national environmental legislation, sectoral policies (agriculture, energy, forests, water) and national development strategies. The legal framework of NDC 3.0 draws in particular on constitutional provisions and environmental laws in force, reinforced by international conventions ratified by Burundi (UNFCCC, Convention on Biological Diversity, United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification).

The Burundi Vision of an emerging country by 2040 and a developed country by 2060, the revised National Development Plan (NDP) 2018–2027, the initial National Adaptation Plan (2023) and the sectoral strategies constitute the pillars of the political alignment of NDC 3.0.

2.9 Institutional Framework

The Ministry of the Environment, Agriculture and Livestock (MINEAGRIE) is the supervisory institution for the implementation of NDC 3.0 and coordinates inter-ministerial actions. The institutional framework comprises:

  1. The NDC Steering Committee: the strategic coordination body that ensures the supervision of NDC 3.0 implementation, in accordance with Presidential Decree number 100/032 of 27 March 2025.3
  2. Sectoral Ministries: they implement the mitigation and adaptation measures in their respective sectors (energy, water, health, infrastructure, etc.).
  3. Local authorities: communes and provinces play a key role in operationalising measures at the local level.
  4. Hydro-climatic monitoring systems: the country has a hydro-climatic monitoring and forecasting system. The data from monitoring systems that allow monitoring of climate change impacts and the effectiveness of adaptation measures are provided by IGEBU.

3. NDC 3.0 Elaboration Process

3.1 Lessons Learned from NDC 1.0 and 2.0

NDC 1.0 (2015) and NDC 2.0 (2021) allowed Burundi to lay the foundations of its climate commitment. The assessment of the implementation of these commitments revealed an implementation rate of approximately 40% for NDC 1.0, primarily due to financial constraints and institutional challenges. The main lessons include the need for better institutional anchoring, greater mobilisation of climate finance, and better integration of local communities into the planning and implementation of measures.

3.2 Participatory Elaboration Process

The elaboration of NDC 3.0 was conducted through a participatory and inclusive process. The process of elaborating Burundi's Third Nationally Determined Contribution was carried out in a participatory and inclusive manner, under the coordination of the Ministry of Environment, Agriculture and Livestock (MINEAGRIE), through the Technical Multidisciplinary Committee for Inventory Coordination and Development (CTPCDI).3 Regional workshops were organised in different regions of the country, involving more than 426 participants representing sectoral ministries, local authorities, civil society, the private sector, youth organisations and vulnerable groups (women, persons with disabilities, indigenous peoples). The consultations made it possible to collect sectoral and local priorities for mitigation and adaptation.

3.3 Technical Capacity Building

The elaboration of NDC 3.0 benefited from technical capacity building through training on GHG emission modelling tools, in particular on the GACMO software, organised in collaboration with the Regional Hub on Climate Transparency in Central Africa (HuTAC).4 National consultants were also trained on IPCC methods for GHG inventory and on participatory approaches to adaptation planning.

4. Mitigation

4.1 Context and Reference Parameters

Burundi's national GHG inventory covers five sectors: Energy (including transport), Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU), Agriculture, Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF), and Waste. The reference year is 2015, consistent with the First Biennial Transparency Report (BTR 2025).

4.2 Energy Sector

6. Cross-Cutting Issues

6.1 Gender, Youth, Persons with Disabilities, Indigenous Peoples and Local Knowledge Systems

NDC 3.0 fully integrates gender, youth, rights of persons with disabilities and indigenous peoples into the planning and implementation of climate measures. Women, who represent the majority of agricultural actors, are particularly vulnerable to climate change impacts and must be at the heart of adaptation and mitigation strategies. The measures include:

  • Women: economic empowerment, access to clean energy, reproductive health, leadership in climate decision-making and protection against gender-based violence (GBV);
  • Indigenous peoples: recognition and integration of traditional knowledge into climate strategies, economic empowerment, accessibility and integration in the climate response;
  • Children and Youth: climate education, green vocational training, keeping girls in school, active participation in climate decisions;
  • Persons with disabilities: accessibility of climate services and integration in the climate response;
  • Displaced populations: planned mobility management, safe relocation, green employment and access to basic services.

6.2 Displacements Linked to the Adverse Effects of Climate Change

Climate change is one of the main drivers of population displacement in Burundi. In July 2025, the country had 89,114 internally displaced persons (IDPs), of whom 92% were displaced due to natural disasters related to climate change, 35% were children and 56% were women and girls.5 NDC 3.0 takes into account the Kampala Ministerial Declaration on Migration, Environment and Climate Change (KDMECC) and integrates specific measures for:

  • Planned management of climate-related human mobility;
  • Safe and dignified relocation of populations exposed to climate risks;
  • Access to basic services (water, health, education) for IDPs;
  • Green employment and economic reintegration of displaced populations.

6.3 Family Planning and Climate Change

NDC 3.0 recognises the link between demographic growth and climate challenges. The integration of family planning into climate change response strategies aims to:

  • Reduce demographic pressure on natural resources;
  • Improve women's reproductive health, particularly in vulnerable areas;
  • Strengthen access to reproductive health services in communities affected by climate change;
  • Integrate family planning issues into agricultural adaptation programmes.

6.4 National School Feeding Programme (NSFP)

6.4.1 Context and Justification

The National School Feeding Programme (NSFP) is a triple strategic lever for NDC 3.0: it contributes simultaneously to adaptation (food security and household resilience), to mitigation (reduction of wood waste and degradation of forest ecosystems through the use of improved and clean cookstoves), and to human capital (nutrition, education and gender equality).

6.4.2 NSFP Objectives

The NSFP aims to ensure nutritious and regular meals for pupils in primary and secondary schools, as a priority in areas most vulnerable to climate change impacts. It contributes to keeping children, particularly girls, in school during periods of climate stress and food crisis.

6.4.3 Link to Climate Adaptation

The NSFP is part of the climate adaptation strategy by improving the food resilience of rural households, stimulating locally resilient agricultural production, and developing local markets for agricultural products.

6.4.4 Link to Mitigation

The preparation of school meals with improved cookstoves and clean fuels (biogas, briquettes, electricity) reduces the consumption of firewood and associated GHG emissions, while preserving forests.

6.4.5 Implementation and Financing

The NSFP will be developed in connection with the increased engagement of youth in climate actions and will benefit from national and international financing, in particular under climate mechanisms (GCF, Adaptation Fund) and partnerships with international organisations (WFP, UNICEF, FAO).

7.2 Domestic Resources

Burundi will mobilise its national resources to finance the implementation of NDC 3.0, in particular through the national budget, environmental taxes, in-kind contributions from local communities and innovative domestic financing mechanisms. The government commits to progressively increasing budgetary allocations for climate actions.

7.3 External Resources

External financing is essential for the implementation of the conditional measures of NDC 3.0. The main sources of external financing include: the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF), the Adaptation Fund, the Loss and Damage Fund, the African Development Bank (AfDB), the World Bank (WB), the European Investment Bank (EIB), the European Union, bilateral cooperation (KfW, UNDP, etc.) and carbon market mechanisms.

7.4 Carbon Markets

Burundi intends to mobilise financing through carbon market mechanisms, in particular under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. The forestry sector (REDD+) offers significant opportunities for the generation of carbon credits. The country will develop the necessary regulatory frameworks to participate in voluntary and regulated carbon markets.

7.5 Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs)

Public-private partnerships constitute an important lever for mobilising climate investments, particularly in the sectors of renewable energy, resilient infrastructure and sustainable agriculture. The legal framework will be strengthened to facilitate private sector engagement in the implementation of NDC 3.0.

7.6 Capacity Building

The implementation of NDC 3.0 requires significant strengthening of institutional, technical and human capacities. Priority needs include: training of national technicians and experts, strengthening of national institutions (MINEAGRIE, OBPE, IGEBU, INSBU), access to clean and innovative technologies, and the establishment of high-performing climate information systems.

7.7 MRV System (Measurement, Reporting, Verification)

The national MRV (Measurement, Reporting, Verification) system is strengthened to ensure the monitoring of the implementation of mitigation measures (GHG) and adaptation measures (resilience indicators). This system is part of the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) of the Paris Agreement and will enable the production of biennial transparency reports (BTR) and national communications.

9. Conclusion

Burundi's NDC 3.0 represents a firm and ambitious commitment by the country to contribute to the global effort to combat climate change, despite its low GHG emissions and limited resources. It reflects Burundi's determination to pursue a low-carbon and resilient development trajectory, in line with its national priorities and the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

The successful implementation of NDC 3.0 depends on several essential conditions: the mobilisation of national and international financing, the strengthening of institutional and technical capacities, the establishment of a high-performing MRV system, and the commitment of all stakeholders (government, private sector, civil society, local communities, international partners).

Burundi calls on the international community to support its implementation efforts through adequate climate financing, technology transfer and capacity building. With appropriate support, Burundi will be able to achieve its mitigation and adaptation objectives, thereby contributing to the achievement of the global objectives of the Paris Agreement and the SDGs.

NDC 3.0 thus constitutes not only a climate commitment, but also a roadmap for sustainable, inclusive and resilient development in Burundi, placing the human being at the heart of climate solutions.

Annex 4. National Strategic Plan for Adaptation and Mitigation

This annex presents the strategic axes of the national plan for the implementation of NDC 3.0.

A1. Strategic Axis 1: Institutional Strengthening and Climate Governance

This axis aims to strengthen institutional capacities for effective climate governance. The main objectives include:

  • Strengthening of MINEAGRIE's and sectoral ministries' capacities in climate planning;
  • Improvement of inter-ministerial and multi-sectoral coordination;
  • Strengthening of the NDC 3.0 Steering Committee;
  • Development of regulatory and institutional frameworks needed for NDC implementation.

Expected results: national institutions equipped with the necessary capacities to plan, implement, monitor and evaluate climate actions.

A2. Strategic Axis 2: Quality of NDC Implementation

This axis aims to ensure the quality and effectiveness of the implementation of mitigation and adaptation measures. It includes:

  • Development of tools and methodologies for monitoring implementation;
  • Strengthening of sectoral planning systems integrating climate change;
  • Improvement of coordination between national actors and international partners.

A3. Strategic Axis 3: Climate Risk Management

This axis focuses on reducing climate-related disaster risks. The objectives include:

  • Development and strengthening of multi-hazard early warning systems;
  • Integration of disaster risk management into development planning;
  • Strengthening of local communities' capacities to face climate risks;
  • Development of contingency plans at national, provincial and communal levels.

A4. Strategic Axis 4: Research, Development and Technology Transfer

This axis aims to promote innovation and access to climate technologies. It includes:

  • Strengthening of national research capacities on climate technologies;
  • Facilitation of the transfer of clean and innovative technologies;
  • Development of partnerships with national and international research institutions;
  • Promotion of technologies adapted to the local context (improved cookstoves, irrigation systems, drought-resistant varieties).

A5. Strategic Axis 5: Capacity Building

This axis aims to develop the human and institutional capacities for the implementation of NDC 3.0. The priorities include:

  • Training of national technicians and experts on GHG inventory tools, modelling and adaptation planning;
  • Strengthening of capacities of local actors (communes, civil society organisations);
  • Training of youth and women in green jobs;
  • Development of climate education programmes in schools.

A6. Strategic Axis 6: Knowledge Management and Communication

This axis focuses on the management of climate information and communication. It includes:

  • Development of a national climate information platform;
  • Strengthening of climate data collection and analysis systems (IGEBU, INSBU);
  • Public communication and awareness on climate issues;
  • Publication and dissemination of national climate reports (national communications, BTR).

A7. Strategic Axis 7: Human Mobility, Gender, Youth, Persons with Disabilities and Indigenous Peoples

This cross-cutting axis aims to guarantee inclusion and equity in the climate response. It includes:

  • Systematic integration of gender issues into all climate measures;
  • Development of specific programmes for climate-displaced populations;
  • Active participation of youth in climate decisions and implementation;
  • Accessibility and inclusion of persons with disabilities in the climate response;
  • Recognition and integration of traditional knowledge of indigenous peoples.

A8. Strategic Axis 8: Partnerships and Finance Mobilisation

This axis aims to mobilise the resources necessary for the implementation of NDC 3.0. It includes:

  • Development of strategic partnerships with bilateral and multilateral donors;
  • Access to international climate financing mechanisms (GCF, GEF, Adaptation Fund);
  • Development of the climate private sector and PPPs;
  • Exploration of carbon market mechanisms (Article 6 of the Paris Agreement);
  • Mobilisation of national resources (public budget, environmental taxes).

A9. Strategic Axis 9: REDD+ and Forest Mitigation

This axis focuses on reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation. It includes:

  • Implementation of the national REDD+ programme;
  • Development of the Forest Reference Emission Level (FREL);
  • Strengthening of the national forest monitoring system;
  • Promotion of forest carbon sequestration activities;
  • Access to international REDD+ financing.

A10. Strategic Axis 10: Anti-Erosion Efforts and Watershed Management

This axis aims to protect agricultural land and water resources through erosion control. It includes:

  • Watershed management and terrace construction;
  • Planting of live hedges and nitrogen-fixing species;
  • Protection of catchment areas and water sources;
  • Reforestation of degraded areas and bare ridges;
  • Training of farmers in soil conservation techniques.
