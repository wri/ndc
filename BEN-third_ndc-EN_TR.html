EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Republic of Benin submitted its first updated Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 2.0) in October 2021. In accordance with Articles 4.2 and 4.11 of the Paris Agreement, as well as paragraph 24 of decision 1/CP.21, Benin has the honour of submitting its third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) for the period 2026 to 2035 to the CCNUCC Secretariat. As with the previous ones, the preparation of this CDN benefited from broad participation by stakeholders from public and private structures, local authorities and civil society. It is summarised in the following key points. National circumstances Located in West Africa, the Republic of Benin covers 114.763 km² and has a population estimated at 13.224.860 inhabitants in 2025, of whom 79,31% are under 35 years of age. Administratively, the country is divided into twelve (12) departments, subdivided into 77 Municipalities. There are mainly two types of climate in Benin: the subequatorial climate, which characterises the southern region, and the continental tropical climate, which governs the northern region. In socio-economic terms, Benin recorded a growth rate of 8,8% in the third quarter of 2025, driven by all sectors of activity, with increases of 6,2% in the primary sector (supported by agriculture), 9,9% in the secondary sector and 10,0% in the tertiary sector. In 2022, 36,2% of the population was affected by poverty, compared with 38,5% in 2019. The scale of underemployment (72,9%) is also notable. Benin is moreover confronted with major challenges, such as the adverse effects of climate change, the worsening security threat in the north of the country, youth unemployment, persistent social inequalities and the shortcomings of the education and health systems.

Mitigation

Current greenhouse gas emissions and overall mitigation objective

Benin's total Greenhouse Gas (GES) emissions excluding the forestry sector stood in 2023, according to the GES inventory, at 24,54 Mega tonne CO2-Equivalent (Mt E-CO2), i.e. about 1,95 tonnes E-CO2 per capita, up by about 30% compared with the level reached in 2018. These emissions come from the energy (36,86%), agriculture (38,44%), waste (1,86%) and industrial processes (22,84%) sectors. Taking into account the Forestry and other Land Use (FAT) sector, net GES emissions are estimated at 106,09 Mt E-CO2. In terms of projection, if the status quo is maintained, the trend of overall emissions (excluding FAT) shows an increase of about 73% over the period 2023-2035 (Figure 1). Total cumulative overall GES emissions over the period 2026-2035, without any intervention, would be close to 367,8 Mt E-CO2 (FAT sector excluded). These emissions would come 57% from the energy sector, 31,5% from agriculture, 4,5% from the PIUP sector and 2% from the waste sector. The remaining emissions concern vegetation fires (0,09%) and hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), 4,91%. For the coming years, the measures envisaged in this CDN 3.0, in the Agriculture, Energy, PIUP and Waste sectors (see tables 5, 6, 7 and 8) are likely to help reduce cumulative GES emissions (excluding FAT) by about 120,7 Mt E-CO2 compared with the reference scenario, i.e. a reduction of the order of 32,8% over the period 2026-2035; a considerable increase compared with the ambition of 20,15% (48,75 Mt E-CO2) for Benin's CDN2.0. The expected net reductions in GES emissions (including the FAT sector) amount to 590,8 Mt E-CO2 compared with the reference scenario, i.e. 33,8%. Over the period 2026 to 2030 the expected cumulative net reductions (including FAT) are estimated at 222,5 Mt E-CO2, i.e. 27,6%).

Climate change mitigation objectives and measures

On the basis of existing strategies, plans and programmes, the key sectoral objectives and climate change mitigation measures are identified and set out in tables 5, 6, 7 and 8. In total, 32 mitigation measures have been identified. These measures will be implemented through 180 projects, of which 120 are conditional. The energy sector alone accounts for 22 measures and 70 projects. It should be noted that the emissions assessment covers 87 mitigation projects, of which 43 are conditional. All the unconditional projects will make it possible to reduce cumulative emissions over the period 2026 to 2030 by 34,1 Mt E-CO2 (excluding FAT), compared with the reference scenario, i.e. 21,1%. Between 2026 and 2035, the reduction in cumulative emissions under the unconditional scenario excluding FAT will be 93,7 Mt E-CO2, i.e. 25,5%. Taking into account the Forestry and other Land Use sector, these cumulative net emission reductions are estimated at 192,2 Mt E-CO2 (23,8%) and 463,1 Mt E-CO2 (26,5%) respectively over the periods 2026 to 2030 and 2026 to 2035.

Implementation strategies, programmes and projects

The implementation of sectoral mitigation activities (agriculture, energy, forestry and waste) under the CDN will build on existing strategies, programmes and projects, as well as on future programmes and projects that may be developed and implemented over the period 2026 to 2035. Adaptation Although its contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions is marginal, Benin is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. This makes adaptation a central pillar of its resilient development strategy. Benin has therefore put in place a comprehensive and robust legal, policy and strategic framework that gives priority to adaptation in eight (08) key sectors: agriculture, water resources, forestry, coastal zone, tourism, energy, health, urban planning and infrastructure. Despite the efforts made, th Benin shows a low level of readiness (score: 0,342 in 2023), ranking 131 worldwide, which orients the adaptation measures of the CDN 3.0 towards the following sectoral priorities:

Agriculture

Agriculture is a major pillar of Benin's local and national economy. It ensures food and nutrition security, and contributed 1,5 percentage points to national GDP in the third quarter of 2025. It employs about 70% of the population and accounts for 72,9% of export earnings. However, it depends heavily on extreme climatic conditions and remains particularly vulnerable to hazards such as late and violent rains, floods, dry spells, heat waves, violent winds and extreme river flooding. Accordingly, the 30 sectoral projects and the 51 projects led by the Municipalities, identified and included in this CDN 3.0, aim to implement the following priorities for the agricultural sector: - the development and dissemination of agricultural technical practices adapted to the new climatic constraints, in particular in the most exposed agroecological zones; - the promotion of sustainable agricultural practices, notably mulching, aimed at improving the management of soils and water resources; and - the strengthening of integrated crop and livestock systems of the agropastoral type, with a view to increasing the resilience of production systems to the current and future impacts of climate change.

Forestry and other Land Use (FAT)

Benin's forestry sector is an essential pillar of the national economy. It generates significant revenue, supports employment for local communities and provides essential ecosystem services, such as carbon sequestration and hydrological regulation. However, these ecosystems are highly exposed to the effects of climate change, such as recurrent floods, violent rains, prolonged droughts and the accelerated degradation of habitats. To these climate pressures are added persistent anthropogenic factors, such as extensive agriculture, fuelwood harvesting, charcoal production, livestock rearing and urban expansion, which heighten vulnerability and compromise the resilience of forest resources. The 10 sectoral projects and the 37 others initiated by the Municipalities will strengthen the sector's resilience and help reduce the deforestation rate.

Water resources

The analysis of current vulnerability shows that floods, river flooding, periods of excessive heat and acute drought, as well as late and violent rains, constitute the main climatic and hydrological risks affecting Benin's water resources sector. Accordingly, the 09 sectoral projects and the 10 projects led by the Municipalities aim at: - the development and sustainable management of small watersheds, with a view to improving the regulation of water resources and reducing the vulnerability of exposed areas; - the promotion of integrated management of lowlands used for agriculture, notably rice growing, in order to strengthen water control and the sustainability of production systems; - the development of multi-purpose water infrastructure, including boreholes, to increase sustainable and equitable access to water in rural and peri-urban areas affected by climate variability and extremes.

Health

In Benin, the health sector is one of the most vulnerable to climate change, as shown by its vulnerability index of 0,760 in 2023. The main climate risks affecting this sector are floods, drought, violent rains, heat waves, violent winds, sandstorms, rising temperatures, delayed rainy seasons and sea level rise. These risks have a significant impact on the health of the population, on infrastructure and on health services throughout the country. Indeed, among the ten leading causes of death in Benin, four (malaria, malnutrition, acute respiratory infections and cardiovascular diseases) are directly linked to the climate. Benin's CDN 3.0 addresses the growing climate change-related risks for vector-borne diseases, as well as for maternal and neonatal health. The 06 health sector initiatives included in the CDN 3.0 aim to address these risks.

Coastal zone

Benin's coastal zone extends over 30 of the country's 77 Municipalities, 5 of which are coastal (Sèmè- Kpodji, Cotonou, Abomey-Calavi, Ouidah and Grand-Popo), and concentrates about 60% of the population. It faces an intensification of climate hazards (floods, river flooding, violent winds, prolonged droughts and sea level rise), which accelerate coastal erosion and cause a retreat of the shoreline ranging from 12 to 30 metres per year depending on the area. The 05 projects in this sector included in the CDN 3.0 aim to strengthen the resilience of infrastructure, economic activities and populations, as well as to establish integrated coastal zone management.

Tourism

The coastal areas, which account for nearly 30% of the national tourism offer, are highly exposed to coastal erosion and sea level rise, which is leading to a loss of attractiveness of the beaches and a progressive deterioration of hotel infrastructure. Moreover, the increased frequency of extreme climate events, such as floods and heat waves, disrupts tourist flows and increases the costs of maintaining and making facilities resilient, which undermines the sector's competitiveness. Six (06) projects have been identified and included in the CDN 3.0 in order to strengthen the resilience of Benin's tourism sector to climate change.

Infrastructure and urban development

The infrastructure and urban development sector is particularly exposed to climate hazards, notably floods, violent winds, extreme temperatures, as well as sea level rise and slow-onset phenomena. These climate phenomena affect the sub-sectors differently, but overall lead to increased deterioration of infrastructure, disruptions of services and economic losses that could reach 2 to 4% of GDP by 2050. Accordingly, eighteen (18) projects have been identified and included in the CDN 3.0 to strengthen the resilience of the infrastructure and urban development sector to climate change.

Cross-cutting adaptation initiatives

To support the progressive integration of climate resilience into governance, planning and the implementation of public policies, thirteen (13) projects, of which six (06) are conditional, have been identified and included in this CDN of Benin. These projects aim:

- to strengthen climate services and early warning systems, which are essential for improving the anticipation and management of climate risks; - to develop territorial approaches to adaptation, through decentralised financing mechanisms; - to integrate climate resilience into essential social services, notably in the water, hygiene, sanitation and health sectors. Cross-cutting issues: gender and social inclusion, just transition, loss and damage The cross-cutting conditions essential to the effective, equitable and sustainable implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 have been defined and are organised around four major areas: 1. Gender and Social Inclusion: Benin's CDN 3.0 adopts an approach that is sensitive to gender and social inclusion in order to respond to the differentiated vulnerabilities of women, girls, children, young people and persons with disabilities. It promotes their socio-economic empowerment, their active participation in climate leadership and their equitable access to the opportunities and benefits of the transition to green value chains. 2. Private Sector Engagement: the private sector is recognised as a strategic partner and an essential lever, both financially and technologically, in Benin's CDN 3.0. It is mobilised as a co-investor and operator of climate solutions in priority sectors such as energy, agriculture and resilient infrastructure, and supported by de-risking mechanisms and innovative financial instruments aimed at directing investment towards low-carbon and climate-adapted options. 3. Just transition: Benin's CDN 3.0 is part of a just transition approach aimed at ensuring that the transformation towards a low-carbon economy is inclusive and socially acceptable. It emphasises the protection of livelihoods, the creation of decent and green jobs, skills development and social dialogue, so as to leave no group or territory behind. 4. Loss and damage: in accordance with Article 8 of the Paris Agreement, Benin incorporates into its CDN 3.0 strategic measures to address unavoidable and irreversible climate impacts. This includes strengthening early warning systems, deploying climate insurance mechanisms, establishing adaptive social safety nets and mobilising dedicated financing to support local communities facing residual damage. Means of implementation The effective implementation of the mitigation and adaptation measures set out in Benin's CDN 3.0 requires strengthened support in terms of financing, capacity building and technology transfer. Despite the progress made in recent years in mobilising climate resources, the scale of the actions envisaged requires a significant increase in financial means to ensure their implementation. Institutional arrangement for implementation The implementation of Benin's third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) is carried out under the coordination of the Ministry of Living Environment and Transport, in charge of sustainable development (MCVT), in its capacity as national Focal Point of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. It mobilises a set of key actors, in particular:

  • the relevant sectoral ministries and institutions;
  • local authorities;
  • the private sector;
  • civil society.

The bodies for implementing Benin's CDNs are defined by order n°2018-063/MCVDD/DC/SGM/DAF/DGEC/DGRACC/SAECC/CORD-CDN/SA 014SGG18 of 23 April 2018, establishing the creation, responsibilities, organisation and functioning of the bodies for implementing the Nationally Determined Contributions (CDNs). These bodies are the following:

  • the Steering Committee, the supreme decision-making and guidance body; its composition is defined in article 4 of the order and consists of designated representatives of the key actors;
  • the National CDN Coordination Unit, the body bringing together all actions;
  • the Sectoral Implementation Coordination, under the ministries, institutions, agencies and other structures concerned by the measures and actions retained in CDN 3.0;
  • the Communal Coordination is the body responsible for overseeing the implementation of the CDN at local level.

The MCVT will provide the strategic guidance and support needed to assist the sectoral structures in developing projects to mobilise financing and any other initiative, drawing on existing mechanisms. It will also ensure the coordination of the Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MNV) system for the implementation of CDN 3.0, as well as the strengthening of institutional capacities, in collaboration with the stakeholders concerned.

1 INTRODUCTION

Benin, like most developing countries, is strongly affected by the harmful and dangerous effects of climate change, which are reflected both in a trend-based rise in climate normals, in particular the increase in average temperatures, the intensification of evapotranspiration and the alteration of rainfall patterns, and in an upsurge in extreme weather and climate events, such as the increase in the spatial and temporal variability of the dry and rainy seasons, floods, droughts, violent winds, coastal erosion and sea level rise (MCVDD, 2019, 2022). Aware that the fight against climate change is a combination of efforts by States and various institutions, the Republic of Benin has signed and ratified several major international instruments on climate, in particular the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (CCNUCC) in June 1994, the Kyoto Protocol in February 2002 and the Paris Agreement in October 2016. As a State party to these various agreements and treaties, Benin has firmly committed itself to the global drive to combat climate change, as shown by the submission of its Intended Nationally Determined Contributions (CPDN) in September 2015 and then of its CDN2.0 in October 2021. CDN 2.0 aimed to reduce greenhouse gas (GES) emissions, excluding the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (UTCATF) sector, by about 48,75 Mt E-CO₂, that is 20,15% by 2030, while integrating more adaptation measures in the most vulnerable sectors. In order to implement CDN2.0, a Partnership Plan was drawn up in order to bring together technical and financial actors around a concerted and effective implementation of the commitments. This Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) of Benin has been prepared in accordance with article 4.2 of the Paris Agreement, which requires each Party to strengthen, every five (5) years, the scope and ambition of its climate commitments, in a spirit of transparency, progression and shared responsibility. It is based on the rigorous assessment of CDN2.0, in particular on the progress achieved, the persistent challenges and the key recommendations, as well as on newly identified opportunities. It aims to place Benin on an even more ambitious trajectory, consistent with the conclusions of the Global Stocktake, the Sustainable Development Goals (ODD), and the national low-carbon development priorities reflected in the country's low-carbon and climate-resilient development strategy (LT LEDS). This new CDN expresses Benin's determination to further reduce its emissions, in particular by developing renewable energies, promoting sustainable agricultural practices and improving the management of natural resources. It pays particular attention to the cross-cutting issues that are essential for inclusive and equitable climate action, such as gender and social inclusion, peace and security, social equity, just transition, loss and damage, and alignment with national development policies.

2 NATIONAL CONTEXT

2.1 SOCIO-ECONOMIC CHARACTERISTICS

The Republic of Benin is a West African country covering an area of 114.763 km² and home to a population estimated at 13,2 million inhabitants in 2025, characterised by a high proportion of young people, nearly 79,3% being under 35 years of age (INStaD, 2022). Administratively, Benin is divided into twelve (12) departments, themselves subdivided into seventy-seven (77) Communes. The country has a marked climatic diversity, with a sub-equatorial climate in the south and a continental tropical climate in the north, which strongly influences economic activities, particularly agriculture. Benin shows sustained economic momentum. Despite the effects of multiple shocks, it recorded a growth rate of 6,4% in 2023, 7,5% in 2024 and 8,8% in the third quarter of 2025 (INStaD, 2025), driven by all sectors. The primary sector, dominated by agriculture, grew by 6,2%, while the secondary and tertiary sectors recorded growth of 9,9% and 10,0% respectively in the third quarter of 2025, which reflects a gradual diversification of the economy. Despite these results, structural challenges persist. In 2022, 36,2% of the Beninese population was living in poverty, compared with 38,5% in 2019, a slight decrease of 2,3 percentage points (INStaD, 2023). Underemployment remains particularly high, affecting nearly 72,9% of the active population (BAD, 2025), which reflects the predominance of informal employment and the vulnerability of livelihoods. In addition, Benin faces major challenges, such as the harmful effects of climate change, the worsening security threat in the north of the country, youth unemployment, persistent social inequalities and the shortcomings of the education and health systems. In Benin, the children's vulnerability index is 8,1 and the Children's Climate Risk Index (IRCE)1 is 7,6 (UNICEF, 2021). These constitute decisive issues for sustainable development planning and for the climate ambition carried by CDN 3.0.

2.2 EMISSIONS PROFILE

Benin's total emissions, according to the greenhouse gas (GES) inventory established in all sectors for the period 1990-2023, excluding the Forestry and other Land Use (FAT) sector, are assessed at 24,54 Mega tonnes CO2 Equivalent (Mt E-CO2) in 2023, marking a notable increase compared with the 1990 level (10,75 Mt E-CO2) (MCVT, 2026). In 1990, the agriculture sector was the main emitter (77,6%), followed by energy (20,1%). In 2023, the emissions profile remains dominated by the agriculture sector (38,44%), ahead of energy (36,86%), industrial processes (22,84%) and waste (1,86%). Including the FAT sector, net greenhouse gas emissions amount to 106,09 Mt E-CO2. The main sources of emissions are the combustion of biomass and fuels (energy sector), enteric fermentation, manure management and N₂O emissions linked to agricultural soils. The UTCATF sector plays a decisive role in the overall emissions balance, in particular through land converted to cropland, land-use changes and deforestation, which reduce the carbon sequestration potential. 2.3 CLIMATE PROFILE AND VULNERABILITY

Although its contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions remains marginal, Benin is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. With an ND-GAIN index of 38,52 placing it 156th worldwide out of 185 countries, and a vulnerability score of 0,571, Benin ranks 17th worldwide among the most vulnerable countries. Despite the efforts made to address climate change, Benin shows a low level of readiness (score: 0,342 in 2023), ranking 131st worldwide. The main vulnerability analyses highlight five (05) major climate hazards, namely droughts, floods, violent winds, extreme heat waves and sea level rise (MCVDD, 2019; Akponikpé et al., 2019; Ossè et al., 2019; Sintondji et al., 2019). These hazards affect the entire national territory and lead to multisectoral repercussions, in particular the degradation of natural resources, the displacement of populations, the acceleration of coastal erosion and the disruption of economic activities, with economic and social costs that are constantly increasing (MCVDD, 2022). In response, eight (08) priority sectors are the subject of adaptation in Benin: agriculture, water resources, energy, health, infrastructure and urban development, tourism, forestry and the coastal zone.

2.4 LEGAL, POLICY AND INSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

2.4.1 Legal framework

On the legal side, Law n°2018-18 of 6 August 2018 on climate change in the Republic of Benin was adopted and constitutes the legal foundation of national climate action. It endorses the imperative of integrating climate considerations into all public policies, development strategies and investment projects, both at national and local level. It also sets out the guiding principles of climate governance (participation, equity and sustainability) and establishes a framework of accountability regarding international commitments, in particular the Nationally Determined Contributions (CDN).

2.4.2 Institutional framework

National governance on climate change falls mainly to the Ministry of Living Environment and Transport, in charge of Sustainable Development (MCVT), which performs the function of National Focal Point of the CCNUCC. This ministry hosts the Directorate General of Environment and Climate (DGEC), responsible for (i) implementing the multilateral agreements relating to climate change, in collaboration with the relevant struc-

The DGEC ensures the coordination and monitoring of the implementation of the CDN. Collaboration between the MCVT and the other stakeholders is facilitated by law n°2018-18 of 06 August 2018, as well as by the National Committee on Climate Change (CNCC), formalised by the said law and decree N°2020-260 of 29 April 2020. Other provisions have been adopted recently, in particular the creation of the Beninese Council for Sustainable Development (CBDD) in 2022, of the Carbon Project Registration Authority (AEPC) and of the National Fund for Environment and Climate (FNEC), accredited by the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and by the Adaptation Fund (FA). The AEPC works in full synergy with the Designated National Authority (AND), provided by the Directorate General of Environment and Climate (DGEC), which issues the official host country authorisations. In addition, the operational procedures for registering projects are set by decree n° 2022-6984 of 07 December 2022.

2.4.3 Policy framework

On the policy side, the national framework has undergone some changes with the adoption of the national development vision for 2060: Bénin 2060 ALAFIA, a World of Splendours. Through this national compass, the collective ambition is: "By 2060, Benin is a country of peace, prosperity, good governance, cultural and international influence and shared well-being" (Republic of Benin, 2026). Its strategic orientation 2, entitled "Strengthening the resilience of the Benin system", aims to respond to the direct threats that shocks of all kinds represent for ecosystems. 3 PROCESS AND METHODOLOGICAL FOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF CDN 3.0

3.1 PROCESS FOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF CDN 3.0

The process of developing Benin's CDN 3.0 followed a rigorous, inclusive approach in line with the requirements of the Paris Agreement, integrating, on the one hand, the guidance of Benin's low-carbon and climate-resilient development strategy and of the National Climate Change Management Policy (PNGCC 2021-2030) and, on the other hand, the achievements of CDN2.0 and international best practices. It made it possible to mobilise all stakeholders and to ensure consistency with national sustainable development priorities, the methodological frameworks of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (GIEC) and of the CCNUCC, as well as the requirements of transparency and accountability. The process of developing Benin's CDN 3.0 took place from April 2025 to April 2026. It began with the preparation of the review of CDN2.0 and mobilised all national stakeholders, in particular the sectoral ministries and institutions concerned by climate actions, local authorities, the private sector, civil society, youth and women's associations and the Technical and Financial Partners (PTF), in a spirit of consultation and co-construction.

3.2 METHODOLOGICAL APPROACH FOR THE DEVELOPMENT OF CDN 3.0

The estimation of emissions was based on a standard four-step methodological approach: (i) the calculation of historical emissions; (ii) the projections of emissions under the reference scenario; (iii) the projections of emissions under the mitigation scenario and (iv) the estimation of emission reductions.

3.2.1 Historical and projected emissions under the reference and mitigation scenarios

  • Reference scenario

The emissions of the reference scenario established in the framework of CDN2.0 for the period 2010-2030, then projected up to 2040, constitute the baseline for CDN 3.0. This is a scenario that excludes all measures from 2018 onwards. The updates made are justified by: - the publication by INStaD of updated demographic data and projections covering the period 2014-2063; - the collection, from INStaD, of updated data on GDP from 1999 to 2024 in constant 2015 CFA francs; - the reprocessing of data on the saturation of energy product consumption in the residential sector (percentage of user households) and of specific consumption on the basis of data from various sources; - the updating of vehicle fleet data for the road transport sub-sector of the energy sector, as well as the accounting of fuel consumption by agricultural tractors, now included in the agriculture, forestry and fisheries sub-sector; - the updating of agriculture sector data for the categories relating to:

  • enteric fermentation and manure management, data on livestock numbers, in order to take into account the 2019 national agricultural census;
  • the use of fertilisers (urea and NPK), the quantities of which were recalculated from the performance reports of the ministry in charge of agriculture;
  • cropland areas, taken from the FAO database.

- the extension of the estimation of hydrofluorocarbon (HFCs) emissions to other products that had not been taken into account in CDN2.0. On the basis of the activity data thus updated for the establishment of the national greenhouse gas (GES) inventories over the period 1990-2023, in the framework of the preparation of Benin's first Biennial Transparency Report (RBT), the emissions for the periods 2010-2018 and 2010-2023 were also updated using the Low Emissions Analysis Platform (LEAP5) tool for the following sectors: Energy, Agriculture, Waste, Industrial Processes and Product Use (PIUP). The results obtained with LEAP were made consistent with the national GES inventory carried out using the GIEC 2.98 software for the time series from 1990 to 2018, then from 2010 to 2023, in order to constitute a solid basis for the projections of emissions of the reference and mitigation scenarios up to 2040. For the forestry sector, these same tasks were carried out using the Excel tool. For the forestry sector, the results of the GES inventories updated over the period 1990-2018 and extended to 2023 were directly integrated into the LEAP tool for the period 2010-2023, in order to serve as a basis for the projections up to 2040.

  • Mitigation scenario

The starting point of the emission projections of the mitigation scenario is 2024, the most recent inventory year being 2023. It incorporates the measures adopted and planned from 2018 onwards, as well as the new measures identified in the policy, strategy, action plan and project documents of the main GES-emitting sectors in Benin (energy, agriculture, forestry and waste). The emission projections of the mitigation scenario were established using the LEAP tool for the energy and waste sectors, as well as for the measure relating to rice growing with water management in the agriculture sector. For the non-energy sectors (agriculture and forestry), the mitigation potential of the mitigation measures up to 2040 was assessed for each measure using the Nationally Determined Contribution Expert Tool (NEXT)6, designed by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) with the support of the French Development Agency (AFD) and the German ministry of agriculture. The results obtained with LEAP and NEXT were then compiled and processed using the Excel tool.

The estimation of expected emission reductions combines two accounting approaches: - the approach based on the development of scenarios (baseline and mitigation) at sectoral scale (energy and waste sectors), and the determination of reduction potentials using the formula "Mitigation scenario – Baseline" for the reduction potential in volume, and "Mitigation scenario – Baseline)*100/Baseline" for the reduction potential as a percentage; - the measure-by-measure potential assessment approach and the establishment of the annual carbon balance by type of measure (approach used in the agriculture and forestry sectors).

3.2.2 Calculation methodology

The methodology used to quantify emissions in each sector follows the GIEC 2006 guidelines for emission inventories, as improved in 2019. For all sectoral categories, the GIEC default emission factors were used for the inventories, with the exception of enteric fermentation in cattle, for which an emission factor specific to Benin was used.

  • Gases covered by the emissions estimate

The assessment of pollutant mitigation under this CDN 3.0 of Benin covers not only greenhouse gases (GES), but also short-lived air pollutants that have a local impact on human health. The list of pollutants whose emissions were assessed is as follows: - Greenhouse gases (GES)

  • Carbon dioxide (CO2): a greenhouse gas with an atmospheric lifetime of several hundred years and the largest contributor to global climate change;
  • Methane (CH₄): a greenhouse gas and a short-lived climate pollutant with an atmospheric lifetime of about 15 years. Methane emissions are the second largest contributor to the rise in global temperature, after carbon dioxide. They also contribute to the formation of tropospheric ozone (O₃), which has adverse effects on respiration;
  • Hydrofluorocarbons (HFC): greenhouse gases used mainly as refrigerants to replace chlorofluorocarbons (CFC) and hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFC) in the refrigeration and air-conditioning sectors;
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O): a greenhouse gas with an atmospheric lifetime of more than 100 years.

- Short-lived climate pollutants

  • Black carbon (CB): a component of direct particulate matter (PM) emissions that contributes to the adverse effects of air pollution on human health and to the warming of the atmosphere through direct absorption of solar radiation. It is mainly emitted during incomplete combustion.

- Air pollutants

  • Particulate matter (PM2,5 and PM10): small solid particulate matter present in the atmosphere (aerodynamic diameter below 2,5 µm for PM2,5 and below 10 µm for PM10), which contributes to the effects of air pollution on human health because of its effects on the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. The PM₂,₅ and PM₁₀ emissions calculated here correspond to the direct emissions of these particles into the atmosphere. They do not take into account those that may also be generated by other gaseous pollutants, such as nitrogen oxides, sulphur dioxide, ammonia and volatile organic compounds.
  • Nitrogen oxides (NOx): an air pollutant that is a precursor of particulate matter and tropospheric ozone formation, NOx is made up of two pollutants, nitrogen oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂);
  • Sulphur dioxide (SO₂): an air pollutant that is a precursor of particulate matter formation;
  • Ammonia (NH₃): an air pollutant that is a precursor of particulate matter formation;
  • Organic carbon (OC): a component of direct particulate matter (PM) emissions that contributes to the adverse effects of air pollution on human health;
  • Non-methane volatile organic compounds (COVNM): a set of different organic molecules emitted by various sources. COVNM are precursors of tropospheric ozone and particulate matter formation. Common COVNM emitted by human activities include propane, butane, benzene and toluene;
  • Carbon monoxide (CO): a gaseous air pollutant that contributes to the formation of tropospheric ozone.

For the estimation of the emissions of the various gases in CO₂ equivalent iè m(Ee-CO₂), as well as for their aggregation, the Global Warming Potentials (PRG) of the 5 GIEC report were used: CO₂ (1), CH₄ (28), N₂O (265), HFC (1 à 12.400), depending on the gas, PFC (1 à 11 100), SF₆ (23.500) and NF₃ (16.100).

4.3.5 Industrial Processes and Product Use

5.1.1 Agriculture

The agriculture sector comprises the sub-sectors of crop production, livestock, fisheries and aquaculture, as well as all agri-food processing activities. 6.1 GENDER AND SOCIAL INCLUSION

Benin's CDN 3.0 recognises that climate action cannot be effective, sustainable and equitable without explicitly taking gender and social inclusion dimensions into account. Climate change affects children, women, girls, men, young people, persons living with a disability and vulnerable socio-economic groups in differentiated ways, owing to their social roles and their level of access to resources, services and decision-making bodies. In line with the principles of the Paris Agreement, with the CCNUCC decisions on gender (in particular the Gender Action Plan – GAP), and with national priorities on equality and social cohesion, Benin's CDN 3.0 adopts a gender-responsive and socially inclusive approach, mainstreamed across all the pillars of mitigation, adaptation and means of implementation. The gender and social inclusion approach of the CDN 3.0 is set within a coherent national and international framework, comprising in particular:

  • the recently revised Constitution of the Republic of Benin and the laws on equality and social protection;
  • the National Gender Policy and the associated sectoral strategies;
  • Benin's Gender and Climate Change Action Plan (PAGCCB) 2023-2025;
  • Law No. 2018-18 of 08 August 2018 on climate change;
  • Benin's international commitments under the CCNUCC and the Paris Agreement;
  • the declaration on children, young people and climate action10;
  • general comment No. 26 (2023) on children's rights and the environment, with a focus on change (CRC/C/GC/26)11.

At the institutional level, the MCVT, through the DGEC, works in synergy with the Ministry of Social Affairs and Microfinance (MASM), the Ministry of Sports, the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Fisheries (MEAP), the Ministry of Health (MS), the Ministry of Energy, Water and Mines (MEEM), the Ministry of Decentralisation and Local Governance (MDGL), and the National Association of Municipalities of Benin (ANCB), in order to ensure effective mainstreaming of gender and social inclusion in climate policies and actions.

6.2 PRIVATE SECTOR ENGAGEMENT AND ITS ROLE IN THE CDN 3.0

The effective implementation of Benin's third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) rests on the coordinated mobilisation of all national actors, foremost among them the private sector. In a context marked by high climate vulnerability, substantial investment needs and limited public budget capacity, private sector engagement is an essential lever for accelerating the transition towards low-carbon, resilient and inclusive development. In line with the guidance of the Paris Agreement, the lessons of the Global Stocktake and the national priorities set out in the National Development Plan and the Long-Term Low Carbon and Climate Resilient Development Strategy (LT-LEDS), Benin's CDN 3.0 recognises the private sector as a strategic implementation actor, called upon to play a central role in mobilising finance, disseminating technological innovations, creating green jobs and ensuring the sustainability of climate actions.

6.2.1 Strategic role of the private sector in Benin's climate transition

The Beninese private sector, in all its diversity (large enterprises, SMEs/small industries, agricultural enterprises, cooperatives, financial institutions, start-ups), is called upon to contribute actively to achieving the mitigation and adaptation objectives of the CDN 3.0. Its role goes beyond that of a mere beneficiary of public policies and falls within a logic of co-investment, risk-sharing and scaling up of climate solutions. This positioning is consistent with the guidance of international financial institutions, which stresses that a successful energy and climate transition in Benin will depend on the capacity to mobilise the private sector around innovative financial instruments (green bonds, guarantees, PPP partnerships) in order to close the financing gap estimated for the achievement of climate objectives. The African Development Bank (2024) also highlights the importance of involving local and international enterprises in the implementation of the climate framework launched at COP29, particularly in the agricultural and energy sectors. With this in mind, the Beninese State intends to create an environment conducive to private climate investment, by strengthening regulatory predictability, improving access to appropriate financial instruments and facilitating public-private partnerships. This approach aims to maximise the impact of public resources, attract national and international finance and support a just and inclusive transition, in particular for the benefit of women, young people and vulnerable populations.

6.2.2 Functions expected of the private sector in the CDN 3.0

Within the framework of Benin's CDN 3.0, the private sector is called upon to act through several complementary functions:

  • investor and co-financier, through the mobilisation of equity and of commercial or concessional finance for mitigation and adaptation projects, in particular in the energy, agriculture, forest, waste, water and resilient infrastructure sectors;
  • developer and operator of climate solutions, providing the design, implementation, operation and maintenance of climate-compatible infrastructure and services, such as solar mini-grids, clean cooking systems, irrigation solutions, waste management services and resilient urban infrastructure;
  • supplier of technologies and innovations, contributing to the dissemination of clean technologies adapted to the national context, in particular renewable energy, energy efficiency, resilient agricultural equipment, improved seeds and digital climate solutions;
  • financial intermediary, through banks, microfinance institutions, insurance companies and fintechs, for the development of green financial products adapted to the needs of households, farms, SMEs and local authorities;
  • actor in carbon markets and international cooperation mechanisms, in particular under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, for the development of carbon projects, the implementation of measurement, reporting and verification (MNV) systems and the valorisation of emission reductions;
  • employer and actor in skills development, contributing to the creation of green jobs and to the strengthening of technical and entrepreneurial capacities, in particular for young people and women.

Opportunities for private sector engagement are particularly significant in the priority sectors identified by the CDN 3.0, in particular energy (renewable energy, mini-grids, energy efficiency and clean cooking); agriculture and food systems (irrigation, agroecology, agri-food processing and resilient storage); forests and land use (agroforestry, forest landscape restoration, sustainable forest management and REDD+ initiatives); waste (valorisation, composting, biogas and public-private partnerships for urban management); cities and infrastructure (stormwater drainage, resilient construction and adapted transport); and the coastline and water resources (nature-based solutions, coastal protection and integrated water resources management). These sectors constitute priority investment poles, likely to generate co-benefits in terms of emission reductions, climate resilience, job creation and local economic development.

6.2.3 Institutional framework and financial instruments for private sector engagement

Private sector engagement in the implementation of the CDN 3.0 takes place within a structured institutional framework, placed under the coordination of the Ministry of Living Environment and Transport, in charge of Sustainable Development (MCVT), through the DGEC. The National Fund for the Environment and Climate (FNEC) plays a central role as the national instrument for mobilising and channelling climate finance, in particular finance from the Green Climate Fund, the Adaptation Fund and the Loss and Damage Fund. Sectoral ministries, local authorities, employers' organisations and chambers of commerce also contribute to creating an environment conducive to private climate investment. Public-private dialogue mechanisms and consultation platforms will be strengthened in order to foster the co-construction of projects, the alignment of priorities and transparency in implementation. In order to stimulate private investment in climate actions, the CDN 3.0 provides for the mobilisation of a coherent set of financial instruments, organised around three complementary levels:

  • domestic instruments, including green tax incentives, dedicated credit lines, targeted subsidies and national budgetary mechanisms for the climate;
  • de-risking instruments, such as partial credit guarantees, risk-sharing mechanisms, performance-based subsidies and concessional finance, in particular through the FNEC and technical and financial partners;
  • market instruments, comprising green bonds, public-private partnerships and carbon finance mechanisms, including voluntary markets and the cooperation mechanisms provided for under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

The private sector is also called upon to play a key role in the development of carbon markets in Benin, in particular through the identification and implementation of projects with high potential for emission reduction or sequestration, especially in the forest, agriculture, waste and clean cooking sectors. The establishment of a national carbon registry and of governance frameworks compliant with environmental and social integrity requirements will make it possible to secure these investments and to guarantee the transparency and credibility of the emission reductions generated.

6.3 JUST TRANSITION

The implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 forms part of a structural transformation trajectory aimed at reconciling climate action, economic development and social justice. In this respect, the transition towards a low-carbon economy that is resilient to climate change must be just, inclusive and socially acceptable, so as to leave no group or territory behind. In line with the principles of the Paris Agreement, the guidance of the International Labour Organization (ILO) on just transition, and national sustainable development priorities, Benin's CDN 3.0 incorporates just transition as a cross-cutting principle, guiding all mitigation and adaptation policies and actions and the mobilisation of means of implementation.

6.3.1 Rationale and issues of just transition in the Beninese context

Benin faces a twofold challenge: progressively reducing its greenhouse gas emissions while responding to significant socio-economic needs, characterised by high population growth, a large informal economy, dependence on natural resources and increased vulnerability to climate shocks. Certain key sectors of the national economy, in particular agriculture, biomass-based energy, fisheries, forestry, transport and urban informal activities, are both highly exposed to climate impacts and liable to be affected by the transformations brought about by the climate transition. Without adequate accompanying measures, these transformations could give rise to social risks, such as the loss of livelihoods, the precarisation of certain jobs or the exclusion of vulnerable groups. According to the World Bank's Country Climate and Development Report (2023), the country can seize opportunities in renewable energy, sustainable land management and resilient infrastructure to foster inclusive green growth while reducing poverty and inequality. The national sustainable development report (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 2023-2024) emphasises the principle of "leaving no one behind", by integrating regional and social disparities into development strategies.

At COP29, Benin unveiled an ambitious framework for climate action aimed at mobilising innovative finance, creating green jobs and strengthening the resilience of vulnerable sectors (AfDB, 2024). However, challenges remain, in particular access to climate finance, social protection for vulnerable groups, technical skills development and inclusive community participation. In this context, the just transition aims to:

  • anticipate and mitigate the socio-economic impacts of climate policies;
  • guarantee the protection of existing livelihoods and jobs;
  • create new economic opportunities and decent jobs in green sectors;
  • strengthen social and territorial cohesion.

The just transition in the CDN 3.0 rests on five principles: social equity in the distribution of the costs and benefits of climate action; the inclusion of women, girls, children, young people, informal workers and vulnerable communities in decision-making processes; social dialogue between the State, the private sector, local authorities and civil society; the creation of decent jobs in green value chains; and territorial coherence, taking account of the specific features of rural, coastal and vulnerable areas.

6.3.2 Mitigation, adaptation and equity: the three pillars of a just transition

Within mitigation actions, CDN 3.0 ensures that sectoral transformations contribute to improving the living and working conditions of the populations concerned. Priority measures include the promotion of clean cooking and energy efficiency to reduce pressure on forests and improve household well-being; the development of decentralised renewable energy to create local jobs; support for the agroecological transition to increase productivity while preserving natural resources; and waste recovery and the circular economy to generate new urban economic activities. Adaptation to climate change is a central pillar of the just transition in Benin. The adaptation measures provided for in CDN 3.0 are designed to strengthen the resilience of the most exposed populations while reducing existing inequalities. Priority actions concern the protection and diversification of livelihoods in vulnerable areas (coastal zones, floodplains, arid zones); capacity building for farmers, livestock keepers and fishers in the face of climate hazards; improved access to essential services (water, energy, health) and the promotion of nature-based solutions with environmental and social co-benefits. These measures aim to reduce the structural vulnerability of communities and strengthen their self-reliance in the face of climate shocks. The just transition entails strategic investment in skills and green employment, through the training and reskilling of workers towards the occupations of the climate transition (renewable energy, agroecology, waste management, resilient construction), the integration of young people and women into emerging green value chains, support for green entrepreneurship and local innovation, thereby turning climate challenges into sustainable economic opportunities, and the integration of climate education into the training curricula of the various levels of education and support for young people's access to green jobs.

Implementing a just transition rests on inclusive governance, encouraging dialogue among the State, the private sector, trade unions, civil society and local authorities; the integration of the just transition into sectoral and territorial policies; and transparency in the implementation of climate actions, thereby fostering national and local ownership of climate policies.

6.4 LOSS AND DAMAGE

Benin's CDN 3.0 recognises that, despite the mitigation and adaptation efforts deployed, some climate change impacts are already causing, and will continue to cause in the future, irreversible loss and damage for populations, ecosystems and the national economy. This loss and damage results both from extreme climate events and from slow-onset phenomena, whose intensity and frequency are increasing as a result of climate change. In accordance with Article 8 of the Paris Agreement and the successive decisions of the CCNUCC, in particular those relating to the Warsaw International Mechanism and the recently adopted institutional arrangements, Benin's CDN 3.0 incorporates loss and damage as an essential and complementary dimension of national climate action, distinct from but closely linked to adaptation.

6.4.1 Rationale and national context of loss and damage

Benin is exposed to a range of climate risks generating significant loss and damage: recurrent flooding in the Ouémé, Mono and Niger basins; coastal erosion and sea level rise; droughts and rainfall variability affecting agricultural and pastoral areas; heat waves and violent winds; and the progressive degradation of forest ecosystems, wetlands and mangroves. These phenomena cause direct economic losses (destruction of infrastructure, crop losses, livestock mortality), social harm (population displacement, food insecurity, loss of livelihoods), as well as non-economic losses, such as children dropping out of school / school dropout, degradation of cultural heritage, loss of biodiversity, erosion of local knowledge and harm to the health and well-being of populations. Episodes of violent flooding (for example in 2019 and 2022) caused major material damage and population displacement. Some national and media estimates report one-off economic losses on the order of tens or even hundreds of millions of dollars following major floods (e.g. losses estimated at ≈123 million USD for certain recorded floods). In addition, heat waves and droughts are placing growing pressure on agricultural production, a key sector of the Beninese economy. Several economic and climatological analyses show that, without ambitious adaptation, cumulative economic losses will be substantial. The Benin Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) and World Bank publications indicate that a large proportion of the territory will be exposed to extreme temperatures in the coming decades and that inaction could significantly reduce national GDP (scenarios cite reductions on the order of several tens of percent as a result of inaction by 2050 in certain scenarios). The Benin Economic Update also stresses that the combined effect of climate shocks may lead to growing annual losses and worsen poverty if adaptation measures are not scaled up2. In addition to the direct costs to infrastructure and crops, climate impacts cause massive displacement and social disruption: thousands of people have been displaced following recurrent flooding, schools and educational establishments have been damaged, and vulnerable households are losing productive assets (livestock, seeds, tools). These non-economic losses (mental health, cultural losses, displacement, school dropout, school closures, disruption of the school calendar) are difficult to monetise but have lasting consequences for social capital and local resilience. Women, girls, children, young people, persons living with a disability and poor or marginalised communities are particularly exposed to this loss and damage, owing to their greater dependence on natural resources and their limited capacity to anticipate and respond.

6.4.2 Linkage between adaptation and loss and damage in Benin

CDN 3.0 distinguishes two categories of loss and damage: economic losses (crop and fisheries production, housing and infrastructure, income, reconstruction costs and the impacts of climate displacement) and non-economic losses (human lives, school dropout, physical and mental health, ancestral lands, cultural sites, traditional practices, as well as degradation of ecosystems, ecosystem services and biodiversity). This typology makes it possible to guide national responses in a more integrated and coherent manner. Benin's CDN 3.0 recognises that loss and damage often occurs when adaptive capacities are exceeded or insufficient. As such, adaptation actions aim first and foremost to reduce the risks of loss and damage, without however being able to eliminate them entirely. The national approach rests on strengthening early warning and climate risk management systems; integrating loss and damage into territorial and sectoral planning; promoting nature-based solutions to limit irreversible impacts; and developing social protection mechanisms adapted to climate shocks. This linkage strengthens coherence between the adaptation and the loss and damage pillars. In the face of loss and damage, CDN 3.0 provides for an approach structured around three areas: prevention and risk reduction (territorial planning, resilient infrastructure and sustainable ecosystem management); management of residual impacts (climate insurance, compensation mechanisms and adaptive social safety nets); and response to climate displacement (anticipation, support for displaced populations and integration of climate considerations into migration and urbanisation policies). These responses aim to strengthen national resilience while guaranteeing the dignity and rights of affected populations. 6.4.3 Mechanisms for implementing and supporting loss and damage action

Addressing loss and damage requires specific financial resources, distinct from but complementary to mitigation and adaptation financing. CDN 3.0 stresses the importance of access to international mechanisms dedicated to loss and damage (in particular the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage, FRLD); of strengthening national mechanisms through the National Fund for the Environment and Climate (FNEC); and of mobilising innovative instruments such as climate insurance, contingency funds and rapid response mechanisms. These mechanisms will contribute to a more effective and equitable response to irreversible climate impacts. The governance of loss and damage relies on strengthened coordination among the institutions responsible for climate, civil protection, social action, health, spatial planning and local authorities. MCVT, through DGEC, ensures consistency with international commitments, while the other sectoral ministries and local authorities contribute to operational implementation. The participation of local communities, civil society organisations and humanitarian actors is encouraged in order to guarantee an inclusive response adapted to realities on the ground. The monitoring of loss and damage relies on the collection of disaggregated data on climate impacts, their integration into national monitoring and evaluation systems, and the sharing of good practices. The indicators monitored will include economic losses linked to climate events, the number of people affected or displaced disaggregated by age, sex and disability, documented non-economic impacts including the number of school days lost or disrupted, and the resources mobilised for the response. These elements will contribute to the enhanced transparency framework and to the continuous improvement of Benin's national response to climate impacts. By coherently integrating gender equality and social inclusion (GESI), private sector engagement, the just transition and loss and damage, Benin's CDN 3.0 adopts a cross-cutting and integrated approach to climate action. This chapter constitutes an essential lever for ensuring the effectiveness, equity and sustainability of the implementation of national climate commitments, while strengthening alignment with the Paris Agreement and the enhanced transparency framework.

7 MEANS OF IMPLEMENTATION

The effective implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 depends on the availability and mobilisation of adequate financial, technical, technological and institutional means. These means determine the achievement of the mitigation and adaptation objectives and contribute to aligning climate action with national sustainable development priorities.

7.1 TECHNOLOGY TRANSFER NEEDS

The needs expressed with regard to technology transfer for the adaptation and mitigation components stem essentially from the assessments conducted under the Third National Communication on climate change (MCVDD, 2019), from the report on priority adaptation technologies prepared under the Technology Needs Assessment - Technology Action Plan Project, as well as from Benin's CDN2.0. These strategic documents cover the agriculture and water resources sectors for the adaptation component on the one hand, and the agriculture, forestry and Energy sectors for the mitigation component. In a context where developing countries still have limited technological capacities, access to innovative, effective and appropriate solutions is an indispensable condition for achieving the mitigation and adaptation objectives set out in the CDN. Technology transfer aims to facilitate the acquisition, dissemination and integration of proven climate technologies in priority sectors. The technological priorities identified for the implementation of this CDN concern, in particular:

  • energy: deployment of decentralised photovoltaic solutions, rural electrification, biogas, energy efficiency in public buildings;
  • agriculture: solar irrigation, early warning systems, drought-resilient varieties, agroforestry practices;
  • transport: development of electric and hybrid transport, low-carbon urban planning;
  • water and sanitation: technologies for the reuse and treatment of wastewater, faecal sludge and solid waste, integrated water resources management.

This involves not only the importation of innovative equipment or processes, but also the strengthening of the human, institutional and regulatory capacities needed for their sustainable uptake. Technology transfer therefore holds a central place in the successful implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0. It represents a strategic lever for accelerating the transition towards a low-carbon, resilient and competitive economy. In this regard, Benin is considering the creation of a National Climate Technology Centre (CNTC), in connection with the CTCN (Climate Technology Centre and Network), to coordinate sectoral technology needs and foster public-private partnerships in green innovation. In addition, the country should also commit to promoting an enabling framework for the reception and dissemination of climate technologies, through:

  • the introduction of tax and regulatory incentives promoting green investment; and
  • active participation in technology transfer programmes financed by the climate funds.

7.2 CAPACITY BUILDING, COMMUNICATION AND AWARENESS-RAISING

The effective implementation of CDN 3.0 rests as much on the availability of financial and technical resources as on the institutional, human and societal capacities to understand, plan and act on climate issues. Capacity building is therefore an essential pillar for ensuring intersectoral coordination, evidence-based planning and the mobilisation of climate finance. It enables national and local institutions, the private sector and civil society to integrate the CDN objectives into their policies, plans and operational practices.

7.2.1 Strategic objectives

The implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 is based on an integrated approach combining capacity building, communication and awareness-raising, in order to translate climate commitments into measurable results. The strategic objectives are structured around:

  • the strengthening of the institutional and human capacities of public administrations, local authorities, the private sector and civil society, with a view to improving planning, green budgeting and the monitoring and evaluation (MRV) systems of mitigation and adaptation actions;
  • the improvement of governance and intersectoral coordination, through the consolidation of consultation mechanisms and the harmonisation of sectoral policies and strategies;
  • the development of evidence-based planning, in particular through the strengthening of climate risk observation, modelling and anticipation systems;
  • the mobilisation and effective management of climate finance, including the capacity building of the competent national institutions, such as DGEC and FNEC;
  • the promotion of inclusive participation and national ownership, by ensuring the effective involvement of women, girls, children, young people, local communities and vulnerable groups;
  • the development of a national culture of climate communication and education, fostering transparency, accountability and stakeholder buy-in.

These orientations aim to consolidate an institutional, technical and societal foundation capable of supporting Benin's transition towards resilient and low-carbon development.

7.2.2 Priority areas

In order to guarantee the effective and inclusive implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0, several cross-cutting priority areas have been identified. These areas aim to strengthen human and institutional capacities while fostering national ownership of the climate commitments. They constitute strategic levers that apply to all sectors and ensure the coherence of the actions undertaken. The priority areas identified by Benin as part of human and institutional capacity building with a view to the successful implementation of its CDN 3.0 comprise:

  • the training of sectoral officials on low-carbon planning tools, green budgeting and sectoral MRV;
  • the strengthening of the technical capacities of DGEC, FNEC and the sectoral coordination units for the preparation, management and monitoring of climate projects;
  • awareness-raising among local authorities, women, children, young people and the private sector on the implementation of adaptation and mitigation actions;
  • the establishment of a climate communication and education system through the media, schools and community-based organisations.

These cross-cutting priority areas constitute the operational foundation of Benin's capacity-building and climate communication strategy. They aim to create a national momentum in which institutions, local authorities and citizens are fully engaged in the transition towards low-carbon and resilient development.

7.3.2 Sources of financing

The financing of the CDN 3.0 will rest on an integrated approach combining domestic and international sources, through coordinated financial, technical and technological support. 7.3.2.1 Domestic sources Domestic financing constitutes the fundamental basis for the sustainable implementation of a CDN. Indeed, the mobilisation of internal resources reflects the country's political and institutional commitment to assume its share of responsibility in the fight against climate change. It strengthens national ownership of climate policies, reduces dependence on often uncertain external financing, and fosters consistency between climate priorities and economic and social development objectives. Domestic resources also make it possible to ensure the continuity of climate actions over the long term, by supporting the planning, implementation and monitoring of local initiatives, including in periods when international support is scarce. In the case of Benin, these domestic sources of financing include in particular State budget resources, national funds dedicated to the environment and climate, contributions from territorial authorities, public-private partnerships, as well as investments by the national private sector and innovative green financing instruments.

  • National budget and public planning: mainstreaming climate actions into the Medium-Term Budget Frameworks (CBMT) of key ministries (agriculture, energy, transport, water, health, etc.);
  • National funds dedicated to the environment and climate: such as the FNEC, the reference mechanism for mobilising green resources and financing environmental and climate initiatives for sustainable development in Benin;
  • Public-Private Partnerships (PPP): mobilisation of private investment in the fields of renewable energy, green infrastructure, and climate-smart agriculture;
  • National financial markets: issuance of sovereign or municipal green bonds and sustainable debt instruments.

7.3.2.2 International sources International sources of financing also play a decisive role in the effective implementation of the CDNs, particularly for developing countries whose budgetary margin remains limited. These external resources make it possible to bridge the financing gaps between climate ambitions and national economic capacities.

Moreover, access to international financing helps to strengthen the country's credibility on the global climate stage and to stimulate South-South and North-South cooperation on sustainable development. For Benin, international financing for the CDN 3.0 may come in particular from:

  • Multilateral climate funds: Green Climate Fund (GCF), Global Environment Facility (FEM), Adaptation Fund, Least Developed Countries Fund (FPMA);
  • Regional financial institutions: West African Development Bank (BOAD), CEDEAO Bank for Investment and Development (BIDC), African Development Bank (BAD);
  • Bilateral partners: UE, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, the United States, etc.;
  • Global initiatives: NDC Partnership, Global Energy Alliance for People and Planet (GEAPP), etc.

7.3.2.3 Mobilisation of market mechanisms and financing through Article 6 The Republic of Benin formally expresses its intention to make use of the cooperative approaches provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, in order to maximise the mobilisation of the financial and technological resources needed to achieve its conditional climate objectives. In this regard, it envisages, on the one hand, the transfer of Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMO) under the cooperative approaches referred to in Article 6.2 and, on the other hand, its participation in the Article 6.4 mechanism, which contributes to the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions and to sustainable development. To give effect to this intention, Benin has equipped itself with a robust institutional and regulatory framework, reflected in the creation of the Carbon Projects Registration Authority (AEPC). The latter is responsible for ensuring that projects comply with international norms and standards, as well as for overseeing the national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MNV) system. Any carbon credit transaction carried out under this provision will be subject to the prior authorisation of the Designated National Authority (AND) and will systematically give rise to the application of corresponding adjustments, in accordance with the relevant decisions of the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (Decision 2/CMA.3)12. Benin thereby guarantees the transparency, accounting robustness and environmental integrity of its contribution to the global climate effort, while avoiding any double counting of emission reductions.

8 GOVERNANCE FRAMEWORK AND INSTITUTIONAL ARRANGEMENTS

8.1 INSTITUTIONAL ARRANGEMENTS

The success of the implementation of the CDN 3.0 rests on the commitment and effective coordination of all stakeholders. Each is called upon to fully assume its responsibilities, to comply with the institutional arrangements in place and to harmonise its timetables, objectives and monitoring mechanisms, in order to ensure that the national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MNV) mechanisms are fed consistently. The key actors in the implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 include in particular:

  • the sectoral ministries and institutions concerned;
  • local authorities;
  • the private sector;
  • civil society;
  • women, children, young people and persons with disabilities.

The institutional arrangements for implementing the CDNs in Benin are governed by order no. 2018-063/MCVDD/DC/SGM/DAF/DGEC/DGRACC/SAECC/CORD-CDN/SA 014SGG18 of 23 April 2018, establishing the creation, remit, organisation and functioning of the bodies for implementing the Nationally Determined Contributions (CDNs). These implementing bodies are as follows:

  • the Steering Committee (CP), the supreme body for guidance and decision-making, whose composition, defined in Article 4 of the order, brings together designated representatives of the main stakeholders;
  • the National Coordination Unit of the CDN, an umbrella body ensuring the coherence and coordination of all actions. It is composed of the Director General of the Environment and Climate (DGEC), the National Coordinator, the Focal Point of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, the Climate Technology Transfer Focal Point, an executive secretary, as well as two technical assistants to the National Coordinator;
  • the Sectoral Implementation Coordination, provided by the ministries, institutions, agencies and other bodies concerned by the measures and actions of the CDN 3.0. It brings together the Directors of Planning, Administration and Finance (DPAF) of the sectoral ministries, the officials in charge of project monitoring and evaluation, the climate change Focal Point of the National Association of Municipalities of Benin (ANCB), as well as the official responsible for climate change matters within the umbrella body of Civil Society Organisations (OSC) and Non-Governmental Organisations (ONG);
  • the Municipal Coordination, the body responsible for overseeing the implementation of the CDN at local level.

The MCVT will provide the strategic guidance and the support needed to assist sectoral bodies in developing projects to mobilise financing and in implementing any other initiative, building on existing mechanisms. It will also ensure the coordination of the MNV system for the implementation of the CDN 3.0, as well as institutional capacity building, in collaboration with the stakeholders concerned.

8.2 ROLES OF STAKEHOLDERS IN THE IMPLEMENTATION OF THE CDN

The CDN is one of the country's commitments for which political and strategic leadership lies with the MCVT, in coordination on the one hand with the DGEC and its various departments at central level, which pursue related and complementary strategies on combating climate change, conserving natural resources, restoring forest landscapes, the blue economy, etc., and on the other hand with local entities that ensure the application of the general guidance at the level of the Municipalities.

8.2.1 Public institutions

Public institutions are the bodies that execute the General Policy of the State. The CDN 3.0 is a national commitment whose implementation involves first and foremost the organs of the State. The Government implements public programmes that are intersectoral or steered directly by the higher institutions of the State or that are directly subordinate to them. The ministerial departments in charge of the sectors concerned by mitigation and adaptation carry out activities of public climate or related programmes (whether or not associated with multilateral and bilateral support). The decentralised technical services (Municipalities, Departments, Agricultural Development Poles (PDAs)) support local actors in the technical aspects of implementing climate actions. The national executing agencies implement public programmes or collaborate within the framework of bilateral and multilateral programmes and projects. The decentralised territorial authorities mobilise regional and local actors and communities and facilitate administrative procedures at local level.

Vertical coordination between these public institutions ensures the success of the implementation of the CDN and is important for the monitoring and evaluation of the CDN 3.0.

8.2.2 Technical and Financial Partners

The PTF are indispensable actors, especially in the implementation of adaptation and mitigation actions at local level. They implement climate actions through projects developed and financed by the various international financing mechanisms. Bilateral partners, organisations belonging to the United Nations system (FAO, PNUD, PNUE, PAM, UNICEF, etc.) and international ONGs implement actions within the framework of programmes or ongoing activities. National ONGs and the representations of international ONGs carry out, steer or collaborate in project implementation in line with the guidelines of international financing instruments and according to their specific agendas. Multilateral financing institutions and programmes make climate project financing opportunities known and available to stakeholders. National and municipal thematic platforms serve as frameworks for exchanging information and experience, and support government bodies in defining strategies and implementing climate actions. The PTF are an integral part of the institutions implementing the CDN, in particular component 3 relating to financing needs. The establishment of a lasting partnership between public institutions and the PTF facilitates interventions on the ground.

8.2.3 Private sector

The private sector includes enterprises and the various for-profit and non-profit associations. The private sector is currently moving towards climate-conscious visions. Thus, for some years now, the private sector has been talking about carbon footprints and carbon sinks. The activities of enterprises are no longer only profit-making, but increasingly involve actions of commitment in the face of the problems of climate disruption. The private sector plays a major role in carrying out national projects linked to the implementation of the CDN 3.0, since public-private partnership (PPP) is among the strategies favoured by the State. The participation of the private sector is necessary to ensure a certain effectiveness of climate actions.

8.2.4 Women, children, young people and persons with disabilities

Women, children, young people and persons with disabilities are essential actors in the implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0, on account of their heightened vulnerability to the impacts of climate change, but also of their central role in transforming behaviours and development models. In a context of transition towards a low-carbon and resilient economy, their active participation is increasingly recognised as a strategic lever for accelerating the adoption of sustainable climate solutions. Women play an essential role in the management of natural resources, agriculture, water and household energy, and their economic empowerment directly strengthens the resilience of households and communities. Children and young people, for their part, represent future generations and are at the heart of climate education, awareness-raising and innovation efforts. The early mainstreaming of climate education, the development of green skills and access to green jobs are priorities for strengthening their capacity to contribute to national mitigation and adaptation efforts. The participation of these groups is also essential in climate governance mechanisms, in particular through youth organisations, community associations and local initiatives, in order to ensure effective inclusion in the planning, implementation and monitoring of climate actions. All of these arrangements aim to strengthen the vertical and horizontal coherence of national climate action, while ensuring an effective, inclusive implementation aligned with Benin's international commitments under the Paris Agreement. The governance framework as well as the institutional framework are expected to evolve and to be strengthened continuously, adapting to national policy orientations, sustainable development strategies, as well as to new requirements arising from international agreements and mechanisms, in particular the Paris Agreement (2015).

9 CONTRIBUTIONS TO DEVELOPMENT

The implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 forms part of an integrated sustainable development dynamic, generating significant socio-economic co-benefits and strengthening the coherence between climate policies and national development priorities. Beyond its mitigation and adaptation objectives, the CDN 3.0 constitutes a structuring lever for accelerating the country's economic, social and environmental transformation, while contributing to regional and international commitments.

9.1 SOCIO-ECONOMIC CO-BENEFITS AND ALIGNMENT WITH NATIONAL POLICIES

The measures provided for in the CDN 3.0 generate substantial co-benefits in terms of inclusive economic growth, poverty reduction, job creation and strengthened resilience of productive systems. In the agriculture sector, the promotion of climate-smart practices helps to improve productivity, food security, children's nutrition and producers' incomes, while reducing their vulnerability to climate hazards. In the energy sector, the development of renewable energy and energy efficiency fosters access to clean and affordable energy, while supporting economic competitiveness. Investments in resilient infrastructure, sustainable water resources management and coastal protection make it possible to reduce economic losses linked to climate disasters, estimated at several percentage points of GDP by 2050. Moreover, the actions of the CDN 3.0 contribute to improving public health, in particular through the reduction of air pollution and the prevention of climate-sensitive diseases. These co-benefits align closely with the priorities defined in the main national planning frameworks, in particular the Bénin Alafia 2060 vision, which promotes resilient, inclusive and sustainable development, as well as sectoral policies such as the National Development Plan (PND), the National Climate Change Management Policy (PNGCC) and the National Adaptation Plan (PNA). The CDN 3.0 thus helps to operationalise the strategic orientation relating to strengthening the resilience of socio-economic and environmental systems to shocks.

9.2 ALIGNMENT WITH THE SDGs BY 2030

CDN 3.0 contributes directly and indirectly to the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (ODD), in particular:

Contribution to ODD 1 (No poverty) and ODD 2 (Zero hunger) through strengthening the resilience of agricultural systems and securing livelihoods.

Contribution to ODD 3 (Good health and well-being), through the reduction of health risks linked to climate and pollution.

Contribution to ODD 6 (Clean water and sanitation) through improved integrated water resources management. Contribution to ODD 7 (Affordable and clean energy) through the development of renewable energy.

Contribution to ODD 11 (Sustainable cities and communities) through the promotion of resilient infrastructure and appropriate urban planning.

ODD 13 (Climate action) is at the heart of CDN 3.0

Contribution to ODD 14 and ODD 15 (Life below water and life on land) through the protection of coastal and terrestrial ecosystems.

CDN 3.0 thus constitutes an operational framework making it possible to integrate the ODD into climate policies and to maximise synergies between development and climate action.

9.3 CONTRIBUTION TO THE POST-2030 SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT GOALS (2035 HORIZON)

By 2035, CDN 3.0 places Benin on a resilient, low-carbon development trajectory, anticipating developments in post-2030 international agendas. Investments in sustainable infrastructure, clean energy systems and resilient agricultural value chains help to strengthen the structural transformation of the Beninese economy. This dynamic makes it possible to consolidate gains in human development, to strengthen the resilience of territories and to support green, inclusive and job-creating growth. CDN 3.0 thus prepares the country to take part in future global sustainable development frameworks, by capitalising on the synergies between climate, the economy and social inclusion. CDN 3.0 also contributes to the implementation of the African Union's Agenda 2063, in particular to its aspirations relating to inclusive and sustainable growth, the sustainable management of natural resources and resilience to climate change. The planned actions support in particular:

  • aspiration 1 (a prosperous Africa based on inclusive growth and sustainable development);
  • aspiration 7 (Africa as a strong and influential global player and partner).

In the Beninese context, this alignment translates into a convergence between CDN 3.0 and the Vision Bénin 2060 Alafia: un Monde de Splendeurs, which aims to build a resilient, competitive and environmentally sound economy.

10 CONCLUSION

This third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) of the Republic of Benin reflects the country's firm commitment to setting its development on a low-carbon, climate-resilient and socially inclusive trajectory, in line with the Vision Bénin 2060 Alafia: un Monde de Splendeurs, the National Development Plan (PND 2026-2035), Law No. 2018-18 of 6 August 2018 on climate change and the conclusions of the first global stocktake of the Paris Agreement. Through this CDN 3.0, Benin substantially raises its climate ambition. Over the period 2026-2035, the country commits to reducing its cumulative greenhouse gas emissions (excluding the FAT sector) by 120,7 Mt E-CO₂, i.e. 32,8% compared with the reference scenario; a clear increase relative to the ambition of 20,15% (48,75 Mt E-CO2 over 2021-2030) of CDN 2.0. The commitment to reduce cumulative emissions (including the FAT sector) is 590,8 Mt E-CO₂, i.e. 33,8% compared with the reference scenario, of which an unconditional contribution of 26,5%. At the same time, strengthening the resilience of populations, ecosystems and local economies remains a central pillar of national climate action, through adaptation measures targeting eight priority sectors and integrating the initiatives of all 77 Communes of Benin. This raised ambition is accompanied by a strengthened consideration of cross-cutting issues, such as gender, social inclusion, private sector engagement, just transition and loss and damage. The successful implementation of CDN 3.0 would require overall financing of approximately 14.488,127 million US dollars, of which 9.266,867 million dollars are conditional on international support. Resource mobilisation therefore constitutes the major challenge for implementing the commitments made. To this end, Benin intends to combine national budgetary resources, private investment, support from Technical and Financial Partners, as well as innovative climate finance mechanisms, including the cooperative approaches provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. The governance framework and the national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MNV) system will guarantee the transparency, accountability and environmental integrity of the actions undertaken. To operationalise these CDN 3.0 commitments, an Implementation Plan (PMO) will be developed. This plan will detail all the actions and initiatives necessary for the successful implementation of CDN 3.0, specifying institutional responsibilities, the timetable for interventions, the monitoring and evaluation framework, as well as the resource mobilisation and partnership strategy. Through this contribution, the Republic of Benin reaffirms its commitment to the principles of the Paris Agreement and its determination to contribute in a fair and ambitious manner to the global effort to limit climate warming. The success of this undertaking will depend on a collective commitment from all national actors and on a strengthened international partnership, commensurate with the ambitions set out.

6 – How the Party considers that its nationally determined contribution is fair and ambitious in the light of its national circumstances:

(a)

How the Party considers that its nationally determined contribution is fair and ambitious in the light of its national circumstances;

Benin considers that the CDN 3.0 is sufficiently fair and ambitious to contribute to a low-carbon and climate-resilient economy by 2035, in line with its national circumstances and its ambition to become, by 2035, a prosperous, low-carbon and climate-resilient middle-income country. Benin contributes little to global greenhouse gas emissions. In preparing its CDN 3.0, Benin took into account the general principles and provisions of the CCNUCC and of the Paris Agreement, in particular those relating to Common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDR) and to equitable access to atmospheric space. The country's CDN is guided by the will to reduce poverty, to achieve a low-carbon and climate-resilient economy through sustainable development, and to become a country of splendours in line with the country's Vision 2060. The successful implementation of Benin's CDN 3.0 is conditional and depends on the level of support that will be provided under the convention and other multilateral and bilateral agreements. Benin is aiming for an overall cumulative emission reduction target (FAT included) of 33,8% by 2035 compared with the reference scenario. This ambition is higher than that of the CDN 2,0, justifying the guidelines of the Paris Agreement. This overall ambition is divided into an unconditional and a conditional objective:

  • Unconditional objective: Benin is aiming for an emission reduction of 26,5% (i.e. 463,1 Mt E-CO2), FAT included, relying on the mobilization of its domestic resources and secured external resources.
  • Overall objective: (based on unconditional and conditional measures): Benin's ambition is to achieve a reduction of 33,8% (i.e. 590,8 Mt E-CO2).

The objectives of reducing emissions and strengthening resilience will be achieved through 180 mitigation projects and 243 adaptation projects.

(b)

Fairness considerations, including reflecting on fairness;

In terms of emissions, in 2023 Benin has a relatively low emission rate of 1,66 tonnes E-CO2 per inhabitant/year. Benin's stock of cumulative historical emissions remains low compared with that of industrialized countries, which reflects the country's low responsibility for the anthropogenic causes of global climate disruption. This finding nevertheless contrasts with Benin's high vulnerability and growing exposure to the impacts of climate change. While recalling the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, enshrined in the CCNUCC and which should guide the implementation of the Paris Agreement, Benin resolutely adopts a proactive approach to mitigating GES emissions. The country thus undertakes to contribute to the global emission reduction effort to an extent that exceeds what its historical responsibility could strictly require of it, thereby expressing its active solidarity and its unreserved commitment in the face of the global climate emergency.
