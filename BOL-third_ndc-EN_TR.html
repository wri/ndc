1 Introduction and international context

The Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0 of the Plurinational State of Bolivia is formulated within the framework of the Paris Agreement and in compliance with Article 4, which mandates the periodic updating of climate commitments. The document incorporates the outcomes of the Global Stocktake and is articulated with the Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA) and the Framework for Global Climate Resilience, strengthening coherence with the most recent decisions of the UNFCCC.

Bolivia maintains that the climate crisis is a consequence of the consumerist and anthropocentric paradigm, whose most severe impacts fall on developing countries that, like us, are not historically responsible; therefore, action must be taken under the principles of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities, with developed countries taking the lead in achieving carbon neutrality no later than 2035 and negative emissions thereafter, guaranteeing finance, technology transfer and capacity building; in this context, Bolivia is a victim of an unjust energy transition, since, by drastically reducing its production of fossil fuels, it went from being an exporter to an importer, allocating greater resources to the payment of external debt, which shows that without global conditions for a just transition our countries will remain limited in fully exercising their right to development.

Bolivia reaffirms its commitment to climate action under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, recognising the urgency of confronting the climate crisis and, at the same time, asserting national sovereignty over its natural resources. This update is grounded in the vision of Integral Development in harmony with Mother Earth and seeks to contribute in a just and equitable manner to the global goal of limiting the increase in the planet's average temperature to 1,5 °C

This contribution expands the ambition of the previous NDCs (2016 and 2022), consolidating lessons learned, results and gaps identified in their implementation. It explicitly integrates the cross-cutting approaches of gender, interculturality, youth, health, education, innovation and integrated climate risk management, ensuring that climate action contributes to overcoming historical inequalities and strengthens social, productive and ecosystem resilience.

Likewise, NDC 3.0 is aligned with the main international and national instruments: the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, the Global Adaptation Framework adopted at COP28, the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, Bolivian legislation, especially the Framework Law of Mother Earth and Integral Development for Living Well (Vivir Bien) (Law No 300), and the Law on the State Integral Planning System (Law No 777), the Sectoral and Territorial Integral Development Plans and the Plurinational Climate Change Policy. This articulation ensures coherence between international commitments and national development priorities.

For Bolivia, adaptation represents a strategic priority, given its high vulnerability and growing exposure to droughts, floods, glacier retreat and soil degradation, which put at risk food security, as well as water and energy security, health and the livelihoods of the population. However, even though Bolivia emits less than 0,28% of GHG globally, the country is also strengthening its mitigation commitments in key sectors such as energy, forests and agriculture and livestock, recognising that both dimensions are complementary and necessary to achieve resilient, low-carbon development.

The implementation of NDC 3.0 will require sustained national effort and significant international cooperation in finance, technology transfer and capacity building, in accordance with Articles 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11 and 13 of the Paris Agreement. Within this framework, Bolivia is fully open to all modalities of international cooperation, including market and non-market mechanisms, and promotes the mobilisation of resources from multilateral funds, development banks, bilateral cooperation, the private sector and civil society.

2 National circumstances and country vision

Bolivia is a megadiverse and plurinational country, highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, particularly in sectors such as water, agriculture, forests and energy resources. At the same time, the country makes a small contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions, which reinforces its historical position in defence of climate justice and common but differentiated responsibilities.

The country's vision regarding the NDCs is grounded in the paradigm of Living Well, which promotes integral development in harmony with Mother Earth. This vision recognises that climate action cannot be separated from the eradication of poverty, food security, social equity, the recognition of the rights of indigenous peoples and the exercise of national sovereignty over natural resources.

Within this framework, NDC 3.0 not only expresses mitigation and adaptation commitments, but constitutes a political-strategic instrument that guides the transition towards a resilient, low-emission and just economic and social model, consistent with the Political Constitution of the State, the Framework Law of Mother Earth (Law 300) and the Plurinational Climate Change Policy.

The multidimensional nature of the climate crisis in Bolivia manifests itself differently across strategic sectors, each with its own vulnerabilities and challenges that compromise both the resilience of ecosystems and the well-being of the population. These sectors concentrate significant risks in the face of climate change impacts, but also offer opportunities to advance adaptation and mitigation actions that strengthen water, food and energy security, while promoting integral, sustainable and sovereign development. The current situation of the main sectors linked to climate change is presented below, highlighting the challenges and opportunities that frame the national response.

  • The forest sector, which is home to one of the largest expanses of tropical forests in the world and constitutes the country's main carbon sink, faces unprecedented pressures that threaten its integrity and its capacity to provide essential ecosystem services. Deforestation, driven mainly by the expansion of the agricultural frontier, the clearing of land for commercial crops, illegal logging and the construction of infrastructure, has reached alarming levels in certain regions of the country. Forest fires, many of them caused by poorly managed slash-and-burn (chaqueo) practices or by conditions of extreme drought, not only release large amounts of CO2 stored in biomass, but also destroy unique habitats, affect air quality and compromise the health of the population. Forest degradation, caused by unsustainable selective extraction, ecosystem fragmentation and the invasion of exotic species, reduces the capacity of forests to capture carbon, regulate water cycles and maintain biodiversity. This sector, which should be a fundamental ally in the fight against climate change, has paradoxically become a net source of emissions, which underscores the urgency of implementing integrated strategies for forest conservation, sustainable management and restoration that recognise and strengthen the role of indigenous peoples and local communities as guardians of these vital ecosystems.
  • The energy sector, although it constitutes a fundamental pillar of national development, faces direct and growing risks arising from water variability, which affects the optimal operation of hydroelectric plants, and from extreme events such as storms, floods and droughts that damage generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure, compromising national energy security. In turn, the persistent dependence on fossil fuels, both for electricity generation and for transport, not only contributes to national greenhouse gas emissions, but also exposes the country to the volatility of international oil and gas prices, highlighting the urgency of a just, sovereign and resilient energy transition towards renewable sources.
  • The agriculture and livestock sector, which accounts for approximately 17% of national GDP and constitutes the livelihood of a large part of the rural population, is extremely sensitive to climate variability. Late frosts, destructive hailstorms, prolonged droughts and torrential rains are events whose frequency and intensity are increasing worryingly, affecting both subsistence and commercial agriculture. These impacts not only threaten the production of staple foods and the country's food sovereignty, but also exacerbate rural poverty, drive rural-urban migration and weaken the nutritional security of the most vulnerable families. At the same time, extensive livestock farming and certain traditional agricultural practices contribute significantly to national emissions of methane and nitrous oxide, which calls for an urgent transformation towards sustainable, regenerative and climate-resilient production systems.
  • The waste sector faces critical challenges that are aggravated by climate change. With more than 87% of urban solid waste disposed of in open dumps without any type of treatment, this sector has become a major source of methane emissions, one of the most potent greenhouse gases. Increasingly frequent floods and extreme rainfall exponentially increase the risk of environmental disasters due to the overflow of toxic leachates that contaminate soils and surface and groundwater. Paradoxically, the lack of adequate infrastructure for the recovery and use of waste represents a significant missed opportunity for emissions mitigation and for the development of a circular economy that generates green jobs and reduces pressure on natural resources.
  • The water resources sector, vital for life, the well-being of the population and the productive development of the country, faces growing pressures arising from climate variability and climate change, such as the retreat of Andean glaciers, the decline in flows of rivers and springs, the degradation of wetlands and the overexploitation of aquifers, which compromises the availability and quality of water. These factors, together with prolonged droughts, recurrent floods and hailstorms, increase the risk of supply shortages, ecosystem deterioration and loss of livelihoods, particularly affecting rural and indigenous communities that depend directly on this resource. Pressure on water also affects food production, energy generation and public health, but at the same time constitutes an opportunity to strengthen adaptation through integrated watershed management, the conservation of water sources, the expansion of storage infrastructure and technified irrigation, and the promotion of wastewater reuse and treatment. Improving water-use efficiency in productive sectors also contributes to mitigation, reducing energy consumption and fostering circular economy schemes, consolidating the protection, restoration and sustainable management of water resources as strategic pillars to guarantee water security and the country's resilience to climate change.

The management of water resources, forests, agriculture, energy and waste is deeply interconnected, so that impacts and actions in one sector have direct or indirect repercussions on the others. For example, water availability conditions agricultural production and hydroelectric power generation, while deforestation and ecosystem degradation affect water regulation and increase vulnerability to extreme events. Likewise, waste management and pollution can compromise water quality and soil health, impacting food security and the livelihoods of communities. This interdependence highlights the need for integrated approaches that consider the links between sectors in order to reduce risks and enhance synergies in adaptation to and mitigation of climate change.

This complex and multifaceted reality calls for a coordinated and transformative response that addresses the structural causes of climate vulnerability while seizing the opportunities to build a more just, sustainable and resilient development model. Promoting the joint planning of sectoral policies and actions, based on the watershed approach, ecosystem conservation, resource-use efficiency and the circular economy, makes it possible to advance in full accordance with the principles of Living Well and respect for the rights of Mother Earth, ensuring that the benefits of development are distributed equitably and sustainably.

2.1 National regulatory, strategic and institutional framework

Bolivia has a robust regulatory framework that underpins climate action as a State policy. The Political Constitution of the State recognises the rights of Mother Earth and establishes the mandate for integral development in balance with Mother Earth. This mandate is developed in the Framework Law of Mother Earth and Integral Development for Living Well (Law No. 300), which establishes principles of equity, climate justice and integrated management of life systems, and in the Law on the State Integral Planning System (Law No. 777), which guarantees coherence between climate planning and national and territorial development instruments.

The Plurinational Climate Change Policy, the Sectoral Integral Development Plans and the Territorial Integral Development Plans constitute the main strategic instruments that articulate climate action with productive, social and territorial development. Likewise, Supreme Decree 5264 strengthens the institutional framework for the management of climate finance.

The Ministry of Environment and Water (MMAyA) and the Plurinational Authority of Mother Earth (APMT) lead national climate action in coordination with the Ministry of Development Planning and the sectoral ministries. These institutional arrangements enable the planning, implementation and monitoring of NDC 3.0 through sectoral roundtables and spaces for social participation, ensuring a multilevel, intercultural and participatory approach.

2.2 Fundamental Principles for Just and Sovereign Climate Action

Bolivia's climate action is grounded in a set of inalienable principles that guide the construction of a resilient future in harmony with Mother Earth. These principles, anchored in the Political Constitution of the State, are enriched and materialised through sectoral approaches, ensuring that the transition towards a low-carbon economy is equitable, sovereign and culturally appropriate. Each strategic sector of the country, energy, forests, agriculture and livestock, waste and water resources, internalises and applies these principles to shape its targets and actions, guaranteeing a coherent and integrated response to the climate crisis.

Climate Justice and Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR-RC): Bolivia reaffirms its historical demand that developed countries, which are primarily responsible for the climate crisis, fulfil their obligations to provide finance, technology transfer and capacity building. This principle is the cornerstone of conditional ambition. In the energy sector, it means that the conversion of the matrix towards large-scale renewable sources depends on access to international technologies and capital. In the forest sector, the fight against deforestation and degradation requires innovative financial mechanisms that value the ecosystem services of forests. For the agriculture and livestock sector, the adoption of resilient, low-emission practices by small producers is directly linked to technical and financial support. In the waste sector, the transition towards a circular economy and the closure of open dumps demand significant investments that exceed national capacity. Finally, in the water resources sector, the construction of storage, treatment and distribution infrastructure, as well as the implementation of efficient-use technologies, require international cooperation to guarantee the human right to water and sanitation.

Integrated and Synergistic Approach to Mitigation and Adaptation: Bolivia rejects the false dichotomy between mitigation and adaptation, addressing them as two sides of the same coin. Bolivia's climate action is designed to generate co-benefits in both areas. For example, the promotion of agroforestry systems in the agriculture and livestock sector not only captures carbon (mitigation), but also increases the resilience of crops to drought and pests (adaptation). Forest restoration and integrated watershed management in the forest sector protect water sources, a pillar of adaptation, while maximising CO2 capture. Energy generation from biomass in the waste sector reduces methane emissions and, in turn, diversifies the energy matrix, strengthening the resilience of the national electricity system. The water resources sector, through the construction of reservoirs and the efficient management of the resource, not only ensures water availability in the face of extreme events (adaptation), but also reduces emissions associated with water pumping and treatment (mitigation).

Just and Inclusive Transition: The transformation of production systems in Bolivia will leave no one behind. This principle guarantees that the costs and benefits of climate action are distributed equitably, protecting and empowering the most vulnerable groups. In the energy sector, a just transition is sought, leaving no one behind, promoting access to sustainable, clean and affordable electricity for all; and as a fundamental adaptation tool for the effects of climate change. This transition also contemplates the need for labour retraining arising from the technological shift towards renewable sources and electromobility, as well as the active promotion of women's participation in all areas of the process. In the waste sector, the work of thousands of grassroots recyclers will be formalised and dignified, integrating them as key actors in the circular economy. In the agriculture and livestock sector, priority will be given to supporting peasant and indigenous family farming, ensuring that they have access to the tools and knowledge to adapt to climate change. Community forest management will be strengthened, recognising indigenous peoples and local communities as the guardians of these ecosystems. In the water resources sector, it will be guaranteed that rural and indigenous communities have priority access to drinking water and participate actively in the management of their water sources.

Sovereignty and Security with Food and Energy Sovereignty: Bolivia's climate action is designed to strengthen sovereignty and reduce external dependence. In the energy sector, the massive development of electricity generation projects from renewable sources —such as wind, solar and hydroelectric— is being promoted, as well as electromobility initiatives for public and private transport. These actions will help reduce dependence on fossil fuels in sectors with high levels of energy consumption. In the agriculture and livestock sector, the promotion of resilient agricultural practices and crop diversification will strengthen food sovereignty, ensuring the production of healthy and nutritious food for the Bolivian population, even in the face of a changing climate. The sustainable management of forests and the valorisation of their non-timber products contribute to a sovereign bioeconomy that generates local development without compromising natural heritage. The water resources sector is fundamental to guaranteeing national water security, protecting water sources and developing national capacities for the integrated management of water resources.

Respect for the Rights of Mother Earth and Living Well: This principle, a central axis of the Bolivian worldview, permeates the country's entire climate policy. All actions, from the construction of a hydroelectric plant to the implementation of an irrigation system, must be carried out in accordance with the vision of balance and harmony between human beings and nature. This approach requires that projects be evaluated not only for their economic profitability or their mitigation potential, but for their capacity to restore the vital cycles of Mother Earth and promote the Living Well of Bolivia's communities. It is a call to overcome the extractivist paradigm and to build a relationship of respect and reciprocity with the environment.

2.3 National Vision to 2035. Towards Integral, Resilient and Sovereign Development

Bolivia's vision to 2035 is that of a society that has consolidated integral and climate-resilient development, in full harmony with Mother Earth. This vision is built on the sovereign and just transformation of its strategic sectors, mobilising knowledge, technology and finance to build a low-carbon future with well-being for all Bolivians. This vision is not a utopia, but a path laid out with concrete targets and coordinated actions; this vision materialises through the following sectoral pillars:

A strengthened forest sector, guardian of life and engine of the bioeconomy: By 2035, Bolivia will have a vibrant and protected forest sector, which will have succeeded in halting and reversing deforestation, positioning forests as the heart of the country's climate action. Through sustainable community management, biodiversity conservation, a frontal fight against illegal activities and the development of a bioeconomy based on the intelligent and diversified use of forest products (timber and non-timber), the integrity of life systems and the ancestral knowledge of the peoples who inhabit them will be ensured. Forests will not be seen only as carbon sinks, but as sources of water, resilience, culture and sustainable development.

A sovereign, clean and accessible energy sector for all: By 2035, Bolivia will have revolutionised its energy matrix, reaching 75% of electricity generation from renewable sources, with solar and wind energy leading the expansion in the National Interconnected System. Universal access to electricity will be a reality, closing historical equity gaps and bringing clean and reliable energy to the most isolated communities for sustainable development. Energy efficiency will be a State policy, cutting across all sectors, and electromobility will transform cities, with electric mass public transport systems and a growing fleet of private vehicles.

A resilient agriculture and livestock sector that guarantees food and nutritional sovereignty: By 2035, Bolivia will have robust agri-food systems adapted to new climate scenarios. 40% of production units will have adopted conservation, regenerative and climate-resilient agricultural practices, protecting soils and water sources. Livestock farming will be sustainable and regenerative, reducing its carbon footprint, restoring degraded grasslands and coexisting in harmony with native ecosystems. The integrated management of one million hectares of agricultural soils and the strengthening of the resilience of 400,000 producers to extreme climate events, through early warning systems and agricultural insurance, will ensure the production of healthy, diverse and sufficient food for the country, consolidating Bolivia's food sovereignty.

Waste management based on the circular economy and social justice: By 2035, Bolivia will have left behind the linear “use and throw away” model, consolidating integrated solid waste management that recovers at least 25% of municipal waste and eradicating open dumps, which are sources of pollution and disease. The capture and use of biogas in modern sanitary landfills will become a source of clean energy, while the promotion of inclusive recycling, with the formalisation and empowerment of grassroots recyclers, will not only mitigate emissions, but will also generate thousands of green jobs and promote a culture of responsible consumption and sustainability throughout society.

A resilient water resources sector that guarantees the human right to water and sanitation: By 2035, Bolivia will have consolidated integrated and sovereign management of its water resources, guided by the watershed approach and in full harmony with Mother Earth. This process will guarantee the human right to water for the entire population, drive efficient and resilient productive development, and protect ecosystems as the basis of life. The universalisation of basic sanitation, together with the treatment and reuse of wastewater, will turn an environmental challenge into an opportunity for the circular economy and the strengthening of territories. Water planning in the country's strategic watersheds and ecosystems will be consolidated as strategic axes that will guarantee water security in the face of the climate crisis, thus strengthening integral, resilient and sovereign development at the service of the Bolivian people.

Taken together, this integrated vision of five strategic sectors reflects Bolivia's commitment to fulfilling its international obligations, through strategies that deepen its own development model, "Living Well", strengthen its sovereignty in all spheres and guarantee a just, prosperous and sustainable future for present and future generations of Bolivians.

2.4 Climate Ambition and Integrated Sectoral Approach

Built on the foundations of its previous contributions, Bolivia's NDC 3.0 represents a qualitative leap in terms of ambition, transparency and technical robustness.

This contribution not only raises the mitigation and adaptation targets, but also strengthens their foundations through updated baselines, more precise climate modelling and a detailed breakdown of quantified targets by sector. Bolivia assumes with full responsibility the urgency dictated by the most recent climate science, including the findings of the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report and the results of the Global Stocktake, which point to the imperative need for accelerated and deeper action in this critical decade to keep the 1.5 °C goal alive.

Bolivia approaches this task from a complex reality, marked by structural challenges that condition its development trajectory and a low GHG emissions profile. The historical dependence on extractivist economic models, the persistent pressure on forest ecosystems due to the expansion of the agricultural frontier and illegal activities, and an energy matrix still anchored in fossil fuels are critical bottlenecks that require integrated approaches and profound transformations. NDC 3.0 does not evade these challenges; on the contrary, it seeks to catalyse the necessary changes, promoting a just transition towards production and consumption models that are sustainable, resilient and inclusive.

This contribution adopts a synergistic multisectoral approach, recognising that effective climate action can only be achieved through the coordinated participation and shared responsibility of all areas of the economy and society. Specific targets and actions are presented for five key sectors that are fundamental both for emissions mitigation and for building resilience to the inevitable impacts of climate change. These priority sectors, which together form the backbone of Bolivian ambition, are:

Energy: It constitutes the heart of the productive transformation. The sector focuses on accelerating the transition towards a cleaner, safer and more efficient energy matrix. The targets aim at a decisive deployment of renewable energies - wind, solar photovoltaic and hydroelectric-; as well as alternative energies such as green hydrogen, with sustainability criteria. The promotion of energy efficiency and an unprecedented push for electromobility. The objective is clear: to reduce dependence on fossil fuels, decrease vulnerability to the volatility of international prices, and consolidate a robust, sustainable electricity system that is resilient to the effects of climate change. Finally, it seeks to guarantee universal access to modern energy. clean and affordable, recognising it as a fundamental right indispensable for addressing the adverse impacts of climate change.

Forests and Land Use: The Bolivian pillar of resilience and natural heritage. The targets of this sector are the most ambitious, centred on the integrated and sustainable management of forests and land. Quantitative objectives are established to drastically reduce deforestation and forest degradation, tackling the structural causes of forest fires, such as slash-and-burn clearing (chaqueos) without adequate management. Ecological restoration of degraded areas and the strengthening of community and sustainable forest management will be actively promoted. These actions recognise the vital role of forests as the country's largest carbon sinks, reservoirs of biodiversity unique in the world, regulators of water cycles and the livelihood of the communities and indigenous peoples who inhabit them.

Agriculture and Livestock: The guarantee of Bolivia's food sovereignty. This sector is oriented towards building resilient, low-carbon production systems. The targets seek to scale up the adoption of sustainable agricultural practices, such as conservation agriculture, agroforestry and silvopastoral systems, and efficient water management. In livestock farming, a transition towards sustainable and regenerative models that reduce methane emissions and restore soil health will be promoted. Adaptation is a central axis, with actions to strengthen the resilience of small producers, protect agrobiodiversity and develop early warning systems and agricultural insurance.

Waste: The transition towards a circular economy. This sector focuses on transforming an environmental and public health problem into an opportunity for mitigation and sustainable development. The targets aim to reduce methane emissions from final waste disposal through the capture of biogas in sanitary landfills and, fundamentally, through the implementation of an integrated management model that prioritises reduction, reuse and recycling. It seeks to recover organic and inorganic waste, generating new materials and energy sources, promoting the social and economic inclusion of grassroots recyclers in this new circular economy.

Water Resources: The vital resource for life and development. This sector in NDC 3.0 focuses on the integrated and sustainable management of water resources as a cross-cutting axis for climate resilience. The targets centre on guaranteeing water security for human consumption, productive activities and ecosystem conservation, through water sources and soils, improvement of water quality, the construction of resilient infrastructure, the promotion of efficient water use, addressing hydrological risk management, and wastewater management as adaptation measures. This approach recognises that without water there is no life, no development and no possible resilience to climate change.

The integration of these five sectors under a common vision makes it possible to maximise synergies, avoid contradictions and ensure that climate action in one area does not undermine efforts in another. This NDC 3.0, therefore, is not a simple sum of sectoral targets, but an articulated plan for a structural, just and sovereign transformation of the Bolivian economy and society as a whole.

3 Sectoral targets and national commitments

NDC 3.0 defines a set of sectoral targets that integrate mitigation and adaptation actions and joint commitments. These targets represent the articulation between the international commitments assumed under the Paris Agreement and the national priorities of integral development, climate justice and sovereignty over natural resources.

The structure of targets responds to the country's dual condition: a low historical contribution to global GHG emissions, together with high climate vulnerability. For this reason, greater weight is given to adaptation targets (16), complemented by 13 mitigation targets and 7 joint targets, reflecting a balance between emissions reduction and resilience to climate impacts.

4 Forest Sector

4.1 Brief characterisation of the Forest sector in Bolivia

Bolivia has around 54 million hectares of forests, representing 46% of its territory (MMAyA-DGF, 2024). These ecosystems are distributed mainly across three large regions: the Amazon forest, with approximately 31 million hectares, which concentrates the greatest biodiversity and strategic environmental functions; the Chaco dry forest, with some 12 million hectares, considered one of the most extensive and best conserved in South America; and the Andean and Yungas forests, with nearly 4 million hectares, which play a critical role in water regulation and soil protection (Torrico, 2025). Forest cover faces growing pressures. Between 2016 and 2021 Bolivia lost on average 273.000 hectares of forest per year, with deforestation reaching 386.000 ha in 2021, the highest of the period (MRV/DGF, 2024). Added to this are forest fires, which in 2024 affected 12,66 million hectares, of which 7,16 million were forests, with serious impacts on biodiversity and human health (MMAyA-DGF, 2025).

In terms of management, around 11 million hectares are registered under integrated forest management plans, although afforestation and reforestation actions reached only 4.598 ha in 2024, reflecting a considerable gap relative to restoration needs (ABT, 2024). 4.2 Main emission sources

In Bolivia, the forest and land-use sector (UTCUTS) is the main source of greenhouse gas emissions of all sectors, hovering around 50% of national emissions in 2022 (MMAYA-APMT, 2024). The largest contribution comes from deforestation and the conversion of forest land, which released approximately 63.034 kt of CO₂. Added to this are emissions from biomass burning associated with slash-and-burn clearing (chaqueos) and forest fires, responsible for around 16.687 kt of CH₄ and 90 kt of N₂O in the same year. CO₂ is the predominant gas, generated by the loss of biomass and the reduction of carbon stored in soils and vegetation. In relative terms, in 2022 the forest sector contributed 67,1% of the country's CO₂ emissions, 9,1% of CH₄ and 26,3% of N₂O, consolidating its position as the most important source in the national inventory. This reaffirms the need to strengthen integrated forest management, deforestation reduction and fire prevention actions, which are priorities for achieving the NDC targets (APMT, 2024).

Avoided emissions were estimated with the Nationally Determined Contribution Expert Tool (NEXT), developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). NEXT is a GHG accounting tool designed to support annual environmental impact assessments in the Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) sector. The tool provides multi-decade time series with annual and cumulative estimates of carbon removals and GHG emission reductions resulting from actions included in climate policies.

The tool is based on IPCC methodologies, including the 2006 guidelines, their 2019 refinement and the 2013 wetlands supplement, and supports the Enhanced Transparency Framework. This makes it possible to generate results aligned with NDC tracking and the Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement. In addition, NEXT measures annual changes in carbon stocks per unit of land and CH₄ and N₂O emissions (expressed in tCO₂eq/year), and estimates potential GHG variations resulting from climate actions over a 30-year horizon. In this way, it helps to quantify more precisely the mitigation potential associated with each target.

4.3 Main climate vulnerabilities of the forest sector

The forest sector in Bolivia faces multiple vulnerabilities to climate change, arising from the interaction between deforestation, fires and climate variability. In the Bolivian Amazon, the loss of forest cover reduces evapotranspiration and therefore the generation of local and regional rainfall. Climate change has increased the frequency of intense droughts and the risk of recurrent forest fires (MMAYA-APMT, 2024; Torrico, 2025). These fires, in turn, release particles that inhibit cloud formation, further reduce precipitation and degrade the capacity of forests to regulate flows and recharge aquifers (DGF, 2025).

Climate models project that under climate change scenarios rainfall could intensify in the Amazonian lowlands during the wet season, increasing the risk of flooding (Andrade, M.F. 2014). Added to this is the lengthening of the dry season in the southern Amazon, attributed to climate change and deforestation, with direct effects on the reduction of river base flows and aquifer recharge. These processes reinforce a cycle of water degradation that limits the capacity of the Amazon forest to perform its critical ecosystem functions and increases the vulnerability of water-dependent communities.

In the Chiquitano Dry Forest, the combination of prolonged droughts and high temperatures has aggravated seasonal water stress, reducing the availability of surface water and groundwater for local communities. The loss of tree cover due to deforestation and fire decreases water infiltration and causes flash floods with erosion and sedimentation, affecting the quality of water resources. This dynamic has created a cycle of hydrological degradation that threatens the persistence of the forest and its ecosystem services (DGF, 2025).

In general, the climate vulnerability of the forest sector is expressed in the loss of critical ecosystem functions, such as water and climate regulation, and in increased risks of emerging pests and diseases favoured by changes in temperature and humidity. These processes directly affect the productivity of forest and agroforestry systems, community incomes linked to the use of forest products, and the social well-being of thousands of indigenous and peasant families who depend on forests for their food and economic security (Torrico, 2025).

4.4 Forest sector targets

Overall sectoral mitigation target of the Forest sector

By 2035, cumulatively reduce at least 670 million tonnes of CO₂eq in the forest sector, through the sustained decrease of deforestation, the reduction of the area affected by forest fires, the net increase of one million hectares of forest cover, and the expansion of certified sustainable management, contributing to maintaining and increasing national carbon sink capacity and advancing towards the goal of net zero emissions by 2050.

Overall sectoral adaptation target of the Forest sector

By 2035, consolidate a national system for the integrated and resilient management of forests that ensures the protection and restoration of at least 11 million hectares of priority forest ecosystems, eliminates deforestation in Protected Areas, reduces the burned area by 60% and increases forest cover in vulnerable landscapes, strengthening the adaptive capacity of local communities and guaranteeing the sustainable provision of essential ecosystem services in the face of climate change.

Overall implementation cost of the Forest Sector targets

Achieving the forest sector targets in Bolivia's NDC 3.0, which include reducing deforestation outside and inside protected areas, decreasing forest fires, integrated sustainable management, restoring one million hectares and strengthening the value chains of timber and non-timber products, entails an estimated cost of USD 2.200,00 million over 10 years. This amount covers all the basic elements for implementation and only part of the incentives for conservation and recovery, without accounting for direct incentives to local actors which, if fully included, would raise the implementation cost considerably.

The forest component is characterised by strong conditionality on international cooperation (between 60 and 85% depending on the target), since it requires finance, technology transfer and technical support to ensure its sustainability. At the same time, it constitutes an axis of joint mitigation and adaptation, given that forest actions contribute to reducing emissions while simultaneously strengthening the resilience of ecosystems and of the communities that depend on them.

The forest sector in Bolivia recognises that climate action cannot be limited to the conservation of forest cover and the reduction of emissions, but must incorporate social, cultural and justice dimensions that guarantee a just and inclusive climate transition. Mainstreaming these approaches allows mitigation and adaptation measures to simultaneously promote social equity, community resilience and respect for the collective rights of indigenous peoples and of future generations.

4.6 Integrated contribution and co-benefits of the sector

The forest sector constitutes a fundamental pillar for climate action in Bolivia, as it combines mitigation functions, through carbon capture and storage, with adaptation functions, by maintaining ecosystem services essential for communities and productive systems. The integrated and sustainable management of forests makes it possible to conserve their ecosystem integrity and ensure ecological connectivity, key elements for water regulation, regional climate stability and biodiversity conservation.

These contributions generate synergies with other strategic sectors. In the agriculture and livestock sector, the protection of forested watersheds sustains agricultural productivity and food security. In the energy sector, the regulation of water flows favours hydroelectric generation. In the field of public health, forests contribute to air quality, the provision of safe water and the conservation of species with pharmacological potential. Likewise, forests sustain local economies through the use of timber and non-timber forest products, providing employment and territorial dynamism.

Main expected co-benefits:

  • a. Strengthening of water and food security through the protection of watersheds and agrobiodiversity.
  • b. Conservation of biodiversity and of the ecosystem services that underpin the bioeconomy, tourism and sustainable productive activities.
  • c. Increased climate resilience of rural and indigenous territories against droughts, floods and forest fires.
  • d. Generation of green employment and revitalisation of local economies through sustainable forest management and the integrated use of the forest.
  • e. Revaluation of indigenous knowledge and strengthening of community governance in forest management.

5 Energy Sector

5.1 Brief characterisation of the Energy Sector in Bolivia

Bolivia's Energy Sector is recognised as one of the main contributors to greenhouse gas emissions. At the same time, it constitutes a strategic axis for national development, by providing electricity and fuels that drive the economy, production and the well-being of the population. Because of its importance, it has been one of the most closely monitored sectors, showing progress with respect to the targets set and generating evidence that the country can increase its climate ambition. However, challenges persist associated with the high share of fossil fuels and the vulnerability of the electricity system to extreme climate events. In this context, the transition towards renewable energy, energy efficiency, the electrification of transport and the development of vectors such as green hydrogen represent key opportunities to reduce emissions, strengthen resilience and guarantee equitable and sustainable access to energy.

5.2 Main emission sources

Within the framework of the NDC, the emissions of the energy sector in Bolivia are limited to the transport and electricity sectors; the national targets focus mainly on the energy transition. On the one hand, the sources of greenhouse gas emissions come from electricity generation by thermoelectric plants that use natural gas and diesel in Isolated Systems. And, on the other hand, there are the emissions generated by the vehicle fleet, which represents the largest consumption of fossil fuels. This reinforces the need to accelerate the low-carbon and resilient energy transition.

5.3 Main climate vulnerabilities of the energy sector

The energy sector faces multiple vulnerabilities to climate change that may affect the continuity and reliability of supply. Among the main threats are prolonged droughts and variability in precipitation, which reduce the availability of water for hydroelectric plants, which may put generation in the country's electricity system at risk. Similarly, extreme events such as floods and landslides can damage critical generation, transmission and distribution infrastructure, interrupting electricity service and affecting both the economy and the well-being of the population.

Likewise, heat waves and changes in temperature patterns increase energy demand, which generates additional pressures on the grid. Frosts or severe storms can also compromise electrical infrastructure and raise maintenance and replacement costs. These vulnerabilities increase the exposure of vulnerable populations and may generate economic losses due to impacts on the productive sector. In the absence of adaptation measures, climate risks are expected to intensify the fragility of the electricity system, limiting the capacity to sustain a just, resilient and low-carbon energy transition.

Within the framework of the national commitment to climate action, the strategic axis of the energy sector plays a fundamental role both in the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions and in adaptation to the impacts of climate change. These targets integrate mitigation, adaptation or joint approaches, with special emphasis on the transition towards renewable sources, energy efficiency, the electrification of transport, universal access to sustainable electricity and the strengthening of energy resilience in the context of extreme climate events.

The sectoral energy targets contribute in an integrated manner to the achievement of strategic priorities such as a just energy transition, the resilience of the electricity system, the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions and equity in access to energy services. 6.2 Main emission sources

According to the First Biennial Transparency Report 2020-2022 (MMAyA, 2024), the agriculture and livestock sector is the second-largest emitting sector of greenhouse gases (GHG). The sector generates emissions from livestock and other agricultural activities, notably the emission of gases such as methane CH4 and nitrous oxide N2O. The main GHG source categories according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) include: enteric fermentation, manure management, rice cultivation, agricultural soils, prescribed burning of savannas, field burning of agricultural residues, liming, and application of urea and other nitrogen-containing fertilisers.

6.3 Main climate vulnerabilities of the Agriculture and Livestock sector

Climate change has multidimensional and cross-cutting impacts, with agriculture being one of the most vulnerable sectors, due to the greater frequency of extreme climate events such as frosts, hailstorms, temperature variations, changes in precipitation and changes in atmospheric CO₂ concentration (Torrico, 2025; Gabriel, 2016). According to UGRyCC-MDRyT (2025), between 2013 and 2023 the main threats to the agriculture and livestock sector were, in order of importance: hailstorms, frosts, droughts, floods and flash floods, as shown in the following graph.

The impacts of climate change on the agriculture and livestock sector in Bolivia are increasingly severe. Hailstorms cause direct losses in crops and livestock through physical damage, defoliation and the emergence of diseases, affecting up to 50% of the annual production of potato and quinoa in some areas (Torrico, 2018; Walsh-Dilley, 2020). Early and late frosts impact sensitive crops such as potato, maize and vegetables; a 1 °C drop on the Altiplano reduces consumption possibilities by 2–3%, aggravating rural poverty (Andersen & Verner, 2014). Prolonged droughts, with precipitation deficits of up to 70% in 2023 in the Andes and the Amazon, reduce the availability of water for irrigation and affect the yields of priority crops (WMO, 2023; Torrico, 2025).

In addition, climate change favours the spread of pests and diseases (Fujita et al., 2023). These vulnerabilities affect more than 861.608 production units, reduce rural incomes and deepen food insecurity. In the medium term, they could entail losses of up to 10% of agricultural GDP and reductions of 20–23% in potato and quinoa and 9% in soybean by 2050 (Prager et al., 2020; Gonzales et al., 2022; Torrico, 2025).

7 Waste Sector

7.1 Brief characterisation of the waste sector

The solid waste sector in Bolivia comprises the set of activities related to the generation, collection, transport, valorisation and final disposal of municipal solid waste. In 2024, national generation was estimated at 3.02 million tonnes per year, with collection coverage of 82 % and a predominantly organic composition of approximately 59.5 % (MMAyA, 2024).

However, in Bolivia, as of 2022, approximately 84,3 % of municipalities dispose of their municipal solid waste in open dumps, which represents not only an environmental and health challenge, but also a significant source of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, mainly methane (CH₄). The country has made important regulatory progress: Law No. 755 and Supreme Decree No. 2954 of 2015, as well as public investment and international cooperation programmes under way. However, the subsector still faces structural lags in infrastructure, operational efficiency and valorisation. Transforming the subsector towards a resilient and low-carbon model is crucial to achieving the climate targets established in NDC 3.0.

7.2 Main GHG emission sources

GHG emissions in the waste sector come mainly from the anaerobic decomposition of organic waste in uncontrolled dumps, generating large quantities of methane (CH₄), a gas with a global warming potential 28 times greater than CO₂. In 2024, sectoral emissions reached 2.699 kt CO₂e. Based on programme planning, a downward emissions trajectory of 146 kt CO₂e by 2035 is projected.

Achieving the 2035 target will depend on the effective implementation of national programmes and the availability of external financing, including climate funds, development banks and international cooperation.

7.3 Main climate vulnerabilities of the waste sector

The waste sector is particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, both because of its physical exposure and its weak adaptive capacity. The main threats identified include:

  • a. Intense floods that cause leachate overflows, landslides and collapses in poorly designed dumps and landfills.
  • b. Prolonged droughts that affect composting processes and post-closure revegetation.
  • c. Heat waves that increase the risk of fires at disposal sites.
  • d. Extreme climate variability that interrupts waste collection and transport, especially in hard-to-reach rural areas (MMAyA, 2025b).

These conditions increase health and environmental risks for the population, aggravate municipal operating costs and compromise the implementation of mitigation measures. The sector's infrastructure, mostly precarious or deteriorated, lacks adequate drainage, cover, containment or monitoring systems, which increases its fragility in the face of extreme events.

Adaptation of the sector requires incorporating climate resilience criteria into the design and management of infrastructure, as well as institutional strengthening at the local level. Prioritising the most vulnerable municipalities will be key to a just and sustainable transition.

8 Water Resources Sector

8.1 Brief characterisation of the Water Resources sector in Bolivia

The sustainable management of water resources (surface and groundwater) is fundamental in Bolivia in the face of the impacts of climate change, as it guarantees the availability of water for human consumption, irrigation, energy generation and productive activities.

In Bolivia, surface water resources show a high degree of vulnerability to climate change. The Amazon macro-basin concentrates most of the national water supply; however, availability is marked by growing irregularity in rainfall patterns, with periods of high intensity followed by longer droughts. This behaviour puts strain on the management of reservoirs and dams, making storage and stable distribution of the resource difficult.

The Altiplano regions and the Endorheic macro-basin present the most severe deficits, with very low precipitation and high evaporation. Climate change aggravates this scenario, intensifying droughts and reducing the flows of high-Andean rivers and lakes. Examples such as the drastic shrinking of Lake Poopó show how the loss of surface sources impacts not only the supply of communities, but also the ecosystems that regulate the water cycle.

In the eastern lowlands, particularly in the Chiquitanía, rainfall variability has increased. Precipitation tends to be concentrated in short periods, causing floods and flash floods, while dry periods are extended, compromising the availability of water for irrigation and human consumption. These changes, together with anthropogenic pressure and inter-basin transfers, generate social conflicts and greater competition for the resource.

Water resources contribute significantly to the national economy through several sectors. Agriculture represents around 10.6% of national GDP and employs 26.1% of the economically active population, depending heavily on irrigation for food production. Likewise, hydroelectricity provides on average 40% of the country's electricity generation. Rural communities and urban populations depend directly on surface and groundwater sources, whose ecological balance is indispensable for their sustainability.

However, global warming and growing climate variability are causing extreme phenomena that compromise water resources. Bolivia faces droughts, intense precipitation and the accelerated retreat of Andean glaciers. These aspects increase the vulnerability of the population; for example, the 2016 water crisis affected 125 thousand families in the city of La Paz. This vulnerability demands integrated responses that include, among other things, adaptation strategies in resilient infrastructure (for example, integrating water-use efficiency, increasing storage capacity), sustainable management of water resources in strategic basins and the strengthening of governance to face extreme events in the future.

The conservation of aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems plays a central role in water security in the face of climate change. Wetlands, high-Andean bofedales and native forests act as natural regulators of the hydrological cycle, contributing to aquifer recharge and maintaining the overall water balance. Likewise, they regulate surface flows by releasing water gradually and retain water in times of scarcity, while mitigating floods by absorbing excess during intense rainfall.

In response to these challenges, it is essential to prioritise public and private investment in monitoring and sustainable management of water resources with the active participation of communities and the population in their management. In general, to reduce social vulnerability and ensure the quality of water services, productivity and sustainable and resilient development in Bolivia.

8.2 Main climate vulnerabilities of Water Resources

Water resources in Bolivia face growing vulnerability due to climate change, which alters precipitation regimes, accelerates glacier retreat and multiplies extreme events. Towards the end of the twenty-first century, a general decrease in precipitation of around 5% is projected, with strong regional variability: increases of up to 18% in the Amazon and Lake Titicaca basins, and reductions of up to 16% in the central valleys and the Altiplano. In 2023, precipitation in the central Andes and the western Amazon fell by up to 70%, drastically affecting river levels in the south-western region (Torrico, 2025).

Glacier retreat constitutes a critical threat: Bolivia has lost at least 42% of the surface area of its glaciers, with an annual retreat of 1,5 m and a loss of depth of up to 3 m. This reduction limits the availability of flows in dry seasons, generating prolonged periods of water scarcity. In El Alto, for example, water demand could increase by up to 53% by 2036 compared with 2011, while in Alto Beni the availability of springs could decrease by up to 69% (OIEA, 2024; Ramírez, 2001).

Extreme hydrometeorological events are intensifying in frequency and intensity: droughts, floods, hailstorms and strong winds impact food security and agricultural systems, especially in tropical regions and valleys. In Cobija, recent floods affected 4.500 families and damaged rural and urban infrastructure; in La Paz, river overflows destroyed public infrastructure. These conditions, together with deforestation in the lowlands, reduce biodiversity and the water regulation capacity of ecosystems (Torrico, 2020).

The consequences span multiple sectors. In agriculture, reduced flows limit irrigation, decrease crop yields and pastures for livestock, compromising household incomes and food security. In energy, lower water availability affects hydroelectric generation and raises urban pumping and treatment costs. At the social level, poor water quality and limited sanitation coverage increase health risks, especially in rural communities. Finally, the greater recurrence of floods and droughts causes asset losses, forced migration and greater inequalities in household water management, reducing the country's socioeconomic resilience.

8.5 Integrated contribution and co-benefits of the sector

Food security.

Energy transition.

Integrated water resources management is essential to guarantee the sustainability and efficiency of hydroelectric generation and to advance the energy transition. Deforestation and fires.

The conservation and restoration of water recharge zones (Target 1) and the prevention of land degradation (Target 4) are key to protecting ecosystems and reducing susceptibility to forest fires, by maintaining vegetation cover and soil moisture. The construction of macro- and micro-reservoirs (Target 3) increases the availability of water for extinguishing fires and for irrigation in dry seasons, reducing the pressure to expand the agricultural frontier. Target 6 strengthens hydrometeorological risk management with early warning systems and response plans that can integrate fire prevention and fighting, while improving the efficient use of water (Targets 7 and 8) reduces pressure on forest ecosystems by optimising the resource in existing agricultural areas.

Biodiversity conservation and ecosystem recovery.

Reforestation and revegetation with native species are fundamental to preserving habitats, creating ecological corridors and promoting the recovery of degraded ecosystems. Planning instruments can define environmental conservation zones, biological corridors and management priorities to protect biodiversity and guarantee the ecological functionality of the landscape. In addition, improving the quality of water bodies and increasing the volume of treated wastewater reduces pollution in rivers, lakes and wetlands, protecting the species that depend on these ecosystems. Controlling biological and chemical pollutants strengthens the health of aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, especially in wetlands and protected areas that are strategic for Bolivia's NDC 3.0.

9 Synergies and Co-benefits for Sustainable Development

The NDC 3.0 as a strategic instrument for sustainable development

The NDC 3.0 of the Plurinational State of Bolivia should not be interpreted solely as a climate commitment to the international community, but as a national planning instrument that articulates multiple public policies towards integral, just and resilient development. Its implementation represents an opportunity to transform current patterns of production and consumption, strengthen sovereignty over natural resources and align climate action with the development model of Living Well (Vivir Bien) and the principles of Mother Earth.

This NDC incorporates measures that not only reduce emissions or strengthen adaptation, but also actively contribute to meeting the targets of the PDES 2021–2025, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and Law 300. In this way, the NDC 3.0 ceases to be an “environmental burden” and becomes a strategic roadmap for generating well-being, inclusion and resilience in the face of climate risks.

Sectoral synergies and climate co-benefits of the integrated approach

The NDC 3.0 promotes a systemic and intersectoral approach in which the targets of each sector generate synergies with others, amplifying their positive impacts. This integrality is expressed in multiple nexuses: forests that protect watersheds and enable hydroelectric generation; renewable energy that reduces pressure on forest ecosystems; agroforestry that captures carbon, improves soil fertility and conserves biodiversity; waste management that generates biogas and compost, reducing emissions and contributing to food security; and wastewater treatment that improves public health, reduces waterborne diseases and provides water for irrigation in a circular scheme.

These interactions not only reinforce the climate effectiveness of the NDC, but also build more resilient, regenerative and economically viable territorial systems. Mainstreaming the gender, interculturality, youth and indigenous peoples approaches also makes it possible to ensure that the benefits reach populations in situations of vulnerability, recognising their rights and their knowledge.

Long-term social, economic and environmental co-benefits

The implementation of the NDC 3.0 will generate a wide range of co-benefits that go beyond the climate sphere. In the social sphere, an improvement in quality of life is expected through better public health, greater access to sustainable basic services, green employment and recognition of the work of waste pickers and community actors. Food security, rural education through decentralised energy and community resilience to extreme climate events will be strengthened.

In the economic sphere, climate action drives technological innovation, industrialisation with sovereignty over resources such as lithium and hydrogen, the strengthening of the bioeconomy, energy and water efficiency, and the opening of sustainable markets for agroecological and forest products. These elements diversify Bolivia's productive matrix and improve international competitiveness.

In the environmental sphere, the NDC 3.0 contributes to conserving forests, regulating the hydrological cycle, avoiding soil degradation, reducing pressure on fossil fuels, and protecting vulnerable ecosystems and key biodiversity. Recognising, communicating and making these co-benefits visible is essential to mobilise climate finance, generate strategic alliances and position Bolivia as a benchmark in climate action with ecological justice and a rights-based approach.

11 National implementation mechanisms of the NDC 3.0

11.1 Principles and international framework

The implementation of Bolivia's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) is based on the principles of the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, as well as on current national legislation. Notable among these are the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, equity and climate justice, which guide action proportional to the conditions of a country that is highly vulnerable to climate change and has limited economic capacities.

Bolivia combines national efforts with full openness to international cooperation, consistent with Articles 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11 and 13 of the Paris Agreement. This includes financing, technology transfer, capacity building and both market and non-market cooperation mechanisms. NDC 3.0 also ensures coherence with the SDGs, the 2030 Agenda, the Global Adaptation Framework approved at COP28 and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, linking climate action with integral development.

NDC 3.0 is conceived as a dynamic instrument that constitutes an intermediate milestone towards carbon neutrality and resilience by 2050, in alignment with the Global Stocktake (GST) and the long-term international trajectory.

For Bolivia, adaptation is a strategic priority, given the high climate vulnerability and the current economic crisis that limits the national response capacity. Nevertheless, the country also reaffirms its commitment to mitigation actions, especially in energy, forests and agriculture and livestock, recognising that both dimensions are complementary and necessary for resilient, low-carbon development.

11.2 Means of implementation: financing climate ambition

Bolivia recognises that, even with robust mitigation and adaptation measures, the impacts of climate change generate irreversible losses and damages to ecosystems, infrastructure and livelihoods. In this framework, NDC 3.0 underlines the need to strengthen international financing mechanisms for loss and damage, including the fund created under the UNFCCC, and to establish national management and compensation mechanisms that link the State response with social and community protection

Fulfilment of NDC 3.0 requires domestic efforts and substantive international support. The unconditional commitments will be implemented within the framework of Bolivian institutions and regulations, led by the MMAYA and APMT and the competent bodies of the State, in accordance with Law 300, DS 1696 and DS 5264.

However, the conditional targets, which represent the greatest transformation potential, depend on the availability of means of implementation in line with the principles of the UNFCCC. The total cost of NDC 3.0 is estimated to far exceed the country's public investment capacity, which makes access to predictable, concessional and non-debt-generating international financing essential.

Bolivia will mobilise resources from:

  • Multilateral and bilateral funds (GCF, GEF, AF and bilateral cooperation), through robust, high-impact projects.
  • Regional and multilateral development banks (CAF, IDB, World Bank, among others), exploring programmatic loans, guarantees and investment funds that attract private capital.
  • Mechanisms under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement (6.2, 6.4 and 6.8), promoting projects under the carbon market and non-market approaches, and participating in other mechanisms under criteria of sovereignty and respect for rights, ensuring fair benefits for local communities.
  • Private investment and sustainable finance, through an enabling environment for renewable energy, energy efficiency, electromobility, bioeconomy, circular economy and water management projects. This includes incentives, removal of regulatory barriers and the promotion of green and social bonds.

11.3 Technology transfer and capacity building

The transition towards a low-carbon and resilient model requires clean, appropriate and culturally suitable technologies, together with the knowledge to adapt and maintain them. Priority sectors include:

  • Energy: renewable energy, electricity storage, green hydrogen, electromobility and local manufacturing of components.
  • Agriculture and livestock: precision agriculture, biotechnology, efficient irrigation systems and satellite monitoring.
  • Forestry: real-time deforestation monitoring, product traceability and fire prevention.
  • Waste: energy recovery, advanced recycling and data management for the circular economy.
  • Water resources: wastewater treatment and reuse, quality monitoring, hydrological modelling and early warnings.

International cooperation must guarantee a fair and transparent transfer, avoiding technological dependencies, while Bolivia will invest in strengthening human and institutional capacities: research centres, curricular updating in universities and institutes, and mass training programmes involving technicians, producers, entrepreneurs and communities.

11.4 Coordination mechanisms

The governance of NDC 3.0 is organised under an inter-institutional, intergovernmental and multi-stakeholder approach. The MMAyA and the APMT lead implementation, in coordination with the Ministry of Development Planning (MPD), the Ministry of Economy and Public Finance (MEFP) and the sectoral ministries (MDRyT, MHE and others).

Technical work is channelled through seven sectoral working groups (energy, water resources, forests, agriculture and livestock, waste, cross-cutting issues and financing), responsible for planning, execution and monitoring.

The participation of civil society, indigenous peoples, youth organisations, women's platforms, academia and the private sector will guarantee legitimacy, innovation and social oversight, strengthening national ownership of NDC 3.0.

11.5 Strategic implementation guidelines

Bolivia organises the implementation of NDC 3.0 into seven interdependent lines:

  • a. Integrated climate planning: linking climate targets with the SPIE (PDES, PSDI, PTDI, etc.).
  • b. Multilevel governance: national, subnational and social coordination through sectoral working groups and the Plurinational Council.
  • c. Regulatory and institutional adjustment: harmonisation of laws, institutional strengthening and technical competencies, with periodic reviews and an evaluation in 2030.
  • d. Climate budget: start in 2026 of climate budget tagging and biennial transparency reports.
  • e. Climate finance and cooperation: mobilisation of national and international funds, trust funds and innovative instruments.
  • f. Territorialisation and scaling up: integration of climate action into PTDI, POA and Life Plans, and execution of large-scale projects.
  • g. Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV): consolidation of sectoral MRV, and centralised systems such as the SMTCC, INFOSPIE, RENAPP, all aligned with the Enhanced Transparency Framework.

11.6 Short-term priorities and increased ambition

The submission of NDC 3.0 is not an end point, but a fundamental milestone within a dynamic process of planning, action, learning and continuous improvement. The immediate challenge is to translate political commitment into concrete and measurable results on the ground, while at the same time laying the foundations for a progressive increase in ambition in future cycles, consistent with the ratchet mechanism of the Paris Agreement.

Development of detailed sectoral roadmaps

Following the approval of this NDC, each sector (energy, water resources, forests, agriculture and livestock, and waste) shall prepare detailed roadmaps setting out policies, regulations, priority projects, timelines, institutional responsibilities, monitoring indicators and investment needs for the period 2026–2030. These instruments will be the basis for resource mobilisation and the coordinated management of sectoral and subnational implementation.

Strengthening of the enhanced transparency framework

The national multisectoral Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system will be consolidated, guaranteeing rigorous, transparent and timely tracking of mitigation and adaptation targets. This includes improving the collection of primary data at sectoral level, developing methodologies consistent with IPCC guidelines, and integrating indicators of social, economic and environmental co-benefits. Transparency will be strengthened through periodic public reports, enabling the active participation of civil society and social oversight.

Implementation of the Safeguards Information System (SIS)

In parallel with the MRV, the Safeguards Information System (SIS) will be made fully operational, to monitor respect for the Cancun safeguards in all NDC actions. The SIS will document how the measures contribute to the protection of biodiversity, the rights of indigenous peoples, gender equity, youth inclusion and citizen participation, guaranteeing that climate action is socially and environmentally sustainable from design through to execution.

Strategy for mobilising stakeholders and resources

A proactive strategy for the dissemination and ownership of the NDC at national and international level will be implemented. Domestically, platforms for dialogue and participation with civil society, the private sector, academia and subnational governments will be strengthened. Internationally, Bolivia will deploy active climate diplomacy to build strategic alliances with cooperation partners, investors and multilateral organisations, positioning the NDC as the country's calling card to attract sustainable investment.

As part of this strategy, a pipeline of bankable projects will be structured that can be submitted to multilateral funds (GCF, GEF, AF), development banks (CAF, IDB, WB, others) and green financing schemes, prioritising projects with high impact in mitigation, adaptation and social co-benefits.

Technology transfer and capacity building

The next steps include the promotion of technology partnerships in strategic sectors (renewable energy, electromobility, green hydrogen, resilient agriculture, forest fire management, waste and water), guaranteeing that the transfer is fair and culturally appropriate. Domestically, investment will be made in human and institutional capacities through the updating of university and technical curricula, the strengthening of research centres and the mass training of key actors (technicians, producers, entrepreneurs and communities).

Institutionalisation and permanent capacities

The strengthening of technical climate change units in sectoral ministries and subnational governments is planned, to ensure the sustainability of actions over time. This process will make it possible to integrate climate management into the national planning and budgeting systems, and to guarantee consistency in implementation beyond political cycles.

Knowledge management and innovation

A national platform will be established for the exchange of experiences and results from NDC implementation, making it possible to disseminate lessons learned, scale up good practices and foster climate innovation incubators in partnership with universities and the private sector.

Learning cycle and progressive increase in ambition

The implementation of NDC 3.0 is conceived as a process of continuous learning. The results of the MRV, the information from the SIS and the accumulated experiences will feed back into the design of policies and projects, correcting errors and replicating successes.
