Chile's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) — 2025 Update

Chile has consistently demonstrated its willingness to assume ambitious climate commitments that collaborate with global efforts to address the climate crisis. And it has done so as a State policy, transcending governments and political colors. Our country ratified the Paris Agreement in 2017, which marks ten years in 2025, and we attended in 2015. Since then, Chile has steadily raised its goals and has institutionalized its climate commitments in a series of public policies, including a Framework Law on Climate Change. Since its enactment in 2022, this law transformed climate action into a State commitment, establishing the goal of being carbon neutral by 2050 at the latest, involving ministries, services, regional governments, and municipalities in this objective. Although some deny it or choose to ignore it, climate change is today a problem that is already affecting people, our cities, and economies. Not addressing it with urgency and determination will put our well-being and the survival of life on earth at risk. Far from being a brake on development, climate policies in our country have meant investment, new jobs, and better well-being for Chileans. This has been demonstrated over the last decade. For example, Chile took 90 years to install a capacity of 5,500 MW of coal-fired power plants, but only 10 years to reach the same amount in solar energy. And it will take us less than 3 years (by 2026) to exceed this figure in clean energy storage capacity, which ensures that energy is not lost and can permanently supply the entire energy system. That's not all. In terms of electromobility, 10 years ago there were no electric buses in public transportation, and by the end of 2025, 3 out of 5 buses in Santiago will be high-standard electric buses. Starting from our Government, moreover, this fleet has been expanding to the rest of the country's regions, bringing environmental and health benefits to the main cities of the country, as well as greater comfort and dignity to their inhabitants. Today our cities are also better adapted and prepared to withstand the impacts of the climate crisis: during these years we have invested heavily in resilient infrastructure such as floodable urban parks, alluvial control works, and tanks to ensure the provision of drinking water during extreme weather events. Likewise, we are preparing our regions and municipalities for fire, flood, and drought prevention. We still have a way to go. This last decade of climate action has allowed us to evaluate which strategies work and which we should expand. Aware of these learnings, we move forward to face this central challenge. We are convinced that Chile's development is closely linked to the capacity we show to implement policies and actions to address climate change. Chile today can contribute significantly to the world in this process, while at the same time opening new opportunities for sustainable and inclusive development in our country. Therefore, our commitment is to advance in energy transition and electromobility, to clean the air in our cities, and to ensure that important ecosystems such as forests, wetlands, and watershed headwaters remain healthy. Chile's new NDC places even greater value on public-private collaboration as an engine to mobilize the country toward sustainable development. Additionally, it recognizes in an unprecedented way the key role of municipalities and regional governments in meeting the proposed goals. Its hallmark is putting people and territories at the center of climate action, convinced that without them no just transition is possible. We must ensure that this socio-ecological transition process addresses past mistakes and generates equitable development that leaves no one behind. The NDC is more than a symbolic declaration. It is a commitment we assume with the world, reinforcing the idea that together we are stronger and that no one can save themselves alone. It is also a compass to achieve the sustainable development we want for Chile in the coming decades, a roadmap with a plan of concrete actions for a better future. I especially thank the teams, institutions, scientific community, social organizations, private sector, and citizens involved in the process who allowed for a broad, cross-cutting, and committed dialogue. Because the future we dream of is built by all of us together, this NDC is one more step toward a fairer, more dignified country better prepared for the climate crisis. Gabriel Boric Font President of the Republic of Chile

UPDATE OF THE NATIONALLY DETERMINED CONTRIBUTION 2025

1. FOREWORD

Chile has demonstrated a strong commitment to climate action through a permanent and consistent State policy on this matter, recognizing that the triple planetary crisis of biodiversity loss, climate change, and pollution is a global problem that requires cross-cutting efforts. Therefore, the country established a cross-cutting, general, and mandatory regulatory framework through the enactment of Law No. 21,455, the Framework Law on Climate Change (LMCC, 2022). This regulation aims to establish the institutional framework, instruments, and procedures necessary to fulfill the commitments established in the Paris Agreement, the mitigation of greenhouse gases (GHG), and the implementation of adaptation measures. This law formalizes and establishes the obligation to develop and update, periodically and progressively, a series of short-, medium-, and long-term public policy instruments, highlighting that the country shall achieve and maintain GHG neutrality no later than 2050, to transition toward low-emission development and adapt to climate change, reducing vulnerability and increasing resilience to the adverse effects of climate change. This update of the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) is the first carried out under the mandate of the LMCC, and represents a progression in relation to the commitments contained in the NDC submitted to the UNFCCC on April 9, 2020. The update of Chile's NDC constitutes the country's renewed commitment to keep the increase in global average temperature below 1.5°C, and to mobilize public and private investments on the path toward low-carbon and climate-resilient development, that considers economic, social, and environmental variables in a balanced manner for the period 2025-2035. The process to update Chile's NDC was participatory, cross-cutting, and broad, gathering opinions and proposals through early citizen participation during 2024. This process took place between June and September of that year, through 16 regional workshops and 4 multi-stakeholder workshops that included the participation of academia, small businesses and producers, medium and large companies, civil society and its organizations. The workshops recorded the participation of 337 people, with 1,100 comments collected, while 92 people participated in the multi-stakeholder workshops and 441 comments were gathered. This was followed by a closing seminar, in which the systematization and analysis of the results obtained were carried out. 1

As a result of this process, and with the collaboration of various State administration bodies, a preliminary draft was prepared and submitted to citizen consultation between January 17 and April 11, 2025, during which period observations were received from various stakeholders, including civil society, the private sector, academia, and various public services. In parallel with the citizen consultation, the preliminary draft was sent to various supporting entities, which are the collaborative bodies for climate change management as established in the LMCC. Within this process, notable contributions include the preliminary report from the Scientific Advisory Committee on Climate Change, the Report from the Interministerial Technical Team on Climate Change, and the opinion of the National Consultative Council for Sustainability and Climate Change. Additionally, in 2024, a Carbon Neutrality and Resilience Committee (CCNyR) was convened, composed of distinguished experts along with national climate leaders from the private sector and NGOs, who were invited to participate in this instance given their relevance in the implementation of national climate change commitments. This committee generated 14 concrete consensus proposals to accelerate the transition toward GHG emissions neutrality and resilience in the country. Some of these proposals are also included in this NDC. In 2023, within the framework of the Twenty-Eighth Conference of the Parties of the UNFCCC (COP) in Dubai, the report on the First Global Stocktake was presented, designed under the Paris Agreement framework to assess the global response to the climate crisis. This NDC must be guided by the results of that report, to ensure that its level of ambition is consistent with the objectives of the Paris Agreement. That report indicated that the collective effort is not in line with the long-term objectives of the Paris Agreement, and therefore the implementation of climate action must be accelerated and scaled up, extending to the entire economy and aligned with long-term strategies. Among its main conclusions, the agreement to move toward the phase-out of fossil fuels in energy systems, accelerate the reduction of non-carbon dioxide GHGs—particularly methane—and conserve and restore ocean and coastal ecosystems stands out. All these conclusions are addressed in this NDC, demonstrating Chile's commitment to climate ambition. The Paris Agreement establishes, in its Article 7, the Global Goal on Adaptation to strengthen adaptive capacity, increase resilience, and reduce vulnerability to climate change, in the context of keeping the global temperature increase well below 2°C and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. At the fifth session of the Conference of the Parties held in 2023 in Dubai, serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (CMA 5), a framework was agreed to operationalize the goal, defining thematic targets and targets associated with the adaptation cycle. The thematic targets include: water resources, food security, health, ecosystems and biodiversity, infrastructure and human settlements, poverty eradication and livelihoods, and cultural heritage. The adaptation cycle targets establish progress in vulnerability and risk assessment, including early warning systems; planning; implementation; monitoring, evaluation, and learning, promoting participatory approaches based on science and traditional knowledge. In this context, Chile aligns its new Nationally Determined Contribution with the Global Goal on Adaptation framework, incorporating specific commitments aimed at strengthening national adaptation capacities. These commitments include actions aimed at improving adaptive planning, increasing resilience in vulnerable sectors and territories, protecting ecosystems, safeguarding livelihoods, and ensuring access to essential services in the face of climate impacts.

Additionally, at COP29 in Baku (2024), Chile recognized the urgency of the triple crisis and reaffirmed its commitment to the sustained reduction of its GHG emissions across all sectors of the economy until reaching net zero emissions, through a just, phased, and sustained transition over time that helps halt global warming and its impacts on the planet and its inhabitants. In particular, regarding policies, strategies, and plans, Chile has strengthened the coordination of climate action on adaptation and mitigation at the national scale through the update of the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan; the development of 7 sectoral climate change mitigation plans and 12 sectoral climate change adaptation plans in accordance with the requirements of the LMCC. Achieving the neutrality goal, and a future sustainable and climate-resilient development for society in the coming decades, implies addressing the impacts that this transition process will have on the economy and society as a whole. To this end, Chile's 2020 NDC presented a Social Pillar to articulate and generate synergies between the country's international climate commitments and the national agenda on this matter, clearly demonstrating the close link that exists between the climate and socio-environmental dimensions. This new NDC deepens this work with the incorporation of a National Strategy for a Just Socio-Ecological Transition (ENTSEJ), which reflects a vision of future development for the country in the next decade in the context of the triple planetary crisis, and the transition process that begins from it. The ENTSEJ is aimed at promoting a sustainable, inclusive, and climate-resilient economic and social model; it aligns with the principles of the LMCC, and adopts the necessary measures to guarantee a just, informed, and participatory process for workers, communities, and territories. The above is in line with the principles and guidelines of the Regional Agreement on Access to Information, Public Participation and Justice in Environmental Matters (Escazú Agreement) adopted by Chile in 2022. This ENTSEJ seeks to leverage the benefits of sectors or activities that may have a multiplier effect on employment and social benefits, while helping to decarbonize and build resilience in the national economy. At the same time, it places a focus on and considers special measures in 5 transition territories of the country. Regarding integrated mitigation and adaptation, commitments are included on forests, wetlands, and ecosystem conservation, including oceans. Through this component of the NDC, the aim is to create conditions that increase the resilience of biodiversity and ecosystems to forest fires. These matters correspond to priority lines of work for the country, which is why they have been maintained and highlighted for the NDC update process. Regarding means of implementation, the "Strategy for Climate Capacity Development and Empowerment" (EDCEC) will be implemented, developed in 2024 to strengthen sectoral, national, and subnational capacities; the Climate Change Financial Strategy will be updated and implemented during 2025; and during the same year, the Strategy for Development and Technology Transfer for Climate Change will be updated and implemented.

Additionally, this NDC incorporates commitments in the subnational component for capacity building and consolidating multilevel governance that strengthens the resilience of communities and local territories. These commitments are framed within the fulfillment of the obligations defined in the LMCC, which mandates the development of subnational climate change management instruments at the regional and municipal levels for mitigation, adaptation, and risk management related to climate change, as well as to contribute to the fulfillment of international commitments adopted by Chile in these matters. 3. SOCIAL PILLAR OF JUST SOCIO-ECOLOGICAL TRANSITION

3.1 Chile's NDC update in a context of just socio-ecological transition and sustainable development

The impacts of climate change have a strongly inequitable expression across territories and social groups, accentuating pre-existing social vulnerabilities and becoming factors that intensify the social, political, and institutional crisis. Chile faces deep structural inequalities, exacerbated by climate change, that disproportionately affect vulnerable groups, such as indigenous communities, women, children, adolescents, elderly persons, persons with disabilities, chronic illnesses, or those in poverty and social exclusion. 29In parallel, environmental degradation resulting from economic activities, together with the effects of climate change, put the conservation of vulnerable ecosystems at risk. According to the Scientific Advisory Committee on Climate Change (Deuman, 2024), in the last 29three decades Chile has lost 20% of its wetlands. Chile is a diverse country in geographic and social terms, so adaptation plans or measures applied in one territory are not necessarily effective in others, making it essential to emphasize rurality, decentralization, and the social demands of local populations. 29Existing gender and socioeconomic inequalities, combined with geographic and climatic differences, reinforce the need to incorporate a gender, intercultural, and territorial approach in planning climate change adaptation and mitigation initiatives, in order to reduce and ultimately overcome gender inequalities. According to data from Chile's National Statistics Institute, a 3031gender gap of 18.9% less in income for employed women compared to men was identified. Gender inequalities, in terms of roles, access to and control of resources, decision-making, and access to information, make women more vulnerable to climate change impacts. The country ranks 31first regionally in the United Nations Human Development Index (HDI) ranking, placing it 44th worldwide. The poverty rate in Chile is 6.5%, and it has been reported that 5.9% of households lack access to basic housing and environment services. 29In turn, the implementation of the 2030 Agenda and the Paris Agreement establish the need to incorporate social aspects in climate management, addressing challenges related to inequality, housing, gender, education, and health, among others. In this regard, the Global Goal on Adaptation of the Paris Agreement, adopted at COP28, seeks to substantially reduce the adverse effects of climate change on poverty eradication and livelihoods, particularly by promoting the use of adaptive social protection measures for the benefit of all; and to protect cultural heritage from the effects of climate risks through the development of adaptation strategies to preserve cultural practices and sites of heritage importance and through the design of climate-resilient infrastructure, guided by traditional knowledge, indigenous peoples' knowledge, and local knowledge systems. For its part, Chile's 2020 NDC unprecedentedly established a Social Pillar as an important part of climate commitments, in order to "enhance the synergy between Chile's climate commitments and the national agenda on social development, social and ecological systems, clearly demonstrating the close link between the climate and socio-environmental dimensions." The Social Pillar sought to mainstream 32the concept of climate justice, highlighting the greater vulnerability of certain groups to climate change, seeking and ensuring their inclusion, equity, and equality of opportunity in the transition process. Likewise, in the strengthening of the NDC in 2022, the "Just Socio-Ecological Transition" was integrated, where the term "Socio-Ecological" allows for greater visibility of the interrelation between social and ecological systems as systems independent of each other. In this way, the concept of "Just Socio-Ecological Transition" encompasses social, economic, institutional, and ecological components, aiming at a refocusing of economic development that underpins the social, economic, and environmental inequities of the country, seeking to protect ecosystems and people's well-being.

The Just Socio-Ecological Transition is a process that, through social dialogue and collective empowerment, seeks the transformation of society into one that is resilient and equitable, capable of confronting the social, ecological, and climate crisis. Thus, continuing this evolution, the Social Pillar of the NDC has been renamed "Just Socio-Ecological Transition Pillar." Chile's 2020 NDC considered the application of the social pillar of just transition and sustainable development and, for this purpose, a set of criteria was established that must be met in the design, implementation, and monitoring of each of the NDC commitments. These criteria corresponded to the following: synergy with the Sustainable Development Goals, just transition, water security, equity and gender equality, cost-efficiency, nature-based solutions (NbS), consideration of types of knowledge, and active participation. It should be noted that the 2020 NDC was developed prior to the entry into force of the FCCL. This new regulation establishes a set of legal principles that must be considered in all policies, plans, programs, standards, actions, and other instruments issued or executed within the framework of the FCCL. There are a total of 15 legal principles: scientific, cost-effectiveness, ecosystem approach, equity and climate justice, non-regression, citizen participation, precautionary, preventive, progressivity, territoriality, climate urgency, transparency, cross-cutting nature, territoriality, coherence, and flexibility³³. Given the application of these principles, both with respect to the NDC and all other climate change management policies, plans, and instruments, it is not necessary to reiterate the criteria of the previously referenced 2020 NDC. Recognizing that the effects of climate change and climate action measures have differentiated effects on the territory, ecosystems, people, and social groups due to their particular characteristics, climate change management must consider a fair allocation of burdens, costs, and benefits³⁴, protecting vulnerable ecosystems and safeguarding human rights, the right to development of present and future generations, and the health and comprehensive well-being of the population. In parallel, a territorial approach is sought, prioritizing the elimination of pre-existing social gaps and historical environmental burdens. The FCCL requires a fair allocation of burdens, costs, and benefits in climate management³⁵, making it necessary for CCMIs to monitor their positive and negative social impacts. Well-designed climate policies generate co-benefits in quality of life, reducing inequalities and promoting long-term sustainable development³⁶. In this regard, the National Strategy for Just Socio-Ecological Transition (NSJSET)³⁷ proposes guidelines, objectives, goals, and indicators that guide Chile's transition toward a sustainable, low-carbon, environmentally conscious, and resilient society, within the framework of the triple global crisis of biodiversity loss, climate change, and pollution. Furthermore, this strategy seeks to ensure the application of human rights approaches, territorial approach, gender equality, and decent work. In the transition process toward carbon neutrality and climate change resilience, it is vital to safeguard job security and people's quality of life, ensuring that adaptation and mitigation actions protect employment and/or help with reconversion, and promote the sustainable transformation of the productive system. The International Labour Organization (ILO) emphasizes the need for a just transition toward a greener and low-carbon economy, which must also promote decent work and leave no one behind³⁶, making it necessary to have a methodology that allows measuring and managing employment impacts due to climate change, related to adaptation and mitigation³⁹.

3.3 Ancestral best practices and cultural heritage

Local communities, traditional communities, and indigenous peoples are references for achieving low-emission development that is respectful of nature and resilient to climate change. 4. MITIGATION COMPONENT

4.1 Context

Regarding mitigation, the Paris Agreement establishes, in its Article 2.1.a, the following long-term objective: "Holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C." To achieve this objective, the Agreement, in its Article 4.1, proposes to the Parties, on the basis of equity and in the context of sustainable development and efforts to eradicate poverty, the following:

  • Reach a global peak of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions as soon as possible; and
  • Rapidly reduce greenhouse gas emissions to achieve a balance between emissions and absorption by sinks.

To advance toward these objectives, the Agreement establishes that Parties must submit, every five years, their contributions and mitigation measures through Nationally Determined Contributions (Articles 4.2 and 4.9). Furthermore, it states that countries should strive to formulate and communicate long-term strategies for low greenhouse gas emission development (Article 4.19). Additionally, it indicates that each country's successive NDCs will represent a progression beyond the current NDC, and will reflect the highest possible ambition taking into account common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in light of national circumstances (Article 4.3). Chile has advanced in the transformations needed to achieve mitigation goals; among them, agreements for the retirement of coal-fired thermoelectric plants by 2040, the promotion of renewable energy generation in its energy matrix, the promotion of electromobility in its public transportation systems, and energy efficiency. Additionally, from the presidency of the Forum of Environment Ministers of Latin America and the Caribbean in 2024, the country promoted the "Declaration on Methane Reduction and Waste Management in the NDCs of Latin America and the Caribbean." 44The evidence collected in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report shows that continued GHG emissions will lead to increased global warming, in the most optimistic estimates, of 1.5°C in the near term according to modeled scenarios and pathways. This will intensify multiple simultaneous hazards, and only deep, rapid, and sustained emission reductions would allow a perceptible slowdown of global warming within approximately two decades.

4.4 Carbon Pricing Instruments

Carbon pricing instruments represent one of the most cost-effective tools for incentivizing the reduction of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across all sectors of the economy. By reflecting the socio-environmental cost of emissions in investment and consumption decisions, these instruments allow for assigning an economic value to carbon and promoting a shift toward cleaner technologies and practices. The World Bank, OECD, and other international bodies have consistently recommended the use of these instruments, highlighting their key role in the transition toward low-carbon economies. 71 72 In this context, the progressive elimination of inefficient fossil fuel subsidies has been recognized by multilateral bodies as an urgent priority to align economic signals with climate objectives. The Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement and multiple COP decisions have reiterated this need. Likewise, organizations such as the IMF and IPCC recommend strengthening carbon taxes and expanding their sectoral coverage, considering social safeguards and just transition mechanisms. Since 2013, Chile has had a Social Price of Carbon (SPC) for use in evaluating projects, programs, and public policies, which has been updated upward on more than one occasion (most recently in 2025) and currently stands at a value of 71.1 USD/Ton CO eq. The SPC allows for internalizing the social costs of greenhouse gas 732(GHG) emissions in the social evaluation of public projects, promoting investment decisions more aligned with sustainable and low-emission development. By reflecting climate externalities, the SPC guides the allocation of resources toward alternatives that contribute to Chile's compliance with international commitments, such as the Paris Agreement and the Sustainable Development Goals. In addition to its function in the ex ante evaluation of investments, the estimation of the SPC provides a key economic signal for the design of fiscal and regulatory instruments, such as Pigouvian taxes, which seek to correct market failures through the internalization of negative externalities. In this sense, the SPC strengthens the public policy formulation process and contributes to consolidating a resilient development agenda compatible with long-term climate objectives, by more accurately reflecting the economic value of avoiding GHG emissions. On the other hand, since 2017, the country has had a CO₂ emissions tax applicable to fixed sources exceeding 25,000 tons of CO₂ per year, known as the green tax. This tax establishes an explicit carbon price in specific sectors, thus promoting energy efficiency and the adoption of cleaner technologies. While it represents a significant step in incorporating environmental costs into fiscal policy, challenges still persist regarding its price level and sectoral coverage. Given that the current scope is insufficient to address the magnitude of the climate challenge, it is necessary to move more decisively toward pricing instruments more in line with the global climate reality and with the climate objectives of the Paris Agreement. This implies more effectively incorporating environmental impacts into economic decisions and eliminating distortions that may favor fossil fuel consumption without reflecting their true socio-environmental cost. This process must always consider the safeguarding of the country's financial, fiscal, and macroeconomic stability, while also ensuring a just and orderly transition. In addition to carbon pricing instruments, carbon markets, both national and international, can play a complementary role in meeting Chile's mitigation targets. The strategic use of these mechanisms offers an opportunity to mobilize financial resources, accelerate the implementation of climate actions, and strengthen the transparency and environmental integrity of the commitments assumed by the country. Within this framework, Chile has promoted emissions reduction projects through the bilateral agreement with Japan, through the Joint Crediting Mechanism, and has deepened its international participation through the signing of Bilateral Implementation Agreements with countries such as Switzerland and Singapore, thus consolidating its commitment to climate cooperation under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

5. ADAPTATION COMPONENT

5.1. Context

Chile recognizes the urgency of adaptation processes to strengthen the country's resilience to climate change impacts, in line with the objectives set forth in the LMCC. The implementation of nature-based solutions, the protection and restoration of ecosystems, and the sustainable use of resources are fundamental pillars. There are areas of work that contribute to both mitigation and adaptation, which is why they are addressed from an integrated approach and synergistically within public policies. 6.1 Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF)

6.1.1 Context

The 2006 IPCC Guidelines for national GHG inventory preparation unite under the concept of LULUCF all greenhouse gas emissions and removals resulting from human action in activities related to land use, such as forest management and conservation, grasslands, wetlands, and settlements. In this sector, captures from biomass accumulation are accounted for, which must be balanced with national emissions to achieve and maintain GHG neutrality. One of the most effective activities for capturing carbon from the atmosphere is afforestation and forest management. Mitigation actions related to biomass accumulation bring benefits that contribute to climate change adaptation, contributing to water security and disaster risk reduction, as well as halting land degradation and biodiversity loss. 6.1.2.2 Wetlands

Wetland ecosystems are recognized for their capacity to capture and store large amounts of carbon, as well as to provide ecosystem services related to climate change adaptation; among them, water quality filtering and maintenance, water flow regulation, flood occurrence reduction, and temperature regulation in surrounding areas. Although there is limited information regarding the total wetland area (including peatlands), and their contribution in terms of GHG capture and storage, it is estimated that in Chile there are between 18,000 and 30,000 ecosystems of this type with an area close to 5.6 million hectares, representing almost 7.3% of the national territory. Of these, 743,000 hectares are totally or partially in urban areas. Wetlands, especially coastal, urban, and peatlands, are highly threatened by land use change, unsustainable exploitation, urban expansion and its infrastructure, and the effects of climate change, putting their long-term sustainability at risk. Likewise, progress is needed in understanding these ecosystems and establishing standardized metrics for evaluating their contribution to climate change adaptation or mitigation, as well as measuring the co-benefits of these areas, implementing actions for their conservation and sustainable use. In terms of adaptation, these metrics are linked to highlighting the contribution of peatlands to local climate change adaptation; regarding mitigation, it is oriented toward developing emission factors specific to the national context, derived from land use change in wetlands and peatlands, incorporating the loss of carbon stored in the soil, currently not considered in the NGHGI. 6.3 Ocean

The concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, and the increase in90 global average temperature, will cause severe changes in marine ecosystems and their ecosystem services due to increased acidification, deoxygenation, and sea level rise. The ocean is responsible for capturing about one-third of anthropogenic CO2 emitted to the atmosphere and absorbing about 90% of the heat resulting from these emissions. To address a greater potential contribution of the ocean to climate change mitigation and adaptation objectives, science and marine management policy need to identify and support the rapid development of ambitious actions at the local scale that can be scalable. 7. MEANS OF IMPLEMENTATION COMPONENT

This component groups together the set of commitments necessary to achieve the previously identified goals and objectives; that is, in terms of mitigation, adaptation, and integration to ensure effective, coherent, and sustainable implementation over time. The means of implementation have been grouped into three subcomponents: i) Capacity building and strengthening; ii) Technology development and transfer; iii) Financial guidelines.

7.1 International context

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Paris Agreement indicate that they will promote and facilitate, at the national, regional, and subregional levels, actions regarding education, capacity building (training of scientific, technical, and managerial personnel), and public access to information on climate change. Likewise, the Paris Agreement states that "capacity-building should enhance the capacity and ability of developing country Parties, and should facilitate technology development, dissemination and deployment; access to climate finance; relevant aspects of education, training and public awareness; and the transparent, timely and accurate communication of information." 92Furthermore, the Paris Agreement seeks to promote and facilitate greater action on technology development and transfer in order to support the implementation of said agreement sustained over a long period of time. The long-term vision for technology development and 93transfer shared by the Parties relates to the importance of fully leveraging technology development and transfer to improve the capacity to adapt to climate change and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Regarding climate finance, the Paris Agreement states that "financial flows should be made consistent with a pathway towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development." For its part, the UNFCCC defines climate finance as "local, national or transnational financing—drawn from public, private and alternative sources of financing—that seeks to support mitigation and adaptation actions that will address climate change."

7.2 National context

The implementation of measures to address climate change impacts requires a scientific knowledge base and capacity strengthening, both of individuals and institutions, to facilitate both technology transfer and access to financial resources. Likewise, it is essential to have citizen participation in the development of actions, through education, training, and citizen awareness in the field of climate change. Citizen participation is also necessary in the process of designing, implementing, monitoring, and updating climate change management instruments. Chile's Framework Law on Climate Change (LMCC) defines means of implementation as any "action, measure or process of an institutional or regulatory nature for technology development and transfer, capacity building and strengthening, and financing, among others, required for the implementation of climate change mitigation and adaptation actions." 95Additionally, Article 6 of the same law establishes the contents of the Long-Term Climate Strategy, and in particular, of the aforementioned means of implementation. 96In this line, Chile's National Climate Change Adaptation Plan 2025-2028 included in its final draft a chapter on means of implementation for national, regional, and local resilience, whose strategic lines correspond to the following:

  • Capacity building for climate change adaptation and resilience at the national, regional, and local levels.
  • Strengthening climate financing and investments.
  • Technology development and transfer for climate adaptation and resilience.

To advance in capacity building and strengthening, the Capacity Development and Climate Empowerment Strategy established as its vision that "Chile will have 97a society and institutions capable of responding to climate change challenges, with an informed, sensitized, and proactive citizenry that will contribute to the development of public policies, and will contribute from their personal and organizational spheres to the necessary changes to achieve carbon neutrality and resilience of the country by 2050 at the latest." Under this framework, in recent years several training instances have been carried out aimed at the public sector, private sector, and general citizenry, to advance with the implementation of climate actions. 94In this regard, the Ministry of Education published the document "National Framework for Comprehensive Education for Sustainability and Climate Change Adaptation" (MEISC). 98This document aims to educate in the promotion of knowledge, respect, care and protection of the environment, sustainability, and climate change adaptation throughout the educational system, actively contributing to a more just, sustainable, inclusive, and resilient society, from a perspective of global cooperation. Regarding climate finance, Chile has made progress in formalizing and consolidating the Green Climate Fund (GCF) in the country, establishing institutional arrangements that allow its proper management through a technical secretariat composed of the Ministries of Finance, Foreign Affairs, and the Environment. Also noteworthy is the issuance by the Ministry of Finance of Green Bonds and Social and Sustainable Bonds in 2019, 2020, and 2021, for a total of USD $7.727 billion, obtaining the lowest rates in the history of such instruments, to finance clean transportation, water management, and renewable energy projects, mainly. As of 2024, ESG bonds account for more than 39% of the country's debt stock. Additionally, the Ministry of Finance has developed since 2020 a methodology to measure public spending on climate change. In 2022, the Sustainable Finance Office was created, which incorporated new initiatives such as the Taxonomy of Environmentally Sustainable Economic Activities. Likewise, in 2023 Chile created the region's first Natural Capital Committee, and in 2024 presented the update of its Financial Strategy for Climate Change (EFCC), complying with the provisions of the LMCC. Regarding technology transfer, the aim is to develop, promote, and attract new and better technology, which must have a holistic and comprehensive vision of the climate problem, supporting and driving cultural, social, environmental, and economic transformations to achieve sustainable development. To this end, the Long-Term Climate Strategy establishes objectives, goals, and indicators for promoting and intensifying the transfer of knowledge, skills, techniques, or equipment, in order to increase the conservation, restoration, and sustainable management of biodiversity and natural ecosystems as carbon sinks, climate resilience, and reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

8. SUBNATIONAL COMPONENT

The impacts of climate change occur in territories, and Chile recognizes the importance of capacity building and the formation of multilevel governance to strengthen the resilience of communities and local territories, considering that the country's territorial reality is diverse and, therefore, these types of actions help reduce gaps and inequities. The country has adopted a leadership position in climate action at the global and regional level, demonstrating its continued interest through various commitments, and the implementation of instruments and policies in this area. An example of this was its incorporation into the Coalition for High Ambition Multilevel Partnerships (CHAMP) within the framework of COP28, which seeks to enhance cooperation with subnational governments in the planning, financing, implementation, and monitoring of national climate strategies. One of the main advances in this area is in the FCCL, which indicates the development of subnational management instruments at the regional and municipal level. Thus, it mandates the development of 102Regional Climate Change Action Plans (PARCC), which will be under the responsibility of the Regional Climate Change Committees (CORECC), with the purpose of collaborating in the management of this matter at the regional level, in accordance with the guidelines of the Long-Term Climate Strategy and the Sectoral Mitigation and Adaptation Plans, as well as the Strategic Water Resources Plans for Basins, where they exist. The FCCL also establishes management instruments at the local level, referring to the development of Municipal Climate Change Action Plans (PACCC). In this case, municipalities will be responsible for developing these plans, which will be consistent with the general guidelines established in the Long-Term Climate Strategy and the PARCC. Likewise, the development of Strategic Water Resources Plans for Basins is mandated, which aim to contribute to water management; identify surface and groundwater gaps; establish the water balance and its projections; diagnose the state of information on quantity, quality, infrastructure, and institutions involved in the decision-making process regarding water resources; and propose actions to address the adverse effects of climate change on water resources, in order to safeguard water security. Finally, the FCCL establishes that Disaster Risk Management instruments must incorporate climate change adaptation criteria, both in their design phase and in their development, implementation, and evaluation. For the purposes described above, a multilevel governance system has been established, including the formation of the CORECC, which must carry out planning and implementation efforts for climate change response actions, in close collaboration with the central level, municipalities, and other non-state and subnational actors.

1. Context and National Circumstances

Since the submission of the Nationally Determined Contribution ("NDC") in 2020, Chile has intensified its ongoing work in creating enabling conditions for fulfilling the country's commitments to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change ("UNFCCC"), especially through the adoption of a new national regulatory framework on climate change and the development and implementation of new policies, plans, and management instruments to advance climate action. Chile recognizes the urgency of adaptation processes to strengthen the country's resilience to climate change impacts, considering the special vulnerability affecting the country, its ecosystems, and territories. Evidence of this was the publication of Law No. 21,455, the Climate Change Framework Law ("LMCC") 1, in June 2022, which establishes a binding legal framework, both at the national and subnational levels, to establish and fulfill the objectives, goals, actions, and measures that the country commits to before the UNFCCC. The main objective of this new law is to achieve carbon neutrality and resilience by 2050 at the latest, establishing a set of short-, medium-, and long-term policies and action plans for both climate change mitigation and adaptation. In this way, climate action and adaptation become a State policy that transcends political or governmental cycles. In turn, through the LMCC, the importance of water security 3 has been highlighted, considering that it is one of the country's main needs in terms of vulnerability and resilience regarding adaptation. For this purpose, the LMCC mandates the development and implementation of Strategic Water Resources Plans at the Basin level, in each basin of the country. Likewise, the LMCC mandates that, through the Long-Term Climate Strategy 4 ("ECLP"), all adaptation policies and action plans must consider the application of nature-based solutions, with special emphasis on environmental sustainability in water use against threats and risks associated with droughts, floods, and pollution. Similarly, the ECLP will contain guidelines on risk assessment 5, and losses and damages associated with climate change, which must be integrated into the policies and action plans necessary for adaptation6 Furthermore, the LMCC - ANNEX enshrines the precautionary principle, by virtue of which the State must adopt measures to avoid risks, dangers, and prevent adverse effects of climate change 7 regarding adaptation. On the other hand, the update of the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan ("PNACC") for the period 2025-2028 reinforces the objective of advancing adaptation, articulating climate change management instruments through a common conceptual framework 8, and establishing guidelines that ensure coherence and synergy among them, to reduce vulnerability, avoid maladaptation, and increase resilience. The PNACC establishes objectives, goals, and indicators, dictating minimum actions for climate change adaptation to protect people, their fundamental rights, and ecosystems, and for biodiversity conservation in the medium and long term. Likewise, the LMCC orders the development and implementation of 12 sectoral adaptation plans, which must be reviewed and updated every five years for the following sectors: Biodiversity, Water Resources, Infrastructure, Health, Mining, Energy, Agriculture and Forestry, Fisheries and Aquaculture, Cities, Tourism, Coastal Zone, and Transportation. 9Finally, it establishes climate change action plans at the regional and municipal levels 10, which must be implemented at these territorial levels and include climate change adaptation measures, based on vulnerability, impact, and risk assessments. These plans will consider measures that must contribute to the implementation of the Sectoral Climate Change Adaptation Plans, as well as territorial measures that respond to the needs of each region. Through the climate change management instruments previously mentioned, a series of concerted actions are materialized 2 to protect people and ecosystems, with special emphasis on the most vulnerable groups and taking into account the urgent and immediate needs 11 identified in each sector, at the national and subnational scale. Adaptation is a dynamic process that encompasses essential aspects of the country's development and, therefore, must consider intersectoral and multidimensional cooperation and coordination that accounts for the complexity of the challenge. In accordance with what the draft update of the country's NDC proposes in its Social Pillar of Just Socio-Ecological Transition, Chile recognizes the need to link its economic, social, and environmental development with sustainable and science-based climate action, to advance toward an equitable, inclusive, and resilient society. 12In the Fifth National Communication to the UNFCCC and in Chile's First Biennial Transparency Report (2024), a deeper analysis of the contents of this 13 communication is presented, along with other topics related to the country's adaptation.

2. Impacts, Risks, and Vulnerabilities

Climate change is generating profound transformations in Chile, a highly vulnerable country due to its geography and climate diversity. One of the most notable changes is the sustained increase in temperatures. The year 2023 was recorded as the warmest since 1961, completing 14 consecutive years of unusually high temperatures. This increase, averaging 0.15°C per decade, has not only intensified extreme heat events but has also generated cumulative effects on ecosystems, communities, and productive sectors. In parallel, average annual accumulated precipitation has decreased from 675.3 mm to 536.4 mm, -4% per decade. This trend doubles over the last 40 years, reaching -6% per decade. There are 17 consecutive years with precipitation below the 1961-1990 average. The year 2022 ended with a 22% national deficit, and 15 of the 16 regions of the country showed precipitation deficits, with the regions of Valparaíso, Metropolitan, O'Higgins, and Arica y Parinacota showing the largest deficits around 50%. As a consequence, the mega-drought affecting the country since 2010 has intensified. This phenomenon has reduced river flows, affecting the quality and quantity of water available for human consumption and productive uses. Reservoirs have reached historic lows, and the lack of snow in the Andes Mountains exacerbates water scarcity during critical seasons. This situation puts the water security of millions of people at risk, particularly in rural and urban areas of the central zone of the country. During 2023, major floods occurred in the central-south zone of the country, with large-scale events, river rises and overflows, flooding, and activation of ravines, affecting areas from the Valparaíso region to Los Ríos. Another critical impact is the accelerated retreat of glaciers, essential reservoirs for balancing water availability during dry periods. This process directly threatens water security, especially in the central zone of the country. Additionally, glacier melting has modified mountain landscapes and ecosystems, increasing risks such as avalanches and landslides. The increase in heat waves is another concerning trend. Heat waves, combined with the mega-drought, have created conditions conducive to forest fires, whose devastating consequences include the loss of human lives, homes, critical infrastructure, crops, and biodiversity. Seasons with up to 50 heat waves have been recorded in 2020-21 in the central-south zone, where absolute maximum temperatures ranged between 30 and 37°C, while in the southern zone a total of 49 heat waves were reached in the 2021-22 season, where the highest temperature was 33.6°C. 14ANNEX In the last decade, about twenty mega-forest fires were recorded, coinciding with the mega-drought and with the highest temperatures on record for the central zone of the country. Between 2014 and 2018, 16 mega-fires occurred affecting an approximate area of 444,000 hectares, mainly between the O'Higgins and Biobío regions. 15 In the period from July 2021 to June 2022, a total of 125,335 hectares of vegetation were consumed as a result of 6,947 reported fires, with the most affected regions being La Araucanía (72,353 ha), Biobío (23,246 ha), and Ñuble (9,876 ha), with their consequent impact, not only regarding losses and damages but also regarding GHG emissions and the reduction in capture capacity. In the case of the 2022-2023 season, as of May 25, 2023, an affected area of 431,657 hectares was recorded, with the largest fires occurring in January and February, with the Biobío and La Araucanía regions being the most affected in damaged area, with 184,210 ha and 116,377 ha, respectively. The fire of February 2024, which affected the municipalities of Valparaíso, Viña del Mar, Quilpué, Villa Alemana, and Limache, is classified as one of the most serious in the last 30 years in Chile. This event consumed 9,215.9 hectares and resulted in 136 deaths and 3 presumed fatalities. According to the Research Center for Integrated Disaster Risk Management (CIGIDEN), the Planning Secretariat (SECPLA), and the Legal Medical Service (SML), urban, agricultural, and forest areas were affected, with forests being the most damaged, accounting for 53% of the total burned area. In coastal municipalities, storm surges have increased in both frequency and intensity, causing erosion, flooding, and damage to coastal infrastructure. These events not only affect homes and ports but also key economic activities such as tourism and artisanal fishing. Additionally, coastal ecosystems face challenges due to habitat destruction and the decline of essential marine resources. The oceans are also experiencing significant impacts. An increase in mean sea level and surface temperature is projected, threatening coastal communities and marine ecosystems. In parallel, changes in wave patterns and the intensification of storm surges could exacerbate the vulnerability of Chilean coasts, affecting economic activities and marine biodiversity. The impacts of climate change are already evident in the country. The study "Methodologies and assessment of losses and damages from extreme climate-related events for Chile" 16 evaluated losses and damages resulting from extreme meteorological and hydrometeorological events and their derivatives, considering extreme precipitation, floods, storm surges, mudslides, heat waves, forest fires, and drought, for the period between 2008 and 2023, reaching an estimated value of more than USD 1.7 billion, only considering events for which recorded information existed, and losses and damages in housing, health resulting from loss of human lives, and work days lost by affected persons. The same study conducted an exercise to evaluate losses in infrastructure, agriculture, livestock, tourism, and biodiversity in the mega-forest fire events of February 2023, reaching a value exceeding USD 1.23 billion. These values represent a minimum floor of losses and damages, as they are restricted by information availability. 15 [1]

2.1. Vulnerable Groups

In Chile, vulnerability conditions are present that are associated with the persistence of poverty and marginalization, especially in rural or peripheral areas and in conjunction with inequalities associated with gender and belonging to indigenous peoples, as well as conditions of economic and demographic dependence, particularly in the case of older persons and children. Additionally, there are important differences in adaptive capacity and resilience, due, on the one hand, to differential access to capital and support networks, to the lack of information and understanding among populations regarding risks and possible solutions, as well as important gaps and differences in institutional capacity and preparedness against climate risks. In terms of local risks, of the 346 municipalities in the country, 84% present one or more climate risks categorized as "high," with the most recurrent systems at risk being those associated with health and human settlements, biodiversity, agriculture, forests, and water resources. 17Regarding women, factors such as educational level and poverty negatively affect the capacity to adapt to climate change. Gender roles intensify impacts due to the unequal distribution of resources and power, leading to greater workload and concern during climate events. Women, as the main managers of the household and food in rural areas, face barriers to visibility and access to resources. In disaster risk management, their vulnerability increases due to lack of access to services, information, and training, despite their active role as community leaders. Pregnant women constitute a particularly vulnerable group whose exposure to climate risks requires differentiated attention. 18In the case of older persons, loneliness and lack of support networks limit their capacity to respond to climate events. Mobility challenges during evacuations are evident, especially when intersecting with disability situations. Although they are registered at health centers, coordination with local levels is required for their care during emergencies. Their territorial attachment may hinder evacuation or relocation processes, requiring a rights-based approach and specific training. Despite these barriers, older persons contribute resilience, experience, and community leadership in adaptation processes. 19Regarding children, adolescents, and youth (NNAJ), exposure to catastrophist discourses about climate change is affecting their mental health, generating anxiety, hopelessness, and demotivation. Capacities such as the implementation of ANNEX school risk management protocols, the need for access to climate information, and greater participation in adaptation instruments are identified. The role of schools in environmental education is highlighted, proposing educational approaches that foster hope, climate commitment, and interest in sustainable lifestyles and environment-related careers. 20In the case of persons with disabilities, the diversity of this group poses challenges for climate policies due to differences in age, territory, type, and degree of disability. Specific risks during heat waves are identified, such as damage in areas where prostheses are used or thermoregulation difficulties. Persons with disabilities also have limitations in mobility and access to services, which worsens during emergencies. It is recommended to develop targeted campaigns and deepen knowledge about their needs, including those of their caregivers, with territorial and sectoral emphasis. 21Migrants, on the other hand, face language barriers, lack of support networks, and unfamiliarity with the risk context, which limits their participation in prevention and response actions. Some settle in exposed areas due to lack of knowledge, while others migrate due to indirect impacts of climate change on their livelihoods. An increase in internal climate migration is observed, without state recognition, which prevents access to specific support. In turn, the phenomenon of "trapped populations" affects those who cannot migrate, especially women, older persons, and children. Adaptation measures must consider both the migrant population and host communities. 22Chile also has high cultural diversity, with 13% indigenous population. The vulnerability of these peoples to climate change is related, among other aspects, to the interdependence between nature and their culture. Impacts on traditional means of subsistence, access to food, water, and territories are identified, along with the loss of places of cultural relevance and restrictions on practices such as navigation. External factors such as pollution, overexploitation of resources, and tensions with national regulations aggravate their situation. However, indigenous peoples possess ancestral knowledge and practices—such as ecological agriculture, barter, traditional medicine, and circular economy—that constitute key contributions to adaptation, especially in food security, water security, and conservation. 23 Table 1 presents a synthesis of some vulnerability elements for various indigenous peoples of Chile, identified by the "Study on vulnerability and risk of indigenous peoples of Chile to climate change," 24 prepared for the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan.

2.2. Sectoral impacts on sectors prioritized by the LMCC

Climate change significantly impacts biodiversity in Chile, affecting terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems. In terrestrial ecosystems, Andean and Mediterranean ecosystems in the central-south of the country face high risk, with threats to native species and vulnerable forests such as those in the Los Ríos and Los Lagos regions. In marine ecosystems, ocean acidification and marine heat waves are degrading habitats and affecting fishing and aquaculture, while in freshwater ecosystems, climate change favors invasive species at the expense of native ones. Regarding water resources, the decrease in precipitation and the mega-drought have reduced water availability throughout the country, particularly in the north and central-south, affecting both urban and rural populations. Extreme weather events such as intense rainfall are causing floods, mudslides, and landslides that affect human settlements and road connectivity, particularly in the southern macrozone. In parallel, the reduction of groundwater reserves and glacier retreat, which could lose more than 35% of their surface area by 2060, are diminishing strategic water sources, exacerbating water scarcity. In rural areas, it has been necessary to 25resort to potable water supply through tanker trucks. In these areas, the crisis is aggravated given the needs for agricultural and livestock production. Low reuse of wastewater (only 6%) is also identified, which limits sustainable solutions. In the case of infrastructure, excessive rainfall causing floods, mudslides, and landslides affects highways, bridges, airports, and public buildings, while precipitation deficits deteriorate soils and compromise reservoirs and irrigation channels. Extreme temperatures and heat waves deform pavements and increase electrical resistance in transmission networks, while frost increases risks of road accidents and infrastructure wear. Coastal infrastructure, including ports and urban drainage systems, is particularly vulnerable to sea level rise, storm surges, and ocean acidification, which damage river defenses and coastal protection systems. Additionally, the characteristics of materials, design, and conservation of works influence their adaptation capacity, while the physical and social environment, such as soil quality and community organization, also determine their resilience. The health sector is exposed to increased infectious, respiratory, and mental illnesses due to heat waves, forest fires, and extreme events. Extreme weather conditions aggravate chronic diseases and affect vulnerable populations, while loss of air and water quality creates new health challenges. The mental health ANNEX of the population is increasingly affected by stress and anxiety resulting from extreme events such as heat waves, droughts, and disasters, which generate psychosocial impacts at individual and community levels, including psychological trauma and social conflicts. Additionally, climate change favors the emergence and spread of new diseases, such as those transmitted by vectors, including dengue, due to changes in temperature and humidity that expand the habitats of mosquitoes and other transmitters. In mining, water demand is critical, while heat waves and intense rainfall threaten the stability of mining infrastructure and worker safety. Sea level rise and the impact of more frequent and intense storm surges affect port logistics, while extreme events such as mudslides generate significant economic losses. Climate change increases the risk of instability in tailings deposits, where extreme events such as intense rainfall can cause seepage or collapses, generating water and soil contamination, as well as significant repair and mitigation costs. The energy sector faces impacts on generation, transmission, and distribution, with hydroelectric energy particularly affected by decreased water flows. Extreme events such as heat and cold waves increase demand and supply outages, while coastal infrastructure is vulnerable to sea level rise and storm surges. Rising temperatures also increase electrical resistance in transmission lines, generating greater energy losses and reducing system efficiency, while storm surges and sea level rise affect port terminals, hindering fuel unloading and conditioning their availability for thermoelectric plants and various means of transport. Climate change significantly affects the forestry and agricultural sector in Chile, with prolonged droughts reducing agricultural productivity, especially in rainfed crops. Agricultural pests and diseases have intensified due to changes in temperatures and humidity patterns, affecting both the quantity and quality of harvests. Soil degradation, particularly in the Coastal Range and the Andean foothills, has increased erosive processes, while water resource scarcity further limits productive activities. Recurrent forest fires not only destroy forests but also generate carbon emissions that counteract mitigation efforts. Despite possibilities of moving crops southward, the loss of agricultural employment and decreased rural income pose social challenges, disproportionately affecting rural women and indigenous communities.

In the case of the fisheries and aquaculture sector, climate change is profoundly transforming Chile's marine ecosystems, affecting the productivity and distribution of key species for fishing and aquaculture. The proliferation of harmful algal blooms (HABs) and increased water salinity are reducing the biomass of economically important species, such as salmon and mussels, while changes in temperature and ocean currents are altering fish migration patterns. Storm and surge conditions affect navigation safety, fishing operations, diving, and operations in coves and ports, increasing downtime and reducing productivity. Additionally, artisanal fishing faces a decrease in landings due to the loss of marine habitats and the emergence of parasites such as Caligus rogercresseyi, which affects salmon farming. Coastal communities dependent on these activities see their income reduced, exacerbating social and economic inequalities in the most vulnerable areas. Climate change is intensifying vulnerabilities in Chilean cities, which face increased frequency of extreme weather phenomena, such as storms and surges, dust and sand storms, tornadoes and waterspouts, coastal and river floods, mudslides, snowstorms, frost, droughts, heat waves, and proximity to forest fires. Urban heat islands also aggravate health problems in vulnerable populations and increase social inequalities. Torrential rains generate landslides, channel overflows, and sinkholes that damage critical and residential infrastructure, while water scarcity affects supply security in arid and semi-arid regions. Additionally, uncontrolled urban growth in low-elevation coastal areas exposes the population to flooding and erosion. Lack of access to resources such as air conditioning and housing with better thermal insulation limits the adaptation capacity of the most disadvantaged communities, exacerbating existing inequalities. The tourism sector in Chile is particularly exposed to the effects of climate change, given its close link with natural attractions and coastal and mountain infrastructures. Decreased snowfall and glacier retreat are affecting high mountain destinations and reducing ski seasons, while storm surges and sea level rise are eroding beaches and damaging coastal tourist infrastructure. Heat and cold waves, along with biodiversity loss, are reducing the viability of tourist activities in rural and nature areas, affecting the sustainable tourism offer. Additionally, water insecurity in key tourist destinations, such as the north and center of the country, represents a challenge for the 16 operation of tourist services, limiting access to potable water and raising operating costs. In the case of coastal zones, sea level rise, acidification, and the intensification of storm surges are generating severe impact, affecting both natural ecosystems and human settlements and infrastructure. Coastal erosion is accelerating, reducing the extent of beaches and affecting coastal wetlands essential for biodiversity. Some artisanal ports and coves face ANNEX an increase in downtime due to storm surges, affecting logistics and the local economy. Coastal settlements are exposed to flooding that compromises infrastructure and community safety, while in island areas, changes in precipitation and temperature patterns generate freshwater supply problems and affect key economic sectors such as tourism and fishing. The transport sector faces significant impacts due to the intensification of extreme weather events, such as floods, heat waves, and storm surges, which affect the operation and maintenance of road, rail, and port infrastructure. Torrential rains generate landslides and erosion on highways and bridges, while extreme temperatures cause deformations in pavements and rails, increasing repair costs. Port infrastructure and coastal transport systems are particularly vulnerable to sea level rise and coastal erosion, which interrupt operations and damage key facilities. Additionally, extreme heat increases energy consumption in cooling and air conditioning systems, while forest fires impact visibility and access to critical infrastructure. These conditions especially affect the most vulnerable communities, underscoring the need for planning and adaptation strategies to ensure the safety and continuity of transport services.

3. Priorities

In addition to the twelve sectors prioritized for adaptation, the LMCC identifies the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan (PNACC) as a component of the ECLP, 26which must contain guidelines for cross-cutting adaptation actions to be implemented in the country, establish objectives, targets, and indicators for vulnerability and adaptation at the national level, and the minimum works and actions for climate change adaptation to protect the population, their fundamental rights, and ecosystems in the medium and long term. These guidelines must safeguard the use of water for human subsistence consumption and sanitation, and for biodiversity conservation. In line with the above, water resource management and resilience to climate change is highlighted as a key priority for Chile in the LMCC. In this regard, the obligation to develop, update, and implement a Sectoral Climate Change Adaptation Plan for Water Resources is considered, which must establish instruments and incentives that promote resilience to the adverse effects of climate change on water resources, such as drought, flooding, and water quality loss, and must also ensure priority use of water for human consumption, subsistence and sanitation, ecosystem preservation, water availability, and aquifer sustainability. Additionally, a climate change 27management instrument at the subnational level is considered, consisting of Strategic Water Resources Plans in Watersheds, which must identify water gaps, project water balances, and propose measures to address the effects of climate change, in order to ensure water security.

On the other hand, the increasing frequency and intensity of climate-related extreme events has positioned disaster risk management as an adaptation priority for Chile. Phenomena such as heat waves, drought, extreme rainfall and flooding, unusual storm surges, and forest fires are generating significant impacts on the population, infrastructure, productive systems, and ecosystems. In this context, the need to move toward a preventive, territorial, and multisectoral approach is reinforced, strengthening the country's capacity for anticipation, preparation, and response. The implementation of instruments such as Regional Plans for Disaster Risk Management with a climate focus, along with the development of early warning systems and adaptive social protection mechanisms, are considered fundamental to reducing vulnerability and increasing resilience to these events. Losses and damages derived from climate change represent a critical dimension for climate action in Chile, affecting both people and ecosystems and material assets. These impacts include both extreme events and slow-onset events, with the latter being of great relevance for the country, particularly desertification, glacier retreat, ocean acidification, soil degradation, decreased agricultural productivity, and biodiversity loss. These phenomena generate long-term social, economic, and cultural consequences, particularly in rural territories and fragile ecosystems. Given this situation, it is considered a priority to strengthen monitoring, evaluation, and recording systems for losses and damages, and to incorporate measures that allow for the effective identification, reduction, and addressing of irreversible and residual impacts of climate change, with a climate justice and rights protection approach. In relation to the above, non-economic losses and damages constitute a growing priority in Chile's climate action, given their profound and, in many cases, irreversible impact on people, communities, and ecosystems. These include the loss of human lives, physical and mental health, well-being, cultural identity, traditional knowledge, natural heritage, and elements essential for social cohesion, such as community support networks, particularly in disaster contexts. Likewise, forced displacements and climate change-induced migrations are recognized as relevant losses, affecting both those who migrate and host communities. These impacts are not always quantifiable in economic terms, but are fundamental to understanding the magnitude of Chile's climate vulnerability. On the other hand, priorities established in the 2021 ECLP are recognized, which identifies 46 strategic objectives that include strengthening climate governance at different territorial scales, ANNEX promoting institutional capacities and citizen participation in decision-making on environmental and climate change-related matters. It highlights the importance of generating scientific and technological knowledge to support decisions, reducing risks through integrated policies, and considering local specificities when adapting sectoral policy instruments. The implementation of nature-based solutions, ecosystem protection and restoration, and sustainable resource use are fundamental pillars. Additionally, the ECLP promotes water security for human consumption, ecosystems, and productive activities, encouraging efficient water use. The ECLP also recognizes the need for responses adapted to regional realities, strengthening subnational instruments such as Regional Climate Change Action Plans (PARCC) and Municipal Climate Change Action Plans (PACCC), which allow local actors to address specific climate impacts and contribute solutions adjusted to the reality and needs of their communities. Regarding the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan, it guides sectoral and regional measures with an inclusive approach that prioritizes vulnerable groups. An intersectional approach is emphasized to reduce structural inequalities, including analyses that consider gender, ethnicity, age, disability, and socioeconomic status, among others. Inclusive participation and the design of measures with intersectional indicators stand out as tools for fair and effective adaptation. Nature-based solutions are integrated as a key strategy to increase 28the resilience of human systems and ecosystems, simultaneously promoting water security. The PNACC also establishes synergies between disaster risk management and climate change adaptation to strengthen resilience to adverse weather events.

Among the national priorities of the PNACC, the following stand out, among others:

  • 1. Strengthening early meteorological warning systems and optimizing meteorological infrastructure.

  • 2. Ocean and coastal monitoring and generation of climate change-related risk information.

  • 3. Advancing in the identification of losses and damages, including approaches to avoid, minimize, and address them. In particular, the creation of a climate change impact inventory and evaluation of inaction costs is proposed.

  • 4. Effective monitoring of adaptation and associated management instruments.

  • 5. Continuous improvements to the Climate Adaptation Platform (Climate Risk Atlas) to provide accessible information to all citizens. 29

  • 6. Integrate disaster risk approaches into climate change management instruments.

  • 7. Integration of climate resilience into sectoral and territorial public policies.

  • 8. Active participation of the private sector through public-private partnerships.

  • 9. Broad capacity building at all territorial levels, technologies and financing as fundamental means to implement effective adaptation measures.

4. Needs

Chile has made progress in climate change adaptation planning through the development of the twelve Sectoral Climate Change Adaptation Plans (PSA) established by the LMCC, in addition to the PNACC. These instruments have allowed for the systematic identification of a broad set of needs that must be addressed for effective implementation of their adaptation measures, according to the sectoral and territorial particularities of the country. Among the main needs, the development of institutional, technical, and human capacities stands out as cross-cutting. This includes the continuous training of professionals in climate change adaptation, strengthening territorial capacities to incorporate the climate dimension into territorial and sectoral planning, and improving information, monitoring, and evaluation systems for vulnerability and climate impacts. Likewise, the need to advance in climate awareness and education for citizens is identified, as well as strengthening intersectoral and interinstitutional coordination for effective adaptation governance.   • Implementation of the Early Warning Meteorological System:

  • Ocean observation and coastal monitoring:

Additional investments are required to improve the observation and measurement of oceanic variables, with the objective of having updated and permanent information on the impacts of climate change in the country's coastal and marine zones.

  • Strengthening of the Climate Adaptation Platform:

Additional financing to improve the digital platform, including the system, its interface and design, as well as the incorporation of new and improved information on climate threats, extreme events, vulnerability, exposure, climate risk and resilience, a comprehensive management, maintenance, and dissemination system for the Climate Risk Atlas (ARClim).

  • Strengthening of disaster risk management capacities:

Capacities need to be strengthened to address risks and disasters, especially those related to extreme climate events, through training, equipment, protocols, and preparedness and response strategies. As reported in this Second Adaptation Communication, Chile has made significant efforts to advance in climate change adaptation, reflected in the development of the national plan, sectoral and territorial plans, and in the approval of the Framework Law on Climate Change, which establishes a robust institutional framework for climate action.
