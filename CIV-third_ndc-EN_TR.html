Foreword

Our objective: to reduce our GHG emissions by 33,07 % by 2035

Our planet is going through a critical period in its history, marked by an unprecedented intensification of climate change. These upheavals are testing food security, the availability of natural resources, the stability of ecosystems and the well-being of populations. Côte d’Ivoire, like many countries, is already suffering the consequences: shrinking forest cover, coastal erosion, recurrent droughts, destructive floods and increased threats to public health and infrastructure.

Aware of these challenges, our Nation has chosen the path of responsibility, international solidarity and sustainable development. Since ratifying the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change in 1994, followed by accession to the Kyoto Protocol in 2007 and to the Paris Agreement in 2016, Côte d’Ivoire has constantly affirmed its determination to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions and to strengthen the resilience of its populations.

This third version of the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC 3.0) marks a decisive step along this trajectory. It reflects the vision of a country that intends to reconcile economic growth, social justice and environmental sustainability. With a target of reducing GHG emissions by 33,07 % by 2035, and the prospect of long-term carbon neutrality, Côte d’Ivoire reaffirms its firm commitment to building a green, competitive and inclusive economy.

The NDC 3.0 is based on an integrated and territorialized approach that links mitigation, adaptation and just transition. It takes local realities into account, promotes nature-based solutions, and places women, young people and local authorities at the heart of climate action. It relies on updated scientific data and on strengthened governance mechanisms that guarantee transparency, accountability and effectiveness.

The Government intends to pursue its efforts to mobilize financial resources, stimulate green investment, and accelerate the transfer of clean technologies. The full implementation of these commitments will require the support of technical and financial partners, the private sector, civil society and local communities. Together, we must turn the climate constraint into a historic opportunity for structural transformation and sustainable prosperity.

Through this NDC 3.0, Côte d’Ivoire renews its attachment to the principles of the Paris Agreement and its ambition to be a leading player in the ecological transition in Africa. We have a responsibility to bequeath to our children a resilient, green and prosperous Nation.

Mr. Robert MAMBE BEUGRE

Prime Minister, Head of Government, Minister of Sports and Living Environment

Executive Summary

RE 1 NATIONAL CONTEXT

Côte d’Ivoire, a West African country with an area of 322 463 km², has a geographical diversity ranging from coastal plains to the mountains of the West. This position in a transition zone between equatorial and tropical climates results in three major climatic zones (humid equatorial in the South, humid tropical in the Centre and dry tropical in the North), with a variability that strongly influences agriculture, biodiversity and vulnerability to climate extremes.

The population, estimated at 32,7 million inhabitants in 2025, is young (77 % are under 35 years of age) and growing rapidly (2,4-2,6 %/year). This dynamic offers economic potential, but also accentuates challenges related to education, employment, rapid urbanization and pressure on natural resources. Greater Abidjan is home to nearly one fifth of the population, accentuating contrasts with rural areas.

The country has a rich natural capital: forests, water, mineral, energy and fishery resources. However, massive deforestation (from 16 million hectares at the beginning of the 20th century to less than 3 million in 2021), overexploitation, pollution and climate change are weakening this heritage. Preservation and energy transition strategies are under way, notably through renewable energy and forest restoration.

On the socio-economic front, Côte d’Ivoire is one of the most dynamic economies in West Africa, with a diversified GDP: agriculture (20 %), industry (30 %) and services (nearly 50 %). The country is the world’s leading cocoa producer and a regional hub for telecommunications, banking and transport, supported by the port of Abidjan. However, challenges persist: poverty reduction (37,5 % in 2021), youth employment, social and regional inequalities, public health and education.

RE 2 : GENERAL INFORMATION ON THE NDC

Côte d’Ivoire prepares its greenhouse gas inventories (GHGI) on a biennial basis, taking 1990 as the reference year. Whereas the country was initially a net carbon sink thanks to its forests, it has become a net emitter, with 110 345,58 Gg CO2eq recorded in 2022, an increase of 1531% compared to 1990.

This evolution is mainly explained by deforestation and the decline in the absorption capacity of the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector, which is now the main source of emissions. At the same time, emissions from the energy sector have increased sharply (+766 %), those from industrial processes have grown (+5.349 %), while agriculture (+39 %) and waste (+225 %) remain significant contributors.

These trends highlight the need to strengthen actions to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, notably through the promotion of renewable energy, sustainable forest management, improved waste management and the adoption of more efficient agricultural practices.

The country is highly vulnerable to the effects of climate:

  • In agriculture, dependence on rainfall (95 % of crops) exposes yields, notably of cocoa, to significant declines by 2050.
  • Water resources experience droughts in the north and floods in the south, affecting millions of people.
  • Coastal areas (566 km of coastline) are threatened by sea level rise, with annual losses estimated at 460 billion FCFA.
  • Infrastructure already suffers recurrent floods and droughts that compromise hydroelectric power.
  • Public health is weakened by the resurgence of climate-sensitive diseases (malaria, waterborne diseases).
  • Socio-economic impacts could cost 3 to 4,5% of GDP per year by 2030 and up to 13 % in 2050, with more than one million people at risk of falling into poverty.

In the face of these challenges, Côte d’Ivoire has aligned its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) with its development priorities and international commitments (UNFCCC, Paris Agreement). This alignment is based on several key policies: the PND 2026–2030, the National Adaptation Plan (NAP), the REDD+ Strategy, the National Agricultural Investment Programme (PNIA), the Electricity Code, the National Policy on Equality, Equity and Gender (2024-2030), and the National Disaster Risk Reduction Strategy. The Ministry of Environment, Sustainable Development and Ecological Transition ensures governance and multisectoral coordination.

RE 3 MITIGATION TARGETS

The reference scenario (BAU), built from the base year 2012 and excluding all measures adopted after that date, projects a continuous increase in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in Côte d’Ivoire, reaching about 156 374 Gg CO2eq in 2035. This scenario highlights the growing contribution of the energy, industrial processes and agriculture sectors, while forestry and land use gradually lose their role as a carbon sink to become a net source of emissions.

To reverse this trend, the NDC 3.0 sets an ambitious target of reducing emissions by 33,07% by 2035 compared to the BAU scenario, by mobilizing a portfolio of differentiated sectoral measures:

Unconditional measures:

constitute a commitment to implementation, making it possible to lower emissions to 104 659 Gg CO2eq, i.e. a reduction of 33,07% compared to the BAU scenario;

Conditional measures:

dependent on increased international support (financing, technology transfer, capacity building), they would offer a more ambitious potential, limiting emissions to 40 199 Gg CO2eq, i.e. a reduction of 74,29%.

The trajectories illustrate that, while national efforts represent significant progress, they remain insufficient to place Côte d’Ivoire on a trajectory compatible with carbon neutrality. The full realization of the country’s mitigation potential therefore relies on a strengthened partnership with the international community.

Sectoral contributions are an essential lever for translating national climate commitments into concrete actions in each area of activity. They cover all key sectors of the economy:

Energy:

in the absence of measures, emissions from the sector could double by 2035 to reach about 45 000 Gg CO2eq. The main mitigation levers are energy efficiency, the transition to LEDs, the electrification of transport and the development of renewable energy (46,3% of the mix in 2035).

Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU):

HFCs and solvents are a major and growing source of emissions. Targeted measures would make it possible to reduce HFC consumption by 50 to 70 % by 2035, having a decisive impact on controlling emissions with a high global warming potential (GWP).

Agriculture:

agricultural emissions (CH4, N2O) could double by 2035 under a BAU scenario. Priority measures include sustainable livestock farming, climate-smart rice cultivation, rational fertilizer management and the ban on residue burning, making it possible to stabilize, or even reduce, emissions.

Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF):

a pivotal sector in the national climate trajectory, heavily affected by deforestation. Without measures, deforestation increases net emissions (about 44 000 Gg CO2eq in 2035). Conservation, reforestation and agroforestry programmes could restore up to 4,0 million hectares, restoring the sector’s role as a carbon sink.

Waste:

growing strongly, this sector could exceed 8 000 Gg CO2eq in 2035. Measures for sorting, recovery, promotion of the circular economy and anaerobic digestion make it possible to stabilize, or even sharply reduce, CH4 emissions.

RE 4 ADAPTATION OBJECTIVES

Côte d’Ivoire is highly exposed to climate impacts (droughts, floods, coastal erosion, heat waves), which weaken agriculture, coastal areas, health, infrastructure and cities, accentuating social and gender inequalities. Women, young people, the elderly and persons with disabilities are particularly vulnerable, due to limited access to and control over productive resources and adaptation technologies, as well as their absence from climate decision-making bodies.

Biodiversity is integrated as a cross-cutting pillar of the NDC 3.0, through the promotion of nature-based solutions, sustainable ecosystem management, the fight against invasive species and the promotion of local knowledge.

The main vulnerabilities of Côte d’Ivoire to climate change notably concern:

Agriculture:

high risks of droughts and declining yields of cereals (maize, rice, millet, sorghum) and cash crops (cocoa, coffee, cotton). Smallholders, dependent on rainfall, are the most exposed.

Coastal areas:

two thirds of the coastline affected by erosion; losses of infrastructure and threatened habitats (Grand-Lahou, Grand-Bassam, San-Pédro). By 2050, up to 27 km² of coastal land could disappear depending on the climate scenarios.

Land Use and forestry:

affected by hazards such as droughts, extreme rainfall and rising temperatures, leading to soil degradation and declining agricultural yields. Deforestation, monoculture and the exploitation of marginal lands aggravate this fragility, compromising food security and generating social tensions.

Health:

resurgence of climate-sensitive diseases (malaria, diarrhoeal diseases, ARI), aggravated by malnutrition and vulnerable health infrastructure. By 2050, health risk could cover the entire territory.

Infrastructure and buildings:

roads, dams, public buildings and coastal structures threatened by floods, heat waves and erosion;

high sensitivity linked to unsuitable materials, lack of maintenance and inadequate standards.

Resilient cities:

rapid and unplanned urbanization, inadequate sanitation (less than 20 % of master plans integrate climate risks), expansion of informal settlements and increased exposure to hazards.

Côte d’Ivoire’s adaptation vision is one of inclusive, equitable and resilient development aiming to:

  • Protect human lives and livelihoods,
  • Preserve ecosystems and productive resources,
  • Reduce risks in exposed areas,
  • Take into account the differentiated vulnerabilities of men/boys, women/girls and other social sub-categories,
  • Ensure the systemic resilience of the economy by 2050.

The sectoral objectives aim to strengthen resilience in key areas such as:

Agriculture:

Increase to 80% the proportion of vulnerable farmers using climate-smart practices and ensure that 100% of regulated fishing areas are covered by sustainable management plans;

Land Use and Forestry:

Bring 80 % of the national forest area under a sustainable management plan by 2035;

Water Resources:

Increase to 75% the proportion of priority watersheds covered by integrated management plans that take climate risks into account;

Health:

Cover 90% of the territory with epidemiological surveillance systems for climate-sensitive diseases and ensure that 60% of health facilities are built or rehabilitated according to climate resilience criteria.

Coastal areas:

Protect 70 % of risk areas through appropriate structures and cover 85 % of coastal areas with local sustainable blue economy strategies.

Infrastructure and buildings:

Ensure that 60% of new public infrastructure and buildings comply with climate resilience standards.

Resilient cities:

Provide at least 60% of local authorities with master plans integrating climate resilience.

Monitoring and evaluation ensures regular measurement of progress made, identification of gaps and adjustment of actions in order to guarantee the effectiveness and transparency of the implementation of climate commitments. Monitoring will be integrated into the national transparency system and structured around three dimensions:

  • Identification of vulnerabilities and needs (climatic, physical and socio-economic indicators),
  • Implementation of actions (sectoral indicators, focal points),
  • Effectiveness and national preparedness (Magnan framework: risk knowledge, planning, action, capacities, evidence of risk reduction).

Indicators disaggregated by sex and age, taking intersectionality into account, will make it possible to measure social inclusion and gender integration.

RE 5 : NEEDS FOR MEANS OF IMPLEMENTATION

The implementation of the NDC 3.0 covers the period 2025–2035 and is based on a coherent vision that links mitigation, adaptation and national sustainable development priorities. It relies on an institutional architecture led by the Ministry of Environment and coordinated with all sectoral ministries.

Success depends on a robust policy framework, integrating the NDC into sectoral regulatory frameworks, strengthening multisectoral coordination, and decentralizing climate action through Territorial Climate Plans (PCT).

Côte d’Ivoire is strengthening its transparency systems to ensure rigorous monitoring: updated GHG inventories, reliable sectoral indicators, and integration into the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) through the BTRs and CTF tables.

Achieving the NDC 3.0 requires massive financing covering:

  • structuring investments (low-carbon infrastructure, smart agriculture, resilient cities),
  • support programmes (R&D, innovation, technology transfer),
  • just transition measures.

The unconditional share constitutes Côte d’Ivoire’s commitment, while the conditional share will depend on increased international support (GCF, GEF, Adaptation Fund, carbon markets, PPP).

The deployment of priority technologies (renewables, energy efficiency, anaerobic digestion, agroforestry, resilient infrastructure) will be supported by the CTCN, the TNA and the TAP. Capacity building aims at: training institutions and local authorities, integrating climate into education and research, and including women, young people and vulnerable groups.

A multi-year roadmap will make it possible to prioritize needs according to impact, feasibility and co-benefits, and to guide technical and financial partners effectively.

Côte d’Ivoire reaffirms its commitment to the Paris Agreement and calls for:

  • Increased mobilization of financing,
  • Appropriate technology transfer,
  • South-South and triangular cooperation,
  • Facilitated access to climate funds and carbon markets.
  • A just transition and territorialization.

The NDC 3.0 adopts an inclusive and equitable approach based on four pillars:

  • Adaptation of the regulatory framework and respect for rights,
  • Strengthened institutional governance and social dialogue,
  • Skills development and professional retraining,
  • Innovative financing of green initiatives.

The territorialization of the NDC 3.0 is a major innovation: Territorial Climate Plans, local financing windows, environmental taxation, local transparency systems, and multi-level governance with permanent dialogue between States, local authorities, civil society and the private sector.

A major democratic innovation is the institutionalization of the voice of young people and children in climate processes, through municipal councils, digital platforms and dedicated funds. Their involvement reflects the recognition of intergenerational justice and strengthens the legitimacy of climate policies.

In conclusion, the NDC 3.0 mobilizes a total budget of 58 795 207 683 US dollars, i.e. 32 925 316 302 480 FCFA, strategically distributed among the different sectors in order to address national priorities for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and strengthening climate resilience.

Introduction

Côte d’Ivoire, a coastal country in West Africa, is among the nations most exposed to the effects of climate change on the African continent. This exceptional vulnerability is explained by the confluence of several geographical, economic and social factors that amplify climate risks.

The concrete manifestations of climate change on Ivorian territory are already dramatically visible and measurable. The country suffers an average annual loss of nearly 200 000 hectares of forest, a green haemorrhage that dangerously compromises the national ecological balance. At the same time, the degradation of its coasts, with 566 km of exposed coastline, directly threatens coastal communities and vital economic infrastructure. The recurrent floods that each year cause loss of human life and significant material damage illustrate the growing vulnerability of populations to extreme climate events.

This critical situation is largely explained by the country’s strong economic dependence on agriculture, a vital sector that represents a large share of the national economy and of jobs. Climate variability, which manifests itself through changes in rainfall patterns, rising temperatures and the increased frequency of extreme events, directly affects the food security and livelihoods of millions of Ivorians.

Economic projections underline the urgency of acting. According to World Bank analyses, if no urgent action is taken, the country could suffer a loss of 13 % of its real Gross Domestic Product by 2050, with 1,63 million additional people falling into poverty. These figures reflect a human reality: behind the statistics are families and entire communities whose future depends on our collective capacity to respond effectively to the climate challenge.

The impact of climate change aggravates the vulnerability of natural ecosystems, notably forests, coastal areas and water resources, compromising biodiversity as well as key sectors of the economy. This environmental degradation, combined with annual floods, the advance of arid zones and intense anthropogenic pressures, contributes to socio-economic and environmental deterioration, creating a vicious circle that must imperatively be broken.

In the face of these considerable challenges, Côte d’Ivoire has chosen engagement and international responsibility. This proactive approach has resulted in a gradual but determined accession to the main international instruments for combating climate change.

The main objective of this report is to present the third version of the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC 3.0) of the Republic of Côte d’Ivoire, prepared in accordance with the Paris Agreement, notably its Article 4, and with the guidelines adopted at the Conferences of the Parties (COP), including the decisions relating to the enhanced transparency framework and progressive climate ambition.

This NDC 3.0 reflects Côte d’Ivoire’s renewed commitment to contribute actively to the global effort to combat climate change, through an update of its greenhouse gas (GHG) emission mitigation targets and its adaptation priorities, while reaffirming the country’s will to align its climate policies with long-term carbon neutrality objectives, as defined in Article 2 of the Paris Agreement.

Côte d’Ivoire’s vision carried in this NDC 3.0 is to consolidate and raise its climate commitments, building on the achievements of the NDC 1.0 and NDC 2.0 prepared in 2015 and 2022 respectively, updated scientific data and strengthened technical capacities. The ambition is to build a sustainable, resilient and low-carbon development model.

More specifically, this NDC 3.0 aims to:

  • Update Côte d’Ivoire’s sectoral commitments to 2035 and beyond, on the basis of progress made, new available scientific data and strengthened national capacities;
  • Demonstrate the consistency of national contributions with international guidance, in particular decision 1/CP.21 on the progression of ambition with each new NDC;
  • Report on the country’s climate trajectory in terms of emission reductions, resilience building, and mobilization of means of implementation, including finance, technology transfer and capacity building;
  • Facilitate understanding, by technical and financial partners, of Côte d’Ivoire’s strategic priorities and needs for implementing this NDC, and foster bilateral, regional or multilateral international cooperation;
  • Ensure the systematic integration of gender issues into climate policies in order to take into account the differentiated vulnerabilities of men/boys and women/girls in the implementation of climate strategies, policies, projects and programmes at national, sectoral and local levels;
  • Ensure transparency, clarity and comparability with other Parties, in accordance with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities in line with the founding principles of the UNFCCC.

Through this report, Côte d’Ivoire intends not only to meet its communication obligations under the UN process, but also to use the NDC as a strategic tool for planning and financing resilient and low-carbon development, fully integrating the fight against climate change at the heart of its public policies and its national development framework.

1. NATIONAL CONTEXT

I.1. Geographical Situation

Côte d’Ivoire is a West African country located in the Northern hemisphere, between latitudes 4° and 11°30’ North, and longitudes 8° and 8°30’ West. It is bordered to the South by the Atlantic Ocean, to the West by Liberia and Guinea, to the North by Mali and Burkina Faso, and to the East by Ghana. Its total area is 322 463 km², making it one of the largest countries in the sub-region.

Ivorian territory presents a great diversity of landscapes. The South consists mainly of humid coastal plains, while the centre is dominated by plateaus. In the West, there is a mountainous zone with Mount Nimba, the country’s highest point, which reaches an altitude of 1 752 metres.

This geographical diversity is also reflected in the vegetation cover: dense tropical forests in the south, savannahs in the centre and north. This strategic geographical position makes Côte d’Ivoire a climatic transition zone between equatorial and tropical climates, strongly influencing the country’s rainfall patterns, biodiversity and agricultural activities.

I.2. Climate

Côte d’Ivoire presents a great climatic diversity due to its geographical position, which makes it a transition zone between the equatorial climate and the tropical climate. Three major climatic zones can be distinguished:

In the South, the humid equatorial climate, characterized by a well-marked bimodal regime consisting of four seasons: a long rainy season from April to July with abundant rainfall, which can reach 450 mm on average, a short rainy season from September to November when rainfall averages 200 mm, a long dry season from December to March with rare rainy episodes, and a short dry season from July to September when rainfall is low and below 50 mm. Annual rainfall in this zone can be very high, reaching up to 2.500 mm, with maximums recorded between May and July.

In the Centre, the humid tropical climate with a less marked bimodal regime, consisting of four seasons: a rainy season from mid-March to mid-July when about 150 mm of rain is recorded, a relatively rainy short dry season from mid-July to mid-September, with rainfall that can exceed 100 mm, a rainy season from mid-September to mid-October when rainfall is about 200 mm, and a long dry season from mid-October to mid-March with few rainy episodes. Average annual rainfall in this zone generally ranges between 1200 mm and 1600 mm.

In the North, the dry tropical climate is unimodal, characterized by two seasons: a rainy season extending from April to October, marked by a peak in July-August reaching about 250 mm of rain, and a dry season from November to March, marked by the harmattan, a dry wind from the Sahara. Annual rainfall in this zone can reach 1200 mm, but varies by locality.

This climate variability strongly influences agriculture, water resources, biodiversity and the country’s vulnerability to extreme climate events.

For several decades the country has experienced significant variations in rainfall patterns and a gradual rise in average temperatures, with visible impacts on crops, water resources and public health. These changes are particularly worrying for agricultural areas and densely populated urban centres.

I.3. Population

The Ivorian population is a central element of the country’s social, economic and environmental dynamics. Its rapid growth, young structure and unequal territorial distribution make it both an asset and a challenge for development. In 2025, estimates put its population at nearly 32,7 million inhabitants, reflecting an increase of more than 7,5 million people in just seven (7) years. With an annual population growth rate of between 2,4% and 2,6%, Côte d’Ivoire ranks among the countries with strong demographic dynamics in West Africa.

This population is characterized by a predominance of young people. About 77% of inhabitants are under 35 years of age, and the median age is around 23 years in 2025, which remains well below the world average. This demographic structure offers considerable potential in terms of labour force and economic dynamism. However, it also represents a major challenge for the State, which must meet growing needs in terms of education, employment and socio-economic integration. Managing this youth is becoming a strategic imperative to guarantee the future stability and prosperity of the country.

The geographical distribution of the Ivorian population reveals strong regional disparities. The South of the country, notably Greater Abidjan, is home to a large share of the population. In 2025, this agglomeration has more than six million inhabitants, i.e. nearly one fifth of the national population. Urbanization is accelerating rapidly: the share of the population living in urban areas rose from 51,6% in 2018 to about 60,5% in 2025. This urban concentration, although it reflects ongoing modernization, increases pressures on infrastructure, notably in terms of housing, transport and basic services. The North of the country, on the other hand, remains less densely populated and is still strongly rural.

Côte d’Ivoire is also distinguished by its demographic and cultural diversity. Nearly 24 % of its inhabitants are of foreign origin, reflecting its historical role as a pole of economic and migratory attraction in West Africa. This diversity is an asset in terms of vitality and regional integration, but it can also be a source of tension when it comes to sharing resources and economic opportunities equitably.

This rapid population growth brings several major challenges. Pressure on natural resources, notably forests, agricultural land and water, is increasing. Unplanned urbanization leads to an expansion of informal settlements and a proliferation of problems related to sanitation and flood risks. Needs for health infrastructure and social services are rising sharply, while youth employment remains a critical challenge in a context of strong labour force expansion.

However, this demographic dynamic also represents an opportunity. A young and expanding population can become a powerful engine of economic growth if it is properly educated, trained and integrated into the productive fabric. Urbanization, when accompanied by adequate planning, can promote access to modern services and strengthen productivity. Cultural diversity and social vitality are also assets in a context of regional and global openness.

I.4. Natural resources

Côte d’Ivoire has significant natural capital that has long been one of the pillars of its economic and social development. Its main assets include forests, water, mineral resources, hydrocarbons, energy and fishery resources. However, this heritage, although abundant, is today under strong pressure linked to population growth, rapid urbanization and the effects of climate change.

Forest resources, once considered one of the largest forest massifs in West Africa, are undergoing alarming degradation. In the early 1900s, Ivorian forests covered nearly 16 million hectares, but they represent only about 2,97 million hectares in 2021, i.e. less than 10% of the national territory according to the latest Forest and Wildlife Inventory (2020). This situation results mainly from agricultural expansion, illegal logging, urbanization and illegal gold panning. The gradual disappearance of forests weakens biodiversity, reduces carbon storage capacity and increases the country’s vulnerability to climate disruption. Aware of these issues, Côte d’Ivoire has adopted ambitious programmes, including the National Strategy for the Preservation, Rehabilitation and Extension of Forests (SPREF), which aims to restore 20% of forest cover by 2030.

In terms of water resources, the country benefits from a relatively abundant water potential, distributed between surface water, groundwater and an extensive lagoon network. Major rivers such as the Bandama, the Comoé, the Sassandra and the Cavally play a decisive role in agricultural irrigation, hydroelectric production and drinking water supply. Groundwater aquifers, heavily used in rural areas, are also a vital resource. In addition, the country has a lagoon network of more than 500 km², with the Ébrié lagoon as the main body of water. These water resources support fishing, transport and tourism. However, they are threatened by domestic and industrial pollution, as well as by climate change, which alters rainfall patterns and increases pressure on their availability.

Ivorian subsoil is rich in diversified mineral resources. Gold, whose production now exceeds 30 tonnes per year, is the main resource exploited and attracts growing investment. Côte d’Ivoire also has deposits of diamond, manganese, nickel, iron, copper and bauxite. The exploitation of these resources contributes significantly to Gross Domestic Product and public revenue, thus strengthening the strategic role of the mining sector. However, this activity brings environmental and social challenges, notably land degradation, pollution and tensions around artisanal mining sites.

Organic resources, consisting mainly of oil and natural gas, are another strategic pillar. Exploited mainly offshore since the 1980s, these hydrocarbons provide an essential share of the national energy supply. Crude oil contributes to exports, while natural gas feeds the thermal power plants that produce a large part of the national electricity. Companies such as the Ivorian Refining Company (SIR) and the National Petroleum Operations Company (PETROCI) play a major role in this sector. Nevertheless, dependence on fossil fuels raises the question of the need to accelerate the energy transition.

In terms of energy, Côte d’Ivoire benefits from a relatively diversified mix, dominated by natural gas and hydroelectricity. The Kossou, Taabo, Buyo and Soubré dams provide nearly one third of electricity production, supplemented by gas-fired thermal power plants. The country has also committed to developing renewable energy, notably solar and biomass, to meet strong growth in demand and honour its climate commitments. Thanks to this production, Côte d’Ivoire is establishing itself as a regional energy hub, exporting electricity to several countries in the West African sub-region.

Finally, fishery resources represent significant potential for the economy and food security. With a coastline of 566 kilometres and a rich network of lagoons and rivers, Côte d’Ivoire has areas suitable for marine and inland fishing. Catches consist mainly of tuna, sardinella and various lagoon species. This sector supports thousands of families, particularly through artisanal fishing. However, overexploitation, pollution of aquatic ecosystems and habitat degradation threaten the sustainability of this resource. To address this, the country is putting in place sustainable management measures and encouraging the development of aquaculture as an alternative.

A Party to the Convention on Biological Diversity since 1992 and having ratified it in 1994, Côte d’Ivoire is actively committed to preserving biodiversity and using its natural resources sustainably; through the implementation of its National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (SPANB-CI), the country aims to halt biodiversity loss, restore ecosystems and promote the equitable sharing of benefits arising from genetic resources. Côte d’Ivoire’s NDC 3.0 marks a major evolution through the systematic integration of biodiversity issues into all climate measures. This cross-cutting approach responds to the recommendations of the IPBES (Intergovernmental Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services) assessments on invasive alien species and the sustainable use of wild species, formulated during the multi-stakeholder consultation workshop of 17 April 2025. Invasive alien species, responsible for 60% of global extinctions, constitute a particular threat for West Africa, requiring specific consideration in national climate strategies. Institutional innovations include the integration of invasive species into early warning systems, the extension of forest planning to savannahs and the sustainable management of easement zones to limit soil and water pollution.

The NDC 3.0 mainstreams Nature-based Solutions (NbS) as a cross-cutting strategy, drawing on local knowledge and community initiatives to link adaptation, mitigation and biodiversity conservation.

Overall, Côte d’Ivoire benefits from rich and diverse natural capital, which is a major asset for its economic and social development. However, overexploitation, deforestation, pollution and the effects of climate change are weakening this heritage. The implementation of sustainable management and energy transition policies therefore appears essential to preserve these resources and ensure their balanced exploitation, in the service of inclusive and sustainable development.

I.5. Socio-economic Profile

The Ivorian economy is one of the most dynamic in West Africa and rests on a diversified structure built around agriculture, industry and services. The agricultural sector, which contributes about 20% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), remains a major pillar of the country’s development. Côte d’Ivoire is indeed the world’s leading cocoa producer, with production of around 2,2 million tonnes, and also holds an important place in the production of coffee, cashew nuts, rubber and palm oil. The agricultural sector, particularly cocoa, coffee and palm oil plantations, depends heavily on migrant labour. Indeed, agriculture in Côte d’Ivoire concentrates many migrant workers, particularly those coming from neighbouring countries, notably Burkina Faso, Mali and Guinea: 58% of migrant workers from these three countries are employed in agriculture, with the proportion of Burkinabè in this sector being as high as 70,4% (source: national labour migration strategy). However, this strategic sector remains highly exposed to climate hazards, soil degradation and price fluctuations on world markets.

The industrial sector, which accounts for nearly 30% of GDP, is dominated by the processing of agricultural products, but also includes mining and oil extraction. Gold, oil, natural gas, manganese and nickel are increasingly exploited resources, generating significant tax revenues and attracting foreign investment. Côte d’Ivoire is also working to diversify its industrial base by encouraging the establishment of new value chains and the local processing of raw materials.

Services, which account for nearly half of GDP, are developing rapidly. The dynamism of telecommunications, banking and transport positions the country as a regional hub. The Autonomous Port of Abidjan, one of the largest in West Africa, is a strategic lever for international trade and an anchor point for regional economic integration.

On the social front, economic growth is still accompanied by significant disparities. The poverty rate, which stood at 39,4 % in 2018, fell to 37,5% in 2021, but inequalities remain, particularly between urban and rural areas, as well as between the sexes. Access to employment remains a major concern, especially for young people, who make up the majority of the working population.

The Ivorian education system has made significant progress, with a primary school enrolment rate of nearly 97%. However, the adult literacy rate remains low, at around 47 %, and disparities between men and women persist.

The health sector, for its part, has seen progress in health coverage, but still faces persistent endemic diseases such as malaria and HIV/AIDS. Life expectancy, ranging between 59 and 61 years, remains below the global average.

Increasing urbanisation, estimated at more than 52% in 2021 and more than 60% in 2025, is profoundly transforming the national territory. Greater Abidjan concentrates a large share of modern infrastructure and economic activity, accentuating the contrasts with other localities in the country, where basic social services remain insufficiently developed.

Finally, tourism, transport and logistics services are expanding sectors, driven by the modernisation of road, port and airport infrastructure. These investments are part of the strategic vision of Côte d’Ivoire, which aims to consolidate its role as the economic engine of West Africa.

In 1990, Côte d’Ivoire was a net GHG sink, absorbing more than it emitted. This situation was mainly due to the important role played by forests, whose Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector absorbed more than 18 000 Gg CO2eq. But this dynamic gradually changed² over the decades. The sink capacity of the LULUCF sector has fallen sharply, while emissions in the energy, agriculture, waste and industrial processes sectors have steadily increased.

Between 1990 and 2022, net GHG emissions in Côte d’Ivoire increased by 1531%, rising from -7 710,62 to 110 345,58 Gg CO2eq. This major reversal of the national balance is explained both by the rise of emitting sectors and by the weakening of the carbon sink role of forests.

The energy sector recorded the fastest growth in GHG emissions, with an increase of +766 % between 1990 and 2022, reaching 23 010,02 Gg CO2eq. This trend is mainly driven by rapid urbanisation, the growth of road transport and industrialisation. Industrial processes and product use have soared spectacularly (+5 349 %), moving from a historically marginal sector to a major contributor with 18 070,73 Gg CO2eq in 2022. This development reflects the acceleration of the country’s industrialisation process. Agriculture, although its growth is more moderate (+39%), remains a pillar of national emissions, totalling 8 230,68 Gg CO2eq. The main sources are enteric fermentation and soil management. The waste sector, strongly influenced by urban and population growth, also shows a significant increase in emissions (+225 %), reaching 7 391,06 Gg CO2eq in 2022.

Finally, the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector shows a particularly worrying trend. Long considered an important carbon sink, it has now become the country’s main source of emissions, with 53 643,11 Gg CO2eq in 2022. This reversal is the direct result of deforestation, agricultural expansion and logging.

The inventory results clearly show the need to strengthen efforts in several key areas:

  • Reduce energy emissions by promoting renewable energy and energy efficiency while fostering the equitable participation of men, women and young people in green value chains;
  • Protect and restore forests, by promoting agroforestry and anti-deforestation policies with considerable involvement of women and young people;
  • Improve waste management in cities to reduce methane emissions by sustainably supporting recycling and composting businesses;
  • Optimise agricultural practices, particularly with regard to fertilisation and livestock management, by promoting technologies that reduce the drudgery of work for men, women and young people.

II.2. Vulnerability to climate change

Côte d’Ivoire is highly exposed to the effects of climate change, which are already reflected in a gradual rise in temperatures, more frequent droughts, extreme precipitation and sea-level rise. These dynamics profoundly affect productive sectors, natural resources, infrastructure and populations.

II.2.1 Agriculture

The agricultural sector, which contributes about 25 to 27% of GDP and generates more than 75% of exports, is the pillar of the Ivorian economy but also one of the most vulnerable. More than 95% of cultivated land depends on rainfall, while less than 0,2% is irrigated, making production highly sensitive to climate variability. Droughts, soil degradation and irregular rainfall reduce agricultural yields. Cocoa, the dominant export crop, could see its production fall by 17 to 23% by 2050 depending on the climate scenarios, jeopardising the incomes of millions of producers and the balance of trade.

II.2.2 Land use and Forestry

Climate change could have a significant influence on the ecology and distribution of tropical ecosystems, even though the scale, level and direction of these changes are uncertain (Shanahan, 2016). With rising temperatures and the increasing frequency and intensity of droughts, the likelihood that wetlands and river networks will turn into other ecosystems increases, with plant populations being replaced by others and animals losing their habitats. Rising temperatures and the increase in drought episodes could also affect the renewal of forest systems while increasing the risk of invasive species becoming established. In addition to these climatic factors, weak agricultural production and population growth could lead to an upsurge in deforestation, land degradation and forest fires, with all that this implies in terms of impacts on animal and plant biodiversity.

II.2.3 Water resources

Ivorian water resources are also affected by climate change. In the north, rainfall deficits are leading to increasingly frequent droughts, increasing pressure on groundwater and compromising access to drinking water for rural populations. Community infrastructure is under heavy pressure due to the influx of displaced persons and herds from neighbouring countries. The combined effects of climate change and insecurity have disrupted traditional transhumance routes. As a result, transhumant herders are extending their stays in Ivorian communities, increasing competition for fertile land and water points. According to a Transhumance Tracking Tool (TTT) survey conducted by IOM in April and May 2024 in the Bounkani region, 85 % of key informants consider climate change a determining factor of transhumance, particularly through its impact on access to water, and 55 % believe that the lack of water contributes directly to the increase in conflicts.

Furthermore, it is estimated that between 1981 and 2000, around 1,3 million people, or 5,4% of the population, were affected by droughts each year, a figure that could reach 2,4 million (7,9% of the population) with population growth. Conversely, in the south and particularly in Greater Abidjan, episodes of extreme rainfall cause recurrent floods that damage sanitation networks and directly threaten public health.

II.2.4 Coastal zones

The Ivorian coastline, 566 kilometres long and home to strategic cities such as Abidjan and Grand-Bassam, is seriously threatened by sea-level rise and coastal erosion. A rise of only 20 cm could generate economic losses of around 460 billion FCFA per year, or about 700 million euros. By 2050, the sea could rise by 30 cm to 1,2 metres in some areas, causing the submersion of port infrastructure, housing and agricultural land. This dynamic threatens not only economic infrastructure but also social stability, with risks of forced population displacement.

II.2.5 Infrastructure and buildings

Ivorian cities, particularly Abidjan, are bearing the full brunt of torrential rains. In June 2024, some neighbourhoods received 214 mm of rain in 24 hours, nearly a quarter of the rainfall expected over three months of the rainy season. These floods caused the deaths of 35 people in 2024, compared with 30 the previous year. They regularly damage roads, bridges and power networks, causing major economic disruption. Droughts, for their part, compromise hydroelectric production, reducing the country’s capacity to meet rapidly growing energy demand.

II.2.6 Health

Climate change increases the health vulnerability of populations. Rising temperatures favour the spread of malaria and other vector-borne diseases, while floods and water pollution increase the risks of waterborne diseases such as cholera and diarrhoea. Heatwaves exacerbate risks for fragile groups such as children, the elderly and the chronically ill. These health threats aggravate a situation already marked by limited health capacities and inequalities in access to care.

II.2.7 Gender and equity

Women, young people, persons with disabilities, children and the elderly are particularly vulnerable to climate change. Women, who are highly dependent on natural resources, notably within agricultural value chains, but hold less than 20 % of land and receive financing 97 % lower than that of men, see their resilience capacities severely limited. In the north, recurrent droughts reduce agricultural yields and aggravate food insecurity, heightening social risks such as early marriage, the domestic burden and exposure to waterborne or vector-borne diseases. Their weak integration into early warning systems and limited access to adaptation technologies compromise their capacity and the effectiveness of climate policies.

II.2.8 Socio-economic impacts

Climate vulnerabilities translate into considerable economic costs. Between 2020 and 2030, losses linked to climate effects are estimated at between 3% and 4,5% of GDP each year, and could reach 12 to 13% of GDP in 2050 if no adaptation measures are implemented. At the same time, climate change could push an additional one million people into extreme poverty by 2030. According to the World Bank, around 1,63 million Ivorians are at risk of falling into poverty because of declining agricultural yields, rising food prices and the destruction of livelihoods. These socio-economic pressures also amplify mobility dynamics, forcing some households to move in order to diversify their sources of income or access more viable living conditions.

II.3. International commitments and alignment with national policies

Côte d’Ivoire, as a Party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement, has framed its climate actions within a dynamic of convergence between international commitments and national development priorities. This approach aims to ensure the coherence, effectiveness and sustainability of interventions through the systematic integration of climate into public policies.

In accordance with the Paris Agreement, Côte d’Ivoire has developed a Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), which constitutes the foundation of its commitments to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and adapt to the adverse effects of climate change. These commitments are structured around a low-carbon and resilient strategy, aligned with the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) and the requirements of progressive implementation, as stipulated in Article 4 of the Agreement.

In this context, Côte d’Ivoire has also adopted a National Strategy for Contribution to Reforestation, led by the Coffee-Cocoa Council (CCC) and aligned with the National REDD+ Strategy and the “zero deforestation” cocoa Policy. This strategy aims to place trees at the heart of cocoa and coffee production systems, through high-performing and traceable agroforestry models, while mobilising environmental and social co-benefits, notably via carbon credits and green jobs.

This commitment to integration has resulted in the development and implementation of national policies, strategies and programmes, including:

  • The National Development Plan (PND) 2026–2030, which integrates environmental sustainability and climate as cross-cutting principles;
  • The National Adaptation Plan (NAP), which aims to integrate climate resilience into priority sectors, strengthen national and local capacities, mobilise financing, put in place appropriate policy and sectoral instruments, ensure awareness-raising and the inclusion of vulnerable populations;
  • The REDD+ Strategy and the Forest Investment Program (PIF), which support efforts to preserve forest cover and promote sustainable rural development;
  • The National Strategy for Contribution to Reforestation in the Coffee-Cocoa sector, which aims to restore forest ecosystems on a massive scale through coffee-cocoa agroforestry and to capitalise on the carbon credits generated by these actions;
  • The Strategy for the Preservation, Rehabilitation and Extension of Forests (SPREF);
  • The Forest Products Valorisation Strategy (SVPF);
  • The Electricity Code, which promotes access to clean and efficient energy;
  • The National Agricultural Investment Plan (PNIA), incorporating climate-smart agriculture objectives;
  • The National Disaster Risk Reduction Strategy (2019–2030);
  • The National Policy on Equality, Equity and Gender (2024-2030), which integrates issues of climate change and natural disaster risks;
  • The National Gender and Climate Change Strategy, which aims at the systematic integration of gender issues into climate projects, programmes and initiatives by 2030;
  • The National Policy for the Development of Livestock, Fisheries and Aquaculture (PONADEPA 2022-2026), taking into account the sustainable management of fishery resources and the modernisation of environmentally friendly livestock farming.

These instruments reflect Côte d’Ivoire’s determination to reconcile economic development, the reduction of inequalities and climate resilience. Furthermore, the establishment of the National Commission for Combating Climate Change (CNLCC) in 2024 and the National Gender and Climate Change Platform strengthen national climate governance and multisectoral coordination.

Thus, the updated NDC reflects not only Côte d’Ivoire’s international climate commitments, but also their concrete translation into development policies at the national, sectoral and territorial levels.

II.4 Strengthening of cross-cutting themes

This NDC marks a step forward towards territorialisation, which fundamentally transforms the Ivorian climate approach, moving from a top-down logic to territorial co-construction. It positions Côte d’Ivoire as an African pioneer of climate decentralisation, creating a replicable model in which each territory becomes an actor in its own resilience. It also strengthens the integration of cross-cutting themes, namely biodiversity, the circular economy, gender, equity, social inclusion and the just transition.

II.4.1 Biodiversity as a major pillar of the NDC 3.0

A Party to the Convention on Biological Diversity since 1992 and having ratified it in 1994, Côte d’Ivoire is actively committed to preserving biodiversity and using its natural resources sustainably; through the implementation of its National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (SPANB-CI), the country aims to halt biodiversity loss, restore ecosystems and promote the equitable sharing of benefits arising from genetic resources. Côte d’Ivoire’s NDC 3.0 marks a major evolution through the systematic integration of biodiversity issues into all climate measures. The objectives set out in Côte d’Ivoire’s NDC thus strengthen the contribution to the 30×30 initiative of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, by integrating actions to protect terrestrial and marine ecosystems, as well as inclusive and effective approaches to conservation.

II.4.2 Integration of the Circular Economy

Faced with growing pressure on natural resources and the increasingly visible impacts of pollution, Côte d’Ivoire sees its sustainable development held back by a linear economic model based on the “produce - consume - discard” triptych. In this context, the circular economy appears as a strategic and cross-cutting response, making it possible to transform production and consumption patterns while fostering green growth, the creation of sustainable jobs and economic resilience. The widespread adoption of circular economy-based solutions across all development sectors is thus encouraged as a key lever for strengthening Côte d’Ivoire’s climate resilience.

II.4.3 Just transition

Côte d’Ivoire’s NDC 3.0 incorporates the principle of a just transition, recognising that the transition to a green economy will have profound effects on all aspects of our modes of production and consumption. This approach aims to “maximise socio-economic gains while mitigating potential negative effects on workers, businesses and vulnerable communities”. This approach recognises that “the just transition is not only an imperative of social justice: it is also a condition for economic success by fostering social peace, national cohesion, productivity and innovation, while strengthening the resilience of the economic fabric in the face of climate and technological disruptions”. The mitigation and adaptation actions planned under the NDC 3.0 would generate significant economic and social co-benefits in terms of wealth creation and sharing, provided that just transition considerations are sufficiently integrated into the implementation of these measures.

II.4.4 Gender and social inclusion

The implementation of the NDC 3.0 is based on a gender-transformative approach, ensuring that climate policies, projects, programmes, measures and investments help reduce inequalities between women/girls and men/boys, and strengthen the resilience of the most vulnerable social subgroups, notably rural women, young people, children, persons with disabilities and people in the 3rd age. This principle of mainstreaming gender issues in the NDC 3.0 requires that climate investments and financing contribute significantly to addressing differentiated and gender-specific issues, taking into account age and intersectionality, arising from the impacts of climate disruption, through financing targeting economic empowerment and capacity-building actions and activities, and equitable access to technological and energy innovations, resources, and specific goods and services for women, young people and children. Thus, the systematic integration of gender, equity and social inclusion issues aims to increase the effectiveness and sustainability of the climate actions of the NDC 3.0.

3. MITIGATION OBJECTIVES

III.1. Business-as-usual GHG emissions scenario (BAU)

III.1.1 Methodological framework of the BAU projection scenario of the NDC 3.0

In the process of developing Côte d’Ivoire’s third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), it is essential to define a rigorous method for projecting greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions under a reference or “Business-As-Usual” (BAU) scenario. This scenario serves as a comparative baseline for assessing the potential effect of future mitigation policies and measures.

The year 2012 was chosen as the base year for these projections, in order to ensure consistency with existing sectoral data series. This choice is based on the availability of reliable and representative historical data, facilitating the analysis of future trends. The BAU scenario extends the trends observed up to 2012, without incorporating the policies and measures introduced after that date.

Thus, only policies and measures implemented before 2012 are considered in the BAU scenario. They are included because they have already influenced historical emission trends, forming an integral part of the reference context. Conversely, interventions after 31 December 2012 are deliberately excluded, in order to simulate an emissions trajectory that would have occurred in the absence of new policies.

To ensure compatibility with the official sectoral series, emissions data between 1990 and 2012 are rigorously aligned with the national inventories. This involves using the same figures and coverage scopes, which enhances the reliability and comparability of the projected results.

The projections cover the period 2013 to 2035, offering a long-term perspective on GHG emission dynamics. They are obtained by combining projected economic activity data and emission factors, which makes it possible to take into account changes in technologies and behaviours.

Finally, the calculations were carried out at a fine level of disaggregation, mainly at CRT level 4 (for example: 1.A.3.b for road transport). Where this level of precision was not possible, CRT level 3 was used as an alternative (for example: 1.A.2 for manufacturing industries). This granularity allows detailed analysis by sub-sector, which is essential for identifying priority mitigation levers.

In summary, the BAU scenario projection methodology is based on a common base year (2012), the inclusion of pre-existing policies, alignment with validated historical data and a disaggregated approach to sectors. It constitutes a robust foundation for assessing the impact of Côte d’Ivoire’s future climate policies.

III.1.2 Construction of the NDC 3.0 BAU scenario at the national level

The projection of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions under the reference or Business-As-Usual (BAU) scenario was developed for all emission categories covered by Côte d’Ivoire’s national inventory. For each category identified in the sectors according to the CRT (Common Reporting Tables) classification, a specific projection method was applied to the corresponding activity data.

The projected emissions were then calculated using the same emission factors and the same parameters as those used in the national inventory, namely constant factors. This constancy ensures methodological consistency between the historical inventory and the projections.

III.1.2.3 BAU scenario for the Agriculture sector

The Business-As-Usual (BAU) scenario for the agricultural sector was developed from the projection of the main historically observed activity data likely to have a significant impact on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. These input variables include:

  • Animal population (ruminants, non-ruminants, etc.);
  • Areas cultivated as rice paddies (emissions linked to soil methanisation);
  • Mineral fertiliser inputs, in particular urea (source of N2O);
  • Liming, which generates CO2 emissions;
  • Areas cultivated for certain specific crops such as rice, maize and sugar cane;
  • The area of burned savannahs, contributing to CH4 and N2O emissions.

The reference periods are as follows:

  • Historical: 1990–2012
  • Projection: 2013–2030

Three methodological projection approaches were used to estimate the future evolution of these parameters:

  • Linear regression over the historical period, when trends are stable and correlated;
  • Average annual growth rate (AAGR), calculated between two key dates, to extrapolate data when the series show a regular evolution;
  • Use of 2013–2022 data, in cases where historical trends are not very representative or too irregular to be extrapolated by simple models.

III.1.2.4- BAU scenario for the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector

The BAU scenario for the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector aims to project the evolution of net greenhouse gas emissions and removals up to 2035, assuming no new mitigation policies after 2012. This projection is based on the historical trends observed between 1990 and 2012, a period that serves as the reference for establishing future dynamics. The approach adopted thus makes it possible to estimate the potential emissions that would be generated in a context of continuity of past practices, without additional interventions.

The data are structured according to the main categories of the LULUCF sector, notably forest land, cropland, grassland, wetlands, settlements, other land, harvested wood products and miscellaneous categories. Each sub-category has historical and projected data series, expressed in tonnes of CO2 equivalent. This breakdown provides a detailed view of the dynamics by type of land cover, which is essential for targeting future interventions.

Three main approaches are used depending on the sub-categories:

  • Linear regressions: used when historical trends are clear and stable.
  • Average growth rate (AAGR): applied to extrapolate data on the basis of past growth.
  • Constant values: used in cases where either the series show no marked trend, or the data are insufficient to model reliable growth.

III.2.1.3.1- Energy mitigation scenario

The implementation of Energy sector measures is an essential lever for reducing GHG emissions in Côte d'Ivoire. The adoption of clean cooking solutions (LPG, improved cookstoves, biogas) significantly reduces pressure on biomass and lowers CO2 and CH4 emissions. The widespread use of LED lighting and improved energy efficiency in households, the tertiary sector and industry make it possible to stabilise the growth in energy demand and to reduce emissions associated with electricity generation and fossil fuel combustion. Finally, the control of fugitive methane emissions in the hydrocarbons sector provides an additional mitigation gain.

III.2.1.3.2- Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) mitigation scenario

IPPU sector measures mainly target the phase-down of HFCs and emissions from industrial processes and solvents. Their implementation allows a 50 to 70% reduction in HFC consumption by 2035, thereby contributing to a sharp reduction in high global warming potential emissions. These actions explain the growing gap between the BAU scenario and the scenarios with PAMs, particularly marked in the conditional scenario.

III.2.1.3.3- Agriculture mitigation scenario

Agriculture sector measures target the main sources of emissions: livestock, rice cultivation, fertiliser use and residue burning. Their implementation makes it possible to substantially reduce CH4 and N2O emissions, which account for the majority of agricultural emissions. These measures thus explain a large part of the gap observed between the BAU scenario, marked by continuous growth in agricultural emissions, and the scenarios with PAMs, where the adoption of sustainable practices allows a stabilisation (INC) or even a reduction (COND) of emissions by 2035.

III.2.1.3.4- Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) mitigation scenario

The LULUCF sector plays a decisive role as a carbon sink. Conservation, reforestation and agroforestry measures not only limit the loss of forest cover, but also increase CO2 sequestration. This sector thus largely explains the marked gap between the BAU scenario (where deforestation continues) and the scenarios with PAMs, in particular the conditional one, which incorporates a massive restoration potential (more than 2,5 million hectares). The LULUCF sector therefore appears as the pillar of long-term carbon neutrality.

III.2.1.3.5- Waste mitigation scenario

Waste sector measures target the reduction of methane (CH4) emissions, the main GHG in this sector, as well as N2O. By improving solid waste management, promoting the circular economy, recovering biowaste and treating wastewater, Côte d'Ivoire can sharply reduce the emissions projected in the BAU. These actions explain the progressive stabilisation of emissions in the unconditional scenario, and their notable reduction in the conditional scenario, mainly thanks to methanisation and recycling.

Climate projections suggest profound changes in the structure and distribution of tropical ecosystems. Rising temperatures, combined with intensifying droughts, could transform wetlands into drier ecosystems, with a progressive substitution of plant species and the loss of habitats for wildlife. Vulnerability to climate change corresponds to the degree of exposure and sensitivity of a system to climate risks. It results from the interaction of several factors that determine a sector's capacity to withstand climate impacts. In Côte d'Ivoire, the analysis covers in particular agriculture, land use and forestry, water resources, coastal zones, health, infrastructure and buildings and resilient cities, with gender, biodiversity, circular economy and blue economy issues taken into account in a cross-cutting manner.

Climate variations and environmental degradation also influence human mobility, which is considered not only as a consequence of climate pressures, but also as an adaptation strategy and a contribution to strengthening community resilience. A study conducted by the International Organization for Migration in 2024 in the Poro and Tchologo regions (Savanes district), and Indénié-Djuablin and Sud-Comoé (Comoé district) reveals that 80% of the populations surveyed observe major environmental changes, such as soil degradation, recurrent droughts and the drying up of watercourses. IV.1.1 Vulnerability of the “Agriculture” sector

Climate change, by altering temperatures and rainfall, increases the vulnerability of Ivorian agriculture. It leads to shorter growing periods, reduced biomass and degradation of arable land, increasing water stress on crops. Smallholders, dependent on rainfall, experience increased variability in weather conditions and have little irrigation due to poor equipment and limited investment. Under the RCP 6.0 scenario, the risk of drought on cultivable land could go from 0,2 to 7% in 2000 to 0,1 to 23% in 2080, with high probabilities of up to 54%. Yields of cereal crops such as maize, millet, sorghum and rice are thus expected to decline.

Ivorian agriculture, in particular export crops such as cocoa, coffee, cotton or tropical fruits, is highly exposed to climate risks. Yields could fall. Some crops could be abandoned or disappear, such as coffee, which could disappear by 2030 without rehabilitation of orchards. In addition to yields, the harvest and storage quality of certain crops key to the economy, such as cashew (40% yield loss in some areas, increase elsewhere - CIAT in UNDP, 2024), or to food security, such as cassava, will be affected. Temperatures are expected to rise by +1,8 °C to +4,0 °C in the 21st century, leading to more frequent and longer heatwaves, while rainfall has been decreasing since the 1970s, increasing the risk of drought. These developments make it urgent to implement adaptation and mitigation measures to protect agriculture and strengthen the value chains that underpin future value and job creation, particularly for women and young people.

In Côte d'Ivoire, the livestock sector is increasingly threatened by the effects of climate change, notably the increasing frequency and intensity of droughts, which has serious consequences for the national herd, food security and the livelihoods of livestock farmers.

During droughts, pastures deteriorate, making animal feed insufficient, which leads to a decline in productivity (milk, meat) and an increase in animal mortality. Access to water also becomes critical, with the gradual disappearance of natural water points. Livestock farmers must then travel long distances with their animals in search of suitable areas, increasing their vulnerability to stress, diseases and conflicts with farming communities. Fisheries and aquaculture are also exposed to increased risks: heat stress, disruption of species' biological cycles, and a resurgence of diseases. Furthermore, coastal zones, where 30 % of the population and 80 % of economic activities are concentrated, are already facing sea-level rise and coastal erosion, directly threatening infrastructure and livelihoods. At national level, climate change could push nearly one million additional people into extreme poverty, exacerbate water stress with more than 10% of the population exposed to water shortages, and increase water- and air-related diseases, particularly in densely populated areas.

IV.1.2 Vulnerability of the “Land Use and Forestry” sector

The land use sector in Côte d'Ivoire is one of the most vulnerable to climate change, as it lies at the interface between agricultural production, the preservation of natural resources and food security. Agricultural land is highly exposed to climate hazards such as prolonged droughts, which reduce water availability and compromise productivity, extreme rainfall, which accentuates erosion and degrades soil structure, and rising temperatures, which accelerate evapotranspiration and cause increased loss of soil moisture. These phenomena lead to continuous degradation of land quality, a reduction in arable areas and a general decline in agricultural yields. In the North, this vulnerability is reflected in progressive desertification and a chronic water deficit that weaken food crops and pastures. In the Centre, land is subject both to unpredictable rainfall variability and to intense demographic pressure, which accentuates the fragmentation of plots and pushes farmers to cultivate marginal, low-fertility areas. In the southern and forest zones, the rapid conversion of forests into agricultural land, in particular for cash crops such as cocoa, accentuates erosion, impoverishes soils and compromises their sustainability.

The causes of this vulnerability are multiple. On the climate side, the increased variability of rainfall and the rise in average temperatures projected by 2050 intensify the risks of drought and soil degradation. On the human side, current land-use patterns aggravate this fragility: massive deforestation, the conversion of forests into industrial or food crop areas, the practice of monoculture, the absence of crop rotation and exploitation without fallow rapidly exhaust soils. Agricultural expansion onto marginal land and overexploitation lead to progressive impoverishment which, combined with the absence of robust land tenure security mechanisms, limits sustainable investment and creates a spiral of degradation. Forests, which play an essential role in water regulation, maintaining fertility and as carbon sinks, are particularly affected by this dynamic, which indirectly increases the vulnerability of the entire land use sector.

The consequences of this situation are worrying. In agricultural terms, declining soil fertility and land erosion lead to a significant decrease in yields, compromising both food security and producers' incomes. In ecological terms, the loss of forests and vegetation cover reduces biodiversity, weakens ecosystem services and diminishes carbon sequestration capacity. In social terms, the growing scarcity of arable land and increasing land pressure intensify tensions between farmers, herders and other rural communities, fuelling increasingly frequent land conflicts. The combination of these impacts profoundly weakens the resilience of rural households and increases their dependence on external support in the event of a climate crisis.

IV.1.3 Vulnerability of the “Water resources” sector

Côte d'Ivoire has strategic water potential with a dense hydrographic network comprising four national river basins (Bandama, Agnéby, Mé, San-Pédro) and seven transboundary river basins (Black Volta, Bia, Comoé, Niger, Sassandra, Cavally and Nuon). Groundwater resources, contained in the aquifers of the Precambrian basement covering 97,5% of the territory and of the coastal sedimentary basin (2,5%), also constitute a vital reserve. However, these resources are under increasing pressure from climate change, characterised by a gradual decline in rainfall since the 1960-1970s and a general rise in average temperatures.

In the northern regions, recurrent rainfall deficits are generating increasingly severe and frequent droughts. This dynamic considerably increases the pressure on already heavily used groundwater tables and seriously compromises access to drinking water for rural populations, who are particularly vulnerable. Historical data reveal that between 1981 and 2000, about 1,3 million people (representing 5,4% of the national population) were affected annually by drought episodes. With sustained population growth and the intensification of climate hazards, this figure could rise to 2,4 million people, or 7,9% of the total population. The 2018 water crisis in Bouaké tragically illustrates this vulnerability: the city was left without running water for three consecutive weeks, following the drastic drop in the level of the Loka reservoir, which normally provides 70% of its supply.

Conversely, the southern regions, notably Greater Abidjan and the coastal areas, face a diametrically opposite but equally worrying problem. Episodes of extreme rainfall, increasingly intense and unpredictable, cause recurrent flooding with disastrous consequences. These events severely degrade already inadequate sanitation infrastructure, contaminate drinking water sources and create conditions conducive to the spread of waterborne diseases, directly threatening public health. Climate projections indicate that these extreme phenomena could intensify, with an increase in the frequency and intensity of torrential rainfall in the south, while the north would experience prolonged periods of drought.

Beyond the direct impacts of climate change, water resources are subject to multiple anthropogenic pressures that aggravate their vulnerability. Agricultural pollution through the leaching of pesticides and fertilisers, toxic industrial discharges (caustic soda, acids, mineral oils), mining contamination notably by mercury from illegal gold panning, and urban pollution compromise both the quantity and the quality of available resources. The demographic impact alone, even in the absence of worsening climatic conditions, is expected to lead to an alarming 55% drop in per capita water availability by 2080 compared to the year 2000. This progressive scarcity is already generating conflicts over the use of and access to water between farmers, herders and urban populations, which are particularly acute during low-water periods. The economic consequences of this water crisis are considerable: according to estimates, climate change could lead to economic losses of between 380 and 770 billion FCFA for the Ivorian economy by 2040, thereby compromising the country's development efforts

IV.1.4 Vulnerability of the “coastal zones” sector

About two thirds of the Ivorian coastline is today affected by coastal erosion, a phenomenon aggravated by human action and climate change. Anthropogenic causes include the destruction of mangroves, demographic pressure, urbanisation, major infrastructure works, sand extraction, industrial pollution and the overexploitation of fishery resources. These practices weaken coastal ecosystems, making coastal areas more vulnerable to climate hazards.

Climate change, notably sea level rise due to global warming, amplifies coastal erosion. This dynamic leads to a progressive retreat of the shoreline, accompanied by marine submersion in certain areas. The combination of natural and human factors makes the phenomenon even more worrying, especially in areas where human intervention has altered sediment dynamics (dams on rivers, uncontrolled extraction, coastal infrastructure, etc.).

Coastal erosion and flooding constitute a direct threat to riverside and shoreline populations. These natural hazards cause loss of life, destruction of infrastructure, landslides and major economic disruption. Certain localities such as Grand-Lahou are particularly threatened. For example, in the village of Lahou Kpanda, the migration of the river mouth reaches a speed of 170 metres per year, endangering homes, local infrastructure and even places of memory such as the cemetery, now submerged.

According to an IOM-CESEC study (2024) conducted in Sud-Comoé, owing to the advance of the sea, about six out of ten respondents report having lost or abandoned their land. In response to the loss of their habitat, half of the respondents (49%) indicate that the victims moved within their locality, 29% state that they changed residence while 18,4% stayed in place. This dynamic reflects the importance of migration as an adaptation strategy in the face of land loss and coastal erosion.

If the current dynamic continues, several coastal areas will be invaded by the sea in the coming decades. Territories such as the localities of Groguida and Likpiliassé, the Azagny park, the banks of the Bandama River and coastal lagoons are in danger of disappearing. This development could redraw the country's seafront, with SICOR island, the Braffedon peninsula and the Azagny park becoming the new coastlines.

IV.1.5 Vulnerability of the “Health” sector

Health in Côte d'Ivoire is increasingly affected by the consequences of climate change, notably floods, heatwaves, droughts and storms. These phenomena aggravate respiratory, waterborne (such as diarrhoea), vector-borne (malaria, dengue) and infectious (HIV/ AIDS, tuberculosis) diseases. Added to this are injuries and deaths linked to extreme events.

The effects of climate on water and food supply further expose populations to hunger and malnutrition, especially in rural areas of the north and west. In 2016, 21,6 % of children under 5 suffered from stunting, and 10,8 % of the population was food insecure. Climate change risks accentuating these fragilities by disrupting agricultural production and exacerbating nutritional vulnerabilities.

With rising temperatures and rainfall variations, the transmission periods and zones of diseases such as malaria could expand. Already, in 2015, the incidence of malaria was estimated at 349 cases per 1 000 people at risk. Episodes of meningitis, particularly in the north, could become more frequent, as could the resurgence of other vector-borne or waterborne diseases.

Heatwaves will become increasingly frequent. According to the RCP scenarios, notably RCP 6.0, the share of the population exposed to at least one heatwave per year could rise from 9 % in 2000 to 31 % in 2080. Heat-related mortality could increase significantly, up to 7 deaths per year per 100 000 inhabitants if no adaptation is put in place.

Climate change risks compromising health progress, including Universal Health Coverage (CMU). It amplifies the risks linked to infectious diseases, malnutrition, difficult access to care, and premature deaths due to climatic causes. Health infrastructure could be damaged by natural disasters, and health systems overloaded.

IV.1.6 Vulnerability of the “Infrastructure and Buildings” sector

The infrastructure and buildings sector in Côte d'Ivoire is bearing the full brunt of the impacts of climate change. Heavy rainfall, rising temperatures, coastal erosion and violent winds cause considerable damage to road, rail and water infrastructure and to buildings. The June 2018 floods in Abidjan illustrate this vulnerability, with 18 billion FCFA in estimated damage, 57% of which concerned infrastructure, and recovery needs of 205 billion FCFA, 52% of which were for infrastructure.

Urban areas, particularly Abidjan, face major challenges. Heavy rainfall causes recurrent flooding of roads and water infrastructure, accompanied by landslides. Projections indicate a growing exposure of major roads and urban land to flooding at least once a year. At the same time, rising temperatures and heatwaves rapidly degrade road surfaces, causing cracks and reducing the durability of roads and bridges. The exposure of GDP to heatwaves could rise from 7% in 2000 to 31% under the RCP2.6 scenario or 27% under the RCP6.0 scenario by 2080.

Energy infrastructure is also highly vulnerable. Rainfall variability seriously threatens hydroelectric production, a sector on which 40% of the national energy supply depends. This vulnerability is all the more critical as the country has invested massively in large-scale hydroelectric projects, notably the Soubré dam, which is the largest infrastructure of its kind with a capacity of 275 MW.

The sensitivity of this infrastructure is amplified by the use of low-resilience materials, a maintenance deficit, non-compliance with inadequate construction standards and a reduced number of transport routes, which increases the systemic vulnerability of the sector.

IV.1.7 Vulnerability of the “resilient cities” sector

Local authorities in Côte d'Ivoire, including communes, districts and regions, play a central role in land-use planning, urban planning and local development. However, most current master plans do not sufficiently take into account growing climate risks such as floods, landslides, prolonged droughts or coastal erosion. A 2020 reference point indicates that less than 20% of these plans integrated climate data or vulnerability analyses, exposing urban and rural areas to severe impacts: loss of property, displacement of populations, destruction of infrastructure and degradation of basic services.

Data from the survey conducted by the International Organization for Migration (IOM) in the Comoé and Savanes districts show that populations move mainly internally in a context of environmental degradation and climate change. This essentially takes the form of labour migration from areas affected by environmental degradation and climate change towards areas better endowed with land, agricultural, fishery, water and gold resources (often to carry out the same production activities) or towards urban centres (where a career change is often essential). This internal migration thus plays a major role in the urbanisation dynamics that Côte d'Ivoire is experiencing, which are essentially fuelled by the influx of populations from rural areas.

This vulnerability is amplified by rapid and unplanned urbanisation, which leads to the expansion of informal settlements and multiplies sanitation problems. Informal dwellings are often built in geographically unstable areas, notably riverbanks and coastal areas. Inadequate sanitation infrastructure, with only 30% of urban households having access to adequate facilities in Abidjan, contributes to frequent flooding. Coastal towns such as Grand-Lahou and Sassandra are particularly exposed to coastal erosion and sea level rise, with scenarios forecasting the submersion of port infrastructure and residential neighbourhoods, including the Félix Houphouët-Boigny airport in Abidjan by 2050.

In the face of the expected intensification of climate hazards, the integration of climate resilience principles into territorial planning documents is becoming essential. However, weak local capacities and the lack of methodological tools to integrate climate resilience into local planning aggravate this situation and require a substantial strengthening of the capacities of local authorities.

IV.1.8 Vulnerability of the “Gender and Equity” sector

Women, young people, persons with disabilities and the elderly are particularly exposed to the effects of climate change, notably women owing to their strong dependence on natural resources, while having less access to essential factors of production such as land and finance. They own less than 20% of land and receive far less financing than that granted to men (97 % less), which severely limits their resilience capacities. In the northern regions, this vulnerability is exacerbated by recurrent droughts which affect rain-fed agriculture, reduce agricultural yields and aggravate food insecurity. These climate pressures also heighten social risks: an increase in early marriage, a heavier burden of domestic tasks and greater exposure to water-related or vector-borne diseases. Furthermore, women are poorly integrated into early warning systems and have only limited access to adaptation technologies or climate-smart innovations. This marginalisation not only compromises their own adaptive capacity, but also limits the effectiveness of climate policies implemented at national level.

IV.2. Overall adaptation vision and objectives

Côte d'Ivoire's adaptation vision is part of a resilient and inclusive development trajectory, capable of reducing the vulnerability of populations, ecosystems and socio-economic sectors to the adverse effects of climate change. This vision aims to:

  • Protect human lives and livelihoods,
  • Preserve natural ecosystems and productive resources,
  • Reduce climate risks in the most exposed areas,
  • Take into account the differentiated vulnerabilities of men/boys, women/girls and other social sub-categories,
  • Ensure systemic resilience of the national economy by 2050.

The NDCs endorse the orientations of the NAP, which are set over the same time horizon (i.e. 2035) and which cover the same priority sectors for adaptation, with gender taken into account in a cross-cutting manner. IV.5 Adaptation monitoring and evaluation

Monitoring and evaluation of adaptation will be based on a common NDC 3.0 monitoring framework included in Côte d'Ivoire's monitoring and transparency system coordinated by the Ministry in charge of Environment, Sustainable Development and Ecological Transition (Section VI). Monitoring and evaluation of adaptation measures is based on an update of an in-depth study by the Interprofessional Technical Centre for Studies on Air Pollution (CITEPA) aimed at identifying and structuring key indicators proposed in Côte d'Ivoire's Fourth National Communication. Climate change indicators are measured, aggregated values obtained on the basis of quantitative or qualitative data. These indicators serve three specific objectives:

  • Identify vulnerability or climate risk and adaptation needs;
  • Monitor the implementation of adaptation;
  • Evaluate its effectiveness and the country's preparedness to cope with climate change

IV.5.1 Indicator for monitoring adaptation needs

To identify adaptation needs and design actions accordingly, the specific vulnerability of the system under consideration must be analysed and potential climate-related impacts must be assessed. Indicators can be defined as part of this process to characterise the factors relating to climate parameters affecting the system (changes in precipitation and temperatures, occurrence of extreme events), physical factors (soil characteristics) and socio-economic factors (household income level), as well as political aspects (conflict-prone areas). These indicators are highly context-specific and can be addressed at different scales depending on the questions considered, using combined quantitative and qualitative methods.

Vulnerability or climate risk can be quantified through composite indices. Climate risk, as defined in the IPCC AR6, corresponds to the interaction of three components: the climate hazard, the exposure of populations, and vulnerability (incorporating sensitivity and adaptive capacity). Reference values can be defined and regularly revised, but targets are not systematically set.

IV.5.2 Indicators for monitoring the implementation of adaptation actions

Under NDC 3.0, indicators for each monitoring sub-measure have been identified (section IV .4) in order to track progress in the implementation of adaptation measures. These indicators will be monitored by sectoral focal points.

IV.5.3 Monitoring the effectiveness of adaptation and the country's preparedness

The adaptation community still struggles to provide specific guidance for assessing the results of adaptation actions, given the characteristics of adaptation measures, which are multiple, context-specific and long-term. Current practices (M&E component of NAPs) define performance, results and impact against a results framework or a theory of change in order to track progress towards predefined objectives - generally defined either at sectoral level or at programme level. Under NDC 3.0, performance indicators have been defined for each of the sectoral measures. These indicators are disaggregated by sex in order to monitor and evaluate the effective integration of gender.

7. JUST TRANSITION: ENSURING SOCIAL EQUITY IN CLIMATE ACTION

The Paris Agreement underlines the importance of a just transition, with the creation of decent and quality jobs, in accordance with national development priorities. The sectoral measures of Côte d'Ivoire's NDC 3.0 will transform the economy and the labour market, with impacts on social inclusion and gender equality. They will generate positive effects, such as the creation of wealth and jobs in green and transitioning sectors (renewable energy, sustainable waste management, sustainable agriculture and forestry), while offering the opportunity to close decent work deficits. However, the transition may lead to job losses in sectors incompatible with environmental preservation, and some adaptation actions may have negative impacts on populations. Effective management of these effects and the integration of socio-economic considerations into policies and investments will make it possible to optimise the distribution of risks and benefits.

VII.1. Guiding principle for the just transition

According to the International Labour Organization (ILO), a just transition means “greening the economy in a way that is as fair and inclusive as possible to everyone concerned, creating decent work opportunities and reducing inequalities, leaving no one behind”. This concept, given concrete form in the 2023 resolution of the International Labour Conference, provides a guiding framework for a climate transition that takes into account the risks, opportunities and socio-economic impacts on all stakeholders, in particular the most vulnerable. The ILO Guiding Principles for a just transition underline the importance of:

  • social dialogue to reach consensus on sustainability goals,
  • respect for rights, including freedom of association and non-discrimination,
  • equality of chances and opportunities between the sexes in environmental policies,
  • policy coherence across the economic, social, environmental and educational fields,
  • social protection to support workers affected by the transition,
  • adapting policies to national and sectoral contexts,
  • international cooperation to support developing countries.

For successful implementation of the NDC 3.0, it is essential to assess the challenges and benefits related to the selected measures, and to anticipate their effects on the most vulnerable people in order to direct resources to those who need them most and to minimise social costs through fairer and more effective social support.

VII.2. Four-component strategic architecture

The just transition strategy, which will be developed as part of the NDC implementation process, could be structured around four fundamental pillars:

Adapting the regulatory framework to ensure respect for and promotion of rights (freedom of association, collective bargaining, non-discrimination, abolition of child labour and forced labour) and the application of international labour standards.

Strengthening institutional governance by integrating the bodies in charge of training, employment and social protection with the actors in charge of the sectoral programmes and strategies with which the NDCs are aligned, as well as the private sector, employers' organisations, workers' trade unions and local authorities.

Skills development through training workers in the technical skills needed for the low-carbon transition and adaptation, professional retraining in changing sectors, and capacity building of populations (including youth, women and migrant populations) in green entrepreneurship.

Promotion of innovative mechanisms for financing green initiatives and of partnerships between different types of capital providers.

VII.3. Priority cross-cutting measures

The NDC 3.0 incorporates specific measures to ensure the social equity of the climate transition:

Universal social protection through the extension of social security schemes (CMU, RSTI) to workers affected by the transition, including the self-employed, informal economy workers and the most vulnerable. This protection includes “appropriate insurance mechanisms (e.g. index insurance for agricultural producers, compensation for displaced communities in coastal or forest areas and management of planned resettlement).

Green and decent jobs through the launch of public labour-intensive (HIMO) green job creation programmes for vulnerable groups (including migrants) and support for sustainable value chains in agriculture, forestry, waste and urban infrastructure.

Occupational health and safety by ensuring that mitigation and adaptation policies involving changes in professional practices systematically include OSH components, and by updating national OSH legislation to take into account heat stress and biological risks linked to ecological changes.

Gender equality and Inclusion through the integration of a “gender approach in all components and sectoral measures of the transition (policies, financing, employment, access to land, training)” and ensuring equitable representation of women in decision-making mechanisms as well as equitable access to and control over resources and investments.

Box: Inclusion of human mobility

Environmental degradation and climate change (notably coastal erosion, floods and droughts) have an enormous impact on human mobility in Côte d'Ivoire. A study conducted by the International Organization for Migration in 2024 in the Poro and Tchologo regions (Savanes district), and Indénié-Djuablin and Sud-Comoé (Comoé district) reveals that 80% of the people surveyed observe major environmental changes, such as soil degradation, recurrent droughts and the drying up of watercourses.

These developments result in the impoverishment of agricultural land, food insecurity and increased poverty, but also in an increase in human mobility. In Sud-Comoé, coastal erosion is causing forced displacement and massive land losses. In the Savanes district, 60% of respondents indicate that increasing water scarcity is a factor driving mobility towards localities where access to this resource is easier.

Labour and internal migration, which is particularly widespread (nearly 70 % of respondents), appears as an adaptive response to diversify incomes and secure livelihoods. This mainly takes the form of labour migration from areas affected by environmental degradation and climate change towards areas better endowed with land, agricultural, fishery, water and gold resources (often to carry out the same production activities) or towards urban centres (where professional retraining is often essential).

According to a Transhumance Tracking Tool (TTT) survey conducted by IOM in April and May 2024 in the Bounkani region, 85% of key informants consider climate change to be a determining factor of transhumance, notably through its impact on access to water, and 55 % believe that lack of water directly contributes to increased conflicts. Community infrastructure is also under heavy pressure due to the influx of displaced persons and herds from neighbouring countries. The combined effects of climate change and insecurity have disrupted traditional transhumance routes. As a result, transhumant herders are extending their stays in Ivorian communities, which increases competition for fertile land and water points.

Women, who are more dependent on natural resources and less mobile, face increased vulnerability, underlining the need for a gender-sensitive approach in climate policies.

VII.4. Inclusive governance and social dialogue

Implementation relies on the establishment of “social dialogue mechanisms in all sectors impacted by the transition, including representatives of government, employers and workers (formal and informal), as well as dialogue with other stakeholders such as local communities and vulnerable groups”.

This approach recognises that “the just transition is not only an imperative of social justice: it is also a condition for economic success” by fostering “social peace, national cohesion, productivity and innovation, while strengthening the resilience of the economic fabric in the face of climate and technological disruptions”.

Box: The territorialisation of the NDC 3.0 - The strategic role of local authorities

Côte d'Ivoire's NDC 3.0 marks a paradigm shift through the systematic integration of the territorial dimension into national climate action”. Although many actions were taken into account in the NDC2.0, this version goes beyond the decentralisation of climate action.

The institutionalisation of Territorial Climate Plans (PCT) is the central innovation of the NDC 3.0. These integrated planning instruments link adaptation and mitigation at the local level, creating strategic coherence between national policies and territorial realities. This bottom-up approach effectively complements traditional national planning.

The decentralisation of climate finance through the creation of dedicated local windows is transforming the financial architecture of climate action. The integration of climate expenditure as a mandatory budget line for local authorities institutionalises this priority in local public management.

At the sectoral level, it can be emphasised that:

Agriculture benefits from an approach differentiated by zone + agro-ecological. The North will favour smart pastoral systems, while the forest regions will develop agroforestry. Making local meteorological data available to farmers strengthens the relevance of climate-smart practices.

Energy adopts a territorialised strategy with the priority development + of solar power in non-connected rural areas, particularly in the North, Centre and West. Solar mini power plants and autonomous public lighting meet the specific needs of these territories.

Waste management relies on local processing ecosystems, + building on existing community initiatives. Decentralised composting centres in agricultural regions create territorial synergies between urban waste management and rural fertiliser needs.

Infrastructure now incorporates local climate specificities. + Coastal areas are developing erosion protection structures, while the northern regions are strengthening road infrastructure against extreme rainfall variations.

Multi-level governance mechanisms

The creation of a national multi-level coordination framework harmonises the action of ministries, local authorities, and technical and financial partners. This architecture ensures coherence between national policies and local initiatives, avoiding redundancies and optimising the effectiveness of interventions.

Regional multi-stakeholder dialogue frameworks institutionalise territorial consultation, creating permanent spaces for exchange between elected officials, deconcentrated services, civil society and the private sector. This collaborative governance strengthens local ownership of climate measures.

Financing and territorial innovation

Local financing solutions, notably Village Savings and Loan Associations (AVEC), mobilise local savings for climate projects. Local environmental taxation applies the polluter-pays principle at the territorial level, creating resources dedicated to the ecological transition.

Local MRV (Measurement, Reporting, Verification) systems enable territorial monitoring of progress, feeding national reports while serving as steering tools for local authorities. This dual function strengthens local and national accountability.

This territorialisation fundamentally transforms the Ivorian climate approach, moving from a top-down logic to territorial co-construction. It positions Côte d'Ivoire as an African pioneer of climate decentralisation, creating a replicable model where each territory becomes an actor in its own resilience.

VII.4.1 Participation of Children and Youth in the NDC 3.0

Côte d'Ivoire's NDC 3.0 inaugurates an unprecedented intergenerational approach through the structured integration of the voices of children and young people in the development of national climate policies. This democratic innovation responds to the imperative of giving future generations, who are the first affected by climate impacts, an active role in defining solutions.

The Children's Declaration for their rights in the NDCs is based on articles 14, 52, 53 and 54 of the Ivorian Environment Code and the Convention on the Rights of the Child. This declaration calls for institutionalised participation in climate decision-making processes.

The children propose concrete democratic innovations: creation of Children's Municipal Councils, adapted public consultations, interactive digital platforms and the integration of civic participation modules into school curricula. These proposals transform the conception of environmental democracy.

Young people clearly identify the obstacles to their participation: lack of political will, limited access to decision-making spaces, financial constraints and complex procedures. This critical analysis reflects their political maturity and their understanding of institutional issues.

Their solutions reveal a systemic vision: creation of a national fund for youth agriculture, development of youth-government platforms, institutionalisation of their participation through dedicated mechanisms. These proposals fundamentally restructure the interface between generations in public policies.

8. DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

VIII.1. Preparation and organisation phase

The development of the NDCs began with the establishment of a clear institutional framework. This involves activating or strengthening the national coordination structure, designating sectoral focal points and planning an operational roadmap. This phase also includes mobilising financial resources through financial and technical partners, as well as providing the technical tools needed for modelling and analyses.

VIII.2. Data collection and harmonisation

Once the institutional framework was established, the second phase focused on the collection and processing of sectoral data. It consisted of gathering historical data (GHG, economic activity, adaptation indicators), drawing on the latest emissions inventories. These data are then validated and harmonised through multisectoral technical consultations, ensuring their consistency with existing reports such as the BTR, the NCs and the TNAs.

VIII.3. Participatory approach and consultations conducted

The development of Côte d'Ivoire's NDC 3.0 was based on a fully participatory approach, aimed at ensuring national ownership and the representativeness of sectoral priorities. This process mobilised a wide range of actors through a series of national and sectoral consultations organised between April and August 2025.

The first step consisted of a national multisectoral consultation bringing together all the sectors concerned in order to take stock of the implementation of the measures planned in the NDC 2.0 and to identify, in a concerted manner, the priority measures to be included in the NDC 3.0. This meeting made it possible to consolidate achievements, measure gaps and guide the formulation of new, more ambitious actions adapted to current challenges.

Targeted thematic consultations were then organised to deepen the analysis and refine the proposed sectoral measures:

Coffee-cocoa sector:

exchanges with actors in the value chain to integrate mitigation and adaptation measures related to sustainable production, agroforestry and the fight against deforestation.

Box: Actors in the coffee-cocoa value chain

The meetings with actors in the coffee-cocoa value chain highlighted a paradox that remains a major challenge: it is an economic pillar of Côte d'Ivoire, but one of the main drivers of deforestation. This situation requires a radical transformation towards agroforestry, although many actions are already under way.

The quantified targets include the conversion of 2,5 million hectares into agroforestry systems, the distribution of 100 million plants, and the reduction of forest losses by 550 000 hectares by 2035. This approach will make it possible to reconcile agricultural production and carbon sequestration through structured collaboration between public and private actors and producer communities.

Circular Economy Unit of the Prime Minister's Office:

workshop dedicated to the systematic consideration of circular economy principles in sectoral measures, in particular for the waste, agriculture and energy sectors.

Box: Integration of the Circular Economy

Faced with growing pressure on natural resources and the increasingly visible impacts of pollution, Côte d'Ivoire's sustainable development is being held back by a linear economic model based on the “produce - consume - discard” triptych. In this context, the circular economy appears as a strategic and cross-cutting response, making it possible to transform production and consumption patterns while fostering green growth, the creation of sustainable jobs and economic resilience.

The consultations led by the Circular Economy Unit of the Prime Minister's Office (CECP) aimed to strengthen the integration of circularity principles into the NDC 3.0, by aligning adaptation and mitigation measures with national sustainability priorities.

Following these consultations, it was notably recommended to ensure the systematic integration of circular economy issues into sectoral strategic frameworks, in order to strengthen the impact of climate policies and ensure better management of natural resources.

Particular attention was paid to promoting social equity, notably through boosting the secondary sector in cities by creating promising activities for young people and women. The widespread adoption of circular economy solutions across all development sectors is thus encouraged as a key lever to strengthen Côte d'Ivoire's climate resilience.

Local Authorities:

regional consultations aimed at gathering local priorities, strengthening consideration of territorial specificities and fostering ownership of the measures by field actors.

Box: Involvement of Local Authorities

The consultations of Local Authorities (CT) in July 2025, which brought together prefects, presidents of regional councils, mayors and civil society representatives, respond to a shared observation: climate change directly impacts territories, requiring localised responses adapted to the geographical, economic and social specificities of each region. They thus revealed the imperative of effective climate decentralisation, positioning territories as indispensable actors in the ecological transition.

These consultations led to the formulation of a series of strategic recommendations aimed at strengthening the integration of local issues into the implementation of the NDC 3.0.

Thus, it is notably recommended to ensure systematic ownership of climate measures at the local level, integrating the specificities of local authorities in order to maximise the effectiveness of actions and the sustainable management of natural resources. The recommendations concerned the revision and adaptation of many existing measures to better reflect territorial realities, notably through the enhancement of local resources, capacity building of decentralised actors, the deployment of appropriate technologies and the promotion of sustainable and resilient infrastructure. The need to refine the technical precision of the measures was underlined, particularly in view of the growing environmental risks in the territories.

Particular attention was paid to promoting social equity, notably by boosting local economies through the creation of promising activities for young people and women. The strengthened integration of territories into the implementation of the NDC 3.0 is thus encouraged as a fundamental lever to strengthen Côte d'Ivoire's climate resilience.

Private sector:

working sessions with companies, professional chambers and employers' organisations to integrate actions that mobilise private investment and stimulate green innovation.

Box: Private Sector Engagement

Côte d'Ivoire has a mature private sector, aware of climate issues and determined to play a driving role. The actors position themselves as strategic partners, bearers of innovative solutions.

Three major challenges were identified during the consultations: access to climate finance, which is the main concern; dysfunctions in the application of the 2020 decree on renewable energy; and the need for technical support for low-carbon mechanisms. The co-constructed solutions include specialised green credit lines, the strengthening of the Carbon Market Office and a one-stop information window on financing.

Youth:

consultation forum with youth organisations and networks to ensure that the concerns, initiatives and proposals put forward by this essential segment of the population, which is particularly engaged in innovative climate solutions, are taken into account.

Box: Involvement of young people in the implementation of the NDC 3.0

In a country where young people under 35 represent 77 % of the population, their involvement in the design, implementation and monitoring of climate policies is not only strategic but essential to achieving the targets set for 2035.

The Gnamblêlê Bootcamp organised in 2025 enabled, for the first time, 500 young people from all regions of the country to express their views directly on the measures proposed in the NDC 3.0 (adaptation and mitigation) and the NBSAP. The consultations highlighted their key recommendations, which concern strengthening education and training, facilitating access to financing and resources, institutionalising youth participation, and promoting innovation and green technologies.

The young people called on the Government, Technical and Financial Partners and the private sector to (i) systematically integrate young people into NDC planning, implementation and monitoring mechanisms, (ii) allocate dedicated resources to green entrepreneurship and innovation led by young people, (iii) recognise youth as a strategic lever for achieving the country's climate and biodiversity objectives.

Gender and social inclusion:

institutional and non-institutional actors engaged on the gender-climate nexus were consulted in line with Côte d'Ivoire's commitment to systematically take gender issues into account in its climate policy.

Box: National commitment on the gender-climate nexus

Côte d'Ivoire has committed to planning issues related to social gender inequalities as a priority and cross-cutting axis in its public development policies. At the crossroads of the current issues and challenges of planning its development, Côte d'Ivoire, like other countries, is resolutely committed to addressing climate issues in accordance with the Paris Agreement. The deployment of the instruments of this expression of its political will is reflected in the implementation of institutional reforms, but above all in the development of policy documents, notably the NDC 1.0, NDC 2.0, the NDC 3.0 and the National Adaptation Plan.

Aware of the cross-cutting nature of SDGs 5 and 13, MINEDDTE and MFFE signed a collaboration MoU to create an enabling institutional environment for the institutionalisation and systematic integration of the gender-climate nexus into national, sectoral and local planning by 2030. Thus, since 2020, national state actors (ministries and supervised structures, universities and research centres) and non-state actors (NGOs, Media, local authorities) whose technical capacities have been strengthened on the issue have been networked through the National Gender and Climate Change Platform. In addition, the development of the National Gender and Climate Change Strategy and the integration of climate issues into the National Policy on Equality, Equity and Gender are guidance tools for effectively taking into account the differentiated needs of men/boys and women/girls and of social subgroups at the strategic and operational levels.

Building on this enabling framework, actors engaged on the gender-climate nexus, with the support of partners, translate the political commitment through the production of knowledge, studies, consultations, advocacy, training and awareness-raising at the national, local and community levels in order to reduce social gender inequalities in the face of climate change impacts.

VIII.5. Methodological approach followed

The methodology adopted combined technical analysis, participatory consultation and institutional validation:

Evaluation of the NDC 2.0 measures on the basis of monitoring data, sectoral reports and implementation reviews.

National multisectoral consultation to identify the priority measures of the NDC 3.0.

Preliminary proposals of measures by the sectoral groups, supported by the PTFs.

Technical work by national experts to refine, harmonise and consolidate sectoral measures, integrating GHG inventory data, updated BAU scenarios and macroeconomic projections.

Consolidation and validation by the national climate governance mechanism before official adoption and submission to the UNFCCC.

This process ensured that the NDC 3.0 rests on solid technical foundations, reflects national priorities and integrates mitigation, adaptation, circular economy and just transition in a balanced manner.

9. ARRANGEMENTS FOR MONITORING, TRANSPARENCY AND IMPROVEMENT

IX.1. Comparison of NDC2.0 and NDC3.0

Côte d'Ivoire's NDC 3.0 builds on the NDC 2.0 (2022) while marking a substantial evolution in national climate ambitions. This comparative analysis identifies the main quantitative and qualitative changes between the two generations of national contributions, making it possible to measure the progression of Ivorian commitments under the Paris Agreement.

IX.2. Evolution of mitigation targets and architecture of measures

The NDC 3.0 significantly raises mitigation ambition, moving from a 30,41% reduction in GHG emissions by 2030 (NDC 2.0) to 33,04% by 2035. This progression of nearly 3 points is accompanied by a time horizon extended to 2035, offering a strengthened planning perspective, with a forward-looking vision up to 2050 for structuring measures.

The architecture of the measures is evolving towards a more targeted and strategic approach. The portfolio goes from 38 measures (27 unconditional and 11 conditional) in the NDC 2.0 to 26 measures in the NDC 3.0. This rationalisation reflects a maturing in the definition of sectoral priorities and increased precision of quantified targets, with each sector now having specific and measurable targets.

IX.3. Sectoral innovations and new areas of intervention

IX.3.1 IPPU sector and energy transition

The NDC 3.0 gives explicit priority to the Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) sector, which was absent from the specific priorities of the NDC 2.0. The target of reducing HFC consumption by 70% by 2035 puts Côte d'Ivoire ahead of the obligations of the Kigali Amendment.

In the energy sector, the ambition becomes more precise with the target of raising the share of renewable energy to 46,3% of the electricity mix by 2035. The NDC 3.0 introduces innovative measures reflecting a forward-looking vision: blending of 5% sustainable aviation fuels, 12% electric vehicles in imports, and development of rubber-tree biofuel to reach a 25% diesel blending rate in the bus fleet.

IX.3.2 Transformation of the forest sector

The Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector is undergoing a major conceptual evolution, moving from a mainly conservation-based approach in the NDC 2.0 to an ambitious programme of active regeneration. The NDC 3.0 provides for the restoration of 1,5 million hectares, including 250 000 hectares of production reforestation and 1,1 million hectares of agroforestry. In parallel, 2,5 million hectares of agricultural land will be transformed into sustainable agroforestry systems, integrating adaptation and mitigation.

IX.3.3 Extension and deepening of the adaptation component

“Infrastructure and buildings” and “Resilient cities” join the priority sectors in the NDC 3.0

The NDC 3.0 significantly extends the scope of adaptation by integrating two new priority sectors: “Infrastructure and buildings” and “Resilient cities”, bringing the number of sectors from 5 to 6. The blue economy is now integrated as a cross-cutting dimension with a target of 85% coverage of coastal areas by blue economy strategies.

This extension is accompanied by remarkable precision in the targets. Unlike the general orientations of the NDC 2.0, the NDC 3.0 establishes 11 adaptation objectives with quantified targets: 80% of vulnerable farmers using climate-smart practices (agriculture), 75% coverage of watersheds by integrated management plans (water resources), 90% territorial coverage by epidemiological surveillance systems (health), and 70% of at-risk coastal areas covered by appropriate protection structures.

IX.3.4 Cross-cutting innovations: just transition, gender and territorialisation

The NDC 3.0 introduces cross-cutting dimensions absent from the NDC 2.0. The just transition principle is a major innovation, structured around four pillars: adapting the regulatory framework, strengthening institutional governance, developing skills and promoting innovative financing mechanisms. This approach explicitly recognises the social impacts of the climate transition.

Gender integration is evolving from a cross-cutting mention to a systematised transformative approach. The NDC 3.0 institutionalises gender-responsive budgeting, systematic disaggregation of indicators by sex and age, and the commitment to reducing inequalities as an evaluation criterion for climate policies.

Territorialisation represents a revolution in the climate governance approach. The NDC 3.0 introduces Territorial Climate Plans (PCT) and innovative local financing mechanisms (local climate windows, mandatory budget lines for local authorities), transforming the traditional top-down approach into territorial co-construction.

IX.4. Financial architecture and innovative mechanisms

The financial assessment reveals the scale of the enhanced ambition: needs rise from USD 22 billion (NDC 2.0) to USD 62,06 billion (NDC 3.0), i.e. a 2,8-fold increase. This increase is divided into USD 34,85 billion for adaptation and USD 27,22 billion for mitigation, reflecting the extension of the scope of action and the deepening of sectoral measures.

The NDC 3.0 introduces innovative financial mechanisms: creation of the Carbon Market Office (BMC) to generate value from carbon credits, development of Village Savings and Loan Associations (AVEC) for local climate projects, and introduction of local environmental taxation applying the polluter-pays principle at the territorial level.

IX.5. Systemic integration of biodiversity and the circular economy

The NDC 3.0 marks a conceptual evolution by systematically integrating biodiversity as a cross-cutting pillar, with an estimated investment of USD 13,5 billion distributed among the agriculture, energy, land use, water resources, coastal areas and infrastructure sectors. This approach responds to the recommendations of IPBES and generalises Nature-based Solutions (NbS) as an intervention strategy.

The circular economy, absent from the NDC 2.0, becomes a generalised cross-cutting lever in the NDC 3.0. This approach aims to transform production and consumption patterns in order to decouple economic growth from pressure on natural resources, responding to the challenges of the traditional linear economic model.

IX.6. Objectives of the monitoring and transparency system

The monitoring and transparency system aims to ensure rigorous oversight of the implementation of the mitigation and adaptation measures included in the NDC. It also makes it possible to meet the requirements of the Paris Agreement, particularly with regard to the enhanced transparency framework. This system is designed to provide reliable data that will feed the biennial transparency reports (BTR), foster learning and support future revisions of the NDC.

IX.7. Institutional organisation of the system

The National Climate Transparency System (SNTC) is based on an institutional structure coordinated via the Official Climate Change Portal of Côte d'Ivoire (https://changementsclimatiques. gouv.ci/).

The Ministry of Environment, Sustainable Development and Ecological Transition, through the Directorate for Combating Climate Change and Ecological Transition (DLCCTE), ensures technical coordination. Sectoral ministries, local authorities, the private sector, civil society and specialised entities support the monitoring of data and indicators, notably those relating to mitigation and adaptation policies and to financial and technological support.

IX.8. Commitment to update the NDCs

Côte d'Ivoire commits to:

  • Regularly update its national GHG inventory data and sectoral projections, in order to faithfully reflect economic, demographic, technological and environmental developments;
  • Progressively strengthen the ambition of mitigation and adaptation targets, taking into account national capacities, new technological opportunities and the international support mobilised;
  • Align future updates with the five-year cycles provided for by the Paris Agreement, in accordance with the principle of progression and non-regression of commitments;
  • Integrate the lessons learned from the implementation of the NDC 3.0, sectoral reviews and independent evaluations to improve the effectiveness of the measures.

IX.9. Mechanism for tracking progress in achieving the NDCs

Côte d'Ivoire will strengthen its tracking mechanism in accordance with the Enhanced Transparency Framework of the Paris Agreement, in order to regularly produce and submit its Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) to the UNFCCC.

This mechanism will include:

  • The compilation and verification of national GHG inventory data, covering all gases covered by the Paris Agreement.
  • Monitoring of the implementation and results of the mitigation and adaptation measures provided for in the NDC 3.0.
  • Reporting of mobilised climate finance flows, including domestic and international resources, as well as monitoring of technology transfers and capacity-building activities.
  • Documentation of the socio-economic and environmental co-benefits linked to climate actions, particularly in terms of job creation, improved quality of life and ecosystem preservation.

The BTR preparation process will be participatory, involving sectoral ministries, local authorities, civil society, the private sector and technical and financial partners, in order to guarantee the quality, transparency and legitimacy of the information submitted.

CONCLUSION

These Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) reflect Côte d'Ivoire's renewed commitment to participate actively in the global effort to combat climate change, in accordance with the Paris Agreement. They are part of a low-carbon, resilient and inclusive development dynamic, taking into account national priorities, the country's socio-economic realities and sustainable development aspirations.

The NDC 3.0 highlights ambitious greenhouse gas emission mitigation targets, supported by concrete measures, both unconditional and conditional on enhanced international support. It also underlines national vulnerabilities to climate impacts and proposes a clear adaptation roadmap in the key sectors of agriculture, water resources, coastal areas, health, infrastructure and biodiversity.

Successful implementation of this NDC 3.0 depends on the effective mobilisation of all actors: public institutions, the private sector, civil society, technical and financial partners, and local communities. It also requires increased means in terms of climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building, as well as a robust transparency system enabling the monitoring, updating and continuous improvement of the commitments made.

Ultimately, these NDCs constitute a decisive step towards the transformation of the Ivorian development model, and reflect the country's determination to build a sustainable, equitable and resilient future for present and future generations.
