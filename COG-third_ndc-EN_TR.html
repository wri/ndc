TECHNICAL SUMMARY

The Republic of Congo, in accordance with its commitments under the Paris Agreement, presents its third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0). This strategic document reflects the national will to place the country's development on a low-carbon, climate-resilient and socially inclusive trajectory, consistent with the country's national priorities and with international efforts to limit Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions.

From NDC2.0 to NDC3.0

NDC3.0 continues Congo's commitments to combating climate change, expressed through NDC2.0 and the country's strategic documents.

In terms of process, its development was informed by the evaluation of NDC2.0, which made it possible to identify progress as well as gaps, challenges and opportunities for redefining the country's ambition. The lessons learned constituted an essential starting point for defining updated targets and feasible measures, while proposing a strengthened monitoring framework and a resource mobilisation mechanism. In addition, the NDC3.0 process proved inclusive, with stakeholders from sectoral ministries placed at the centre of defining the ambitions in order to ensure that these are realistic and aligned with sectoral priorities, while integrating current methodological requirements related to the enhancement of climate ambition.

In terms of scope, in addition to strengthening the adaptation component, NDC3.0 has been broadened to take into account cross-cutting aspects: Gender, Social inclusion, youth/children, Indigenous Peoples and Persons living with a disability (PWD), Just Transition, and responses to Loss and Damage. NDC3.0 also positions research, technological innovation and digital technology as strategic elements to support the implementation of climate actions.

Sectoral coverage

Congo's NDC3.0 covers mitigation as well as adaptation and cross-cutting aspects. The mitigation component includes: (i) the energy sector (production, transport, consumption, energy efficiency, renewable energy); (ii) the agriculture, forestry and land use sector (AFOLU); (iii) industrial processes and product use; (iv) the waste sector. The adaptation component, for its part, includes agriculture, water resources, infrastructure, human settlements & health, the coastal zone, biodiversity and ecosystem services, tourism, and disaster risk reduction. As mentioned in the previous paragraph, the NDC has been broadened to take into account cross-cutting sectors and other strategic considerations to support implementation in an inclusive approach.

Mitigation and adaptation objectives

In terms of mitigation, the Republic of Congo remains a net carbon sink and aims to strengthen this positive contribution to the global climate by 2035.

  • The unconditional scenario takes into account current low-carbon measures in the sectors and those for which the country does not expect external support for their implementation. Thanks to these efforts, the country progressively strengthens its performance to –21 717 ktCO₂eq in 2030, i.e. an improvement of 7,8 % compared with the BAU scenario for the same year. By 2035, removals reach – 21 932 ktCO₂eq, i.e. a 22 % improvement compared with BAU, reflecting the consolidation of the country's role as a carbon sink through programmes that are already operational.
  • By adding additional measures that require external support (combined Unconditional + Conditional scenario), net removals go from –22 033 ktCO₂eq in 2025 to –24 254 ktCO₂eq in 2030, i.e. an improvement of 20,4 % compared with BAU. By 2035, removals reach –26 569 ktCO₂eq, i.e. an increase of 47,8 % compared with BAU.

In terms of adaptation, NDC3.0 focuses on strengthening the resilience of populations and ecosystems. The priorities identified include in particular:

  • food security and the resilience of agricultural systems;
  • integrated water resources management;
  • the protection of coastal zones and their ecosystems;
  • related public health and climate risk management;
  • the development of resilient infrastructure
  • preparedness for responses to climate disasters

Social inclusion, gender and innovation

NDC3.0 attaches particular importance to equity and social inclusion. It promotes the active participation of women, young people, girls, children, indigenous peoples and persons living with a disability in the development, implementation and monitoring of climate actions.

Furthermore, the private sector, scientific research and technological innovation are considered essential levers to accelerate the climate transition and to foster the emergence of endogenous solutions adapted to the national context.

Financing needs and sources

The overall financial need for the implementation of NDC3.0 is estimated at approximately 8 616,33 million USD over the period 2025 – 2035. This need is divided between mitigation actions (5 814,32 million USD, i.e. 67,48 % of the total), adaptation actions (2 505,68 million USD, i.e. 29,08 %) and so-called support measures (296,33 million USD, i.e. 3,44 %). Within these financial needs, unconditional resources represent 20% (1 696,42 million USD) and conditional resources 80% (6 919,91 million USD).

Congo is relying on a mixed mobilisation of resources, combining public financing, private investment, support from international technical and financial partners, and innovative mechanisms (carbon finance, green bonds, market instruments). The country intends to take full advantage of the opportunities offered by the cooperation mechanisms provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, with a view to mobilising additional resources. The same applies to the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRPP), operationalised with the launch of the Barbados Implementation Modalities (BIM). Congo will also benefit from the regional frameworks, namely the Congo Basin Climate Commission (CCBC), the Central African Forests Commission (COMIFAC), the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Congo Basin Forest Partnership (PFBC), in the implementation of its NDC. The NCQG, with the tripling of adaptation finance by 2035, offers greater scope for mobilising resources.

Governance, monitoring and transparency

The governance framework of Congo's NDC3.0 is based on an integrated architecture aimed at ensuring coordination, transparency and the inclusive participation of all stakeholders. At the top, the Strategic Orientation Committee (COS), chaired by the Prime Minister, defines the policy directions, mobilises the whole of Government around the NDC and ensures the integration of climate priorities into national development.

Technical coordination is ensured by the Technical Orientation Committee (COT), chaired by the Minister in charge of climate. This intersectoral body guarantees consistency between mitigation actions, adaptation actions and the mobilisation of means of implementation. It relies on three Technical Units (CT), namely Mitigation, Adaptation and Means of Implementation, which are fed by sectoral Working Groups (GT) covering the various sectors taken into account in the NDC. This arrangement directly includes in the CTs and GTs, as well as in the COT, organisations representing women, young people, indigenous peoples, the private sector and local authorities, in order to ensure truly inclusive participation. The COT benefits from the support of the Monitoring-Evaluation & MRV Committee, responsible for operational monitoring, the production of indicators and the management of national transparency mechanisms.

Advisory bodies such as the National Assembly, the Senate and the CESE strengthen institutional dialogue, while ad hoc bodies, technical and financial partners, regional institutions and experts provide occasional support for expertise and resource mobilisation. The Climate Finance Unit (UFC), bringing together the key institutions for environment/climate, forestry (the carbon task force in particular), planning, finance, budget and cooperation, plays an essential role in the coordination and monitoring of climate finance, as well as in accelerating resource mobilisation.

The arrangement as a whole aims at effective, participatory and results-oriented climate governance. Its establishment will be rapidly formalised by a decree in the months following the submission of the NDC. Meetings of the technical bodies will be integrated into the usual functioning of the ministries, with a limited need for additional resources. Full operationalisation, in particular of the Climate Finance Unit, will take place progressively over the first two to three years, guaranteeing coherent and sustainable implementation of the NDC by 2035.

Final considerations

Through NDC 3.0, the Republic of Congo further strengthens its net carbon sink and sets as its course a low-carbon, resilient, inclusive and sustainable development trajectory. The success of its implementation will depend on a collective commitment at all levels (sectoral ministries, public and private institutions, local authorities, CSOs/NGOs, etc.).

Despite this stated will of the country not only to maintain but to strengthen its status as a carbon sink and to reduce GHG emissions from other sectors, resource mobilisation remains a major challenge for the effective implementation of the identified actions. The support of partners therefore proves decisive in enabling Congo to considerably strengthen its carbon sink, thus contributing significantly to the fight against climate change. Thus, an Implementation Plan and an investment and partnership plan will need to see the light of day rapidly following the submission of the NDC, in order to establish all the mechanisms necessary to accelerate the achievement of the objectives.

The Republic of Congo has based the planning of its NDC3.0 on a participatory and inclusive approach, while relying on the updating of legal texts and the strengthening of the existing institutional framework.

  • The Advisory bodies, notably the National Assembly, the Senate and the Economic, Social and Environmental Commission (CESE), are consulted on specific issues.

Assumptions and methodological approaches

The Republic of Congo has adopted the 2006 IPCC guidelines to account for its approach emissions and produce its projections in accordance with paragraph 31 of decision 1/CP.21 and with Article 4, paragraph 13 of the Paris Agreement.

The projections integrate demographic, economic and energy dynamics, as well as changes in land use and forest management. The GACMO and LEAP tools were used for the estimation of emission reductions and the analysis of scenarios in the Energy, waste and IPPU sectors. The EXACT tool, for its part, was used for the AFOLU sector (Agriculture, forestry and other land use). The gases targeted are carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O) and hydrofluorocarbons (HFC).

How the NDC is fair and ambitious

The NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo is both fair and ambitious. It reflects a principle is fair and of equity, taking into account common but differentiated responsibilities and limited national ambitious capacities, while paying particular attention to the most vulnerable populations. Thanks to the importance of its forests, the country remains a net carbon sink.

NDC3.0 broadens its scope to new priority adaptation sectors and expresses the will to integrate international financing mechanisms, demonstrating Congo's commitment to resilient and low-carbon development.

1 INTRODUCTION

The Republic of Congo, like the other Parties to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), has engaged in a process of updating its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), as underlined by Article 4.2 of the Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015 at the 21st Conference of the Parties (COP 21). This agreement invites Parties to submit to the Convention their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), which reflect the efforts they are prepared to make in the fight against climate change. These contributions must be revised every five (05) years, with an increasing level of ambition, taking into account the respective capabilities of each country and their common but differentiated responsibilities (UNFCCC, 2015).

The global stocktake (Global Stocktake – GST), provided for in Article 14 of the Paris Agreement, enables countries and signatory Parties to take stock of the collective progress made towards achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement on climate change. Decision 19/CMA.1 defines its modalities. This stocktake must be carried out every five (05) years. The first global stocktake took place in 2023, at COP 28 in Dubai. The conclusions of this first global stocktake underline the need for urgent changes to achieve the global goal, which is to keep warming below 1,5 °C. It highlights a persistent « emissions gap », despite the commitments implemented to date, and underlines that these efforts are not sufficient.

It is in this dynamic that the Republic of Congo has engaged in the process of developing its third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0), building on the results of the evaluation of its second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC2.0). This evaluation made it possible to highlight achievements, identify lessons learned and chart the directions needed to ensure effective implementation of the new NDC. The implementation of NDC2.0 was structured around the two major components of climate change, namely mitigation and adaptation. The sectors concerned by the mitigation component are: Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU), Energy, Industrial processes and product use (IPPU) and the waste sector. The sectors selected for adaptation are: Agriculture, Energy, Water resources, Human settlements and health, Coastal zone and Tourism (Republic of Congo, 2021). The evaluation of this NDC2.0 revealed that its implementation encountered several challenges, notably insufficient financing mobilised, insufficient consideration of gender and social inclusion , the absence of gender-sensitive indicators, insufficient gender-specific actions, the absence of specific actions for children, young people , Persons Living with a Disability (PWD), weak technical capacities, data gaps and institutional coordination that could be improved.

In accordance with the provisions of the Paris Agreement and as part of its commitment to the global collective effort, the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo has raised the ambition of its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) compared with the previous one. It is also in line with decisions 6/CMA.1 and 6/CMA.3 and reflects an increased will to make greater efforts and to guarantee more inclusive implementation. The country thus commits to strengthening its policy of reducing greenhouse gas emissions, notably by enhancing carbon sinks and adopting more environmentally friendly technologies and techniques. In the same vein, the Republic of Congo also intends to intensify its efforts to reduce vulnerability and strengthen the resilience of communities to the impacts of climate change. The concerns of groups vulnerable to climate shocks (women, children, adolescents, young people, older persons, Persons living with a disability (PWD), and Indigenous Peoples (IP)) are also taken into account through specific measures. NDC3.0 also aims to strengthen the integration of climate into development policies, to improve transparency and accountability, and to optimise access to international financing mechanisms.

The NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo is based on strengthened and better-structured ambitions, grounded in lessons drawn from previous contributions, improved sectoral data and the establishment of more robust monitoring mechanisms. It clearly illustrates the country's will to participate actively in the collective effort to combat climate change.

2 NATIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES

2.1 Socio-economic context

A country in Central Africa, the Republic of Congo covers an area of 342 000 km² and has a resident population of 6 142 180 inhabitants in 2023 according to the 5th General Population and Housing Census (RGPH-5) (INS, 2023). The low population density, which is 17,96 inhabitants per km², places the country among those with the lowest population density on the continent. The country is also characterised by a very young population, with an average age of 23 years and a proportion of young people under 20 representing 51,06% of the total population (INS, 2023). This constitutes both a significant demographic potential and a challenge in terms of access to basic social services, education and employment. Demographic dynamics are highly concentrated in the country's two main cities, Brazzaville (the political capital) and Pointe-Noire (the economic capital), which alone account for more than half of the Congolese population (58,2%).

In terms of human development, Congo is at a worrying level. According to the Human Capital Index (HCI) published by the World Bank1, the country is ranked 140th out of 174, with a score of 0,42. This score means that a child born today in Congo will only be able to reach 42% of their productive potential in adulthood, given the gaps observed in the health and education systems. Infant mortality remains high, with a rate of 32 deaths per 1 000 live births, although efforts are underway to improve the performance of the health system. In terms of education, school completion rates remain low. In 2018, according to UNESCO statistics, the primary completion rate was 66% for girls and 64% for boys2. These figures drop considerably at the lower secondary level, reaching only 14,1% among girls and 24,2% among boys. This school dropout is explained in particular by early pregnancies, forced marriages, lack of economic means and discriminatory social norms.

In the Republic of Congo, climate change represents a major challenge. According to the World Bank (2023), if no appropriate measures are taken, climate-related economic losses could reach 17 % of GDP by 2050. At the same time, public health costs linked to climate-sensitive diseases would rise from 92 million USD in 2010 to around 260 million USD in 2050, due to the resurgence of water-borne and vector-borne diseases. The floods of 2020-2021, which affected more than 330 000 people and destroyed 120 schools and 34 health facilities, illustrate the country's vulnerability to sudden climate disasters (World Bank, 2023).

However, beyond these general statistics, it is important to recognise that certain categories of the population are particularly exposed to the effects of climate change. Children, young people, women, older persons, Indigenous Peoples and PWD combine vulnerability factors that place them at the heart of risk dynamics (IPCC, 2022, UNICEF 2025).

Gender inequalities remain pronounced in the country. Congolese women, who make up more than half of the total population (50,34 %), continue to be under-represented in decision-making bodies, including those that directly concern their living conditions. Yet they are the first to be exposed to the effects of climate change, owing to their strong involvement in agricultural sectors, the collection of water and wood, as well as in unpaid domestic work. Their limited access to economic resources, combined with the precariousness of their living conditions, makes them particularly vulnerable to extreme climate events (UNDP, 2023). Young girls, particularly in rural areas, are often confronted with early marriage, sexual violence and various forms of exploitation, for lack of educational and economic alternatives. Housewives, for their part, are more exposed to domestic violence, exacerbated by economic and social tensions. Indigenous populations face specific vulnerabilities in particular, with an alarming food insecurity rate of nearly 46 %, higher than the national average of 41 %. In the same context, PWD are not spared, as their vulnerability is all the more pronounced than that of others. According to the World Bank (2023), their risk is increased in the event of evacuation during floods.

Access to basic services remains unevenly distributed across the territory. The rate of access to drinking water is estimated at around 74% at the national level, but it falls to 46 % in rural areas. Access to electricity follows a similar trend: 67 % of the urban population benefits from it compared with only 12,4 % in rural areas, illustrating a strong territorial divide. These disparities limit the adaptive capacities of the most vulnerable populations, particularly in remote areas.

Economically, the Republic of Congo remains heavily dependent on the exploitation of natural resources, in particular oil and timber, which account for nearly 70 % of gross domestic product (GDP). This concentration exposes the economy to fluctuations in world markets. Paradoxically, although nearly 40 % of the working population works in the agricultural sector, it contributes barely 5% of GDP, reflecting very low agricultural productivity and under-exploited potential. In 2023, the Congolese economy recorded a strengthened economic recovery, driven mainly by non-oil sectors, with a growth rate of 3,9 % in 2023 compared with 1,7 % in 2022, which is expected to accelerate to reach 4,3 % in 2024 and 4,4 % in 2025 (AfDB, 2024). Economic projections forecast sustained average growth over the period 2026–2027, accompanied by a downward trend in the poverty rate. However, these prospects remain dependent on economic diversification, macroeconomic stability and better social inclusion.

2.2 Emissions profile

The greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions profile of the Republic of Congo highlights the predominance of the energy sector and the “Forestry and Other Land Use” (FOLU) sector in the national contribution to total emissions. In 2020, national emissions were estimated at around 9 885 ktCO₂eq, and are expected to reach 10 671 ktCO₂eq in 2025, an increase of around 8 % over the period. This trend reflects the country’s economic and demographic growth, as well as the persistence of an energy model heavily dependent on fossil fuels.

The energy sector is the main source of emissions, with 5 406,6 ktCO₂eq in 2020 and 5 529,2 ktCO₂eq in 2025, i.e. nearly 52 % of total emissions in 2020. This trend is mainly explained by increased fuel consumption in road transport, electricity generation from thermal sources and the use of fossil fuels in extractive industries.

The FOLU sector, the second largest contributor, generated 3 218,8 ktCO₂eq in 2020 and 3 699,0 ktCO₂eq in 2025, an increase of nearly 15 %. This rise is linked to deforestation and land degradation caused by agricultural expansion, logging and mining, as well as the conversion of forest land to other uses.

The waste sector ranks third, with 838,0 ktCO₂eq in 2020 and 960,3 ktCO₂eq in 2025, reflecting the increase in urban waste volumes linked to the growth of urban centres and the inadequacy of integrated waste management systems.

The industrial processes and product use (IPPU) sector emitted 357,9 ktCO₂eq in 2020 and 397,4 ktCO₂eq in 2025, reflecting the evolution of construction materials production activities and manufacturing industries.

Finally, the agricultural sector contributes more modestly, with 63,4 ktCO₂eq in 2020 and 84,8 ktCO₂eq in 2025, i.e. less than 1 % of total emissions. The main sources are enteric fermentation, manure management and soil cultivation.

2.3 Climate profile and vulnerability

The Republic of Congo is a country rich in natural resources such as oil, minerals and forests. It also enjoys a climate favourable to agriculture, characterised by a tropical savanna climate over most of the territory, a tropical monsoon-type climate, and tropical rainforest in the northern part. The country’s climate regime is bimodal, both in terms of precipitation and temperatures. Between 2011 and 2020, the average maximum temperature increased by 0,6 °C, while the minimum temperature also rose.

With regard to rainfall, although average annual precipitation has remained broadly stable, an increase in its variability is observed, with more irregular and extreme rainfall episodes. In recent years, intra-seasonal rainfall patterns, particularly during the rainy seasons from September to November and from March to May, have experienced significant fluctuations, leading to a shortening or lengthening of these periods, and thus accentuating the unpredictability of rainfall.

Precipitation will, for its part, remain variable throughout the period. This indicates that the impacts will be greater on the country’s vital sectors. The effects of climate change in Congo hit women, young people, children, Indigenous Peoples, persons with disabilities and refugees hardest. A survey conducted in Brazzaville and Pointe-Noire shows that 98,5 % of farmers perceive climate change and that 85,35 % have adopted adaptation measures, notably the introduction of new varieties4.

Women, who are highly dependent on natural resources, bear an increased burden due to water scarcity and land degradation. Climate crises also exacerbate gender-based violence: in 2021, 1 338 cases were recorded, 90 % of which were sexual violence (UNICEF, 2021). Indigenous Peoples, dependent on forest ecosystems, see their livelihoods threatened by climate, deforestation and extractive projects, aggravated by their marginalisation and the lack of recognition of their rights. Persons with disabilities, often excluded from climate policies and warning systems, suffer a double vulnerability in the face of natural disasters.

2.4 Legal, policy and institutional framework

The Republic of Congo bases its climate action on a solid legal, policy and institutional framework, in order to ensure effective and coherent consideration of all dimensions of the fight against climate change. This architecture aims to strengthen the coordination of efforts and to foster the achievement of national mitigation and adaptation objectives.

2.4.1 Legal framework

The Republic of Congo has a solid and structured legal basis that reinforces its international commitments, notably the ratification of the Paris Agreement as well as other international conventions supporting the fight against climate change. This framework is based on several legislative and regulatory texts that progressively integrate the issue of climate change into provisions aimed at promoting sustainable and resilient development of the country.

The Congolese constitution of 2015 marks the basis for consideration of climate change by recognising in its article 41 that “every citizen has the right to a healthy, satisfactory and sustainable environment and the duty to defend it” and commits the State to be the guarantor of the protection and conservation of the living environment. This provision legally enshrines the need for public action in the fight against climate change.

The Environmental Protection Code (Law No. 003/91 of 23 April 1991) constitutes the main legal basis for environmental management. Although it predates current climate negotiations, it sets out the main principles of nature protection and provides for the integration of the environment into sectoral policies. It was revised in 2023 (Law No. 33-2023 of 17 November 2023 on the sustainable management of the environment in the Republic of Congo) in order to integrate the issue of climate change and the management of peatlands, highlighted in the Congo Basin. The new revised law includes provisions on hazardous waste, toxic chemical substances and liability in the event of environmental damage.

Law No. 74-2022 of 16 August 2022 on the orientation law on sustainable development in the Republic of Congo constitutes a major step forward in integrating sustainability principles at the heart of public policies. It defines the main national orientations for sustainable development and aims to reconcile economic growth, social justice and environmental preservation. Its objectives include the promotion of a green and resilient economy, the rational use of natural resources, the participation of local communities in sustainable management and the reduction of vulnerability to climate change. This law constitutes a strategic reference framework for aligning the country’s development actions with international commitments, notably the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Paris Agreement, thereby strengthening Congo’s capacity to respond to climate-related challenges.

Law No. 33-2020 on the Forest Code, definitively adopted on 8 July 2020, which replaces Law No. 16-2000, establishes the fundamental principles for the organisation and management of the national forest estate, as well as the rules for the exploitation and marketing of forest products. This new law clarifies certain concepts and introduces new ones, notably on certification, legality verification, consideration of riparian communities, deforestation and reforestation, the fight against climate change, carbon credits, etc.

It is important to emphasise that there are several other sectoral laws and regulations governing climate action, gender and social inclusion in the Congolese Republic:

  • the Law on wildlife and protected areas (Law No. 37-2008 of 28 November 2008), which sets the rules relating to the protection of animal species and their habitats;
  • Law No. 4-2010 on child protection;
  • Law No. 19-2022 (MOUEBARA Law) against violence against women;
  • the Law No. 33-2023 of 17 November 2023 on the sustainable management of the environment in the Republic of Congo;
  • Law No. 74-2022 of 16 August 2022 on the orientation law on sustainable development;
  • the Law on land-use planning (Law No. 43-2014 of 10 October 2014), which aims to establish a reference framework for organising the national space, taking into account contemporary challenges and promoting balanced territorial development;
  • Law No. 13-2003 of 10 April 2003 on the water code aims to ensure equitable access to water while preserving aquatic ecosystems;
  • Law No. 14-2003 of 10 April 2003 on the electricity code determines the rules for environmental protection
  • Law No. 8-2010 of 26 July 2010 aims to preserve and enhance the national heritage, whether cultural or natural;
  • the texts governing energy, mining, agriculture and urban planning, which are progressively evolving to integrate sustainability and resilience dimensions.

The national legal arsenal thus reflects an increasingly assertive will to integrate climate issues into Congolese legislation. However, challenges remain related to effective implementation, intersectoral coordination, the updating of old texts, and the strengthening of the technical and legal capacities of the actors concerned.

2.4.2 Policy framework

The political will of the Republic of Congo to include climate issues among its priority commitments and sectoral actions makes it possible to strengthen the framework it has put in place for the fight against climate change. This framework integrates climate concerns into strategic development documents and sectoral policies.

The National Development Plan (2022-2026), which is the main programmatic reference framework for government action towards development, places sustainable environmental management and the fight against climate change at the heart of its main objective, which is “A strong, diversified and resilient economy for inclusive growth and irreversible sustainable development”. This reflects the country’s will to include environmental protection and the improvement of the living environment among its concerns. In implementing the PND, Congo intends to make use of instruments such as the “Blue Fund for the Congo Basin” to both preserve the environment, particularly forests, and guarantee economic development, enabling populations to improve their standard of living in a well-protected environment. In this context, the actions planned for implementation concern:

  • the continuation of efforts undertaken since 2006 to lay the foundations of a green economy within the framework of the national REDD+ strategy;
  • the fulfilment of the commitments made by Congo in the field of the environment at the international level;
  • the promotion of agriculture and livestock farming compatible with environmental protection;
  • the use of natural fertilisers, a source of growth and jobs.

Among the commitments made at the sub-regional level is the Brazzaville Declaration on forest protection, a strong political act adopted by several Central African countries in favour of preserving the forest ecosystems of the Congo Basin, one of the largest and richest in the world. Initially adopted in 2010, this declaration was strengthened in October 2013 by the ministers in charge of the environment and forests of the Member States of the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Central African Forests Commission (COMIFAC). Its objective is to reduce emissions from deforestation and forest degradation within the framework of the REDD+ initiative, while promoting sustainable forest management, forest conservation and the enhancement of forest carbon stocks. 2.4.3 Institutional framework

The Republic of Congo has established a set of public bodies, technical structures and coordination mechanisms to ensure the implementation, monitoring and evaluation of national climate policies in the context of the fight against climate change. The Ministry of Environment, Sustainable Development and the Congo Basin (MEDDBC) is the entity at the heart of the implementation of the country’s climate policy. It is the national focal point for the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), responsible for overseeing the development and implementation of the NDCs. In addition to these prerogatives, the MEDDBC is responsible for ensuring the technical and administrative follow-up of the country’s climate commitments, mobilising international financial resources, and representing the Republic of Congo in climate negotiations, notably at the Conference of the Parties (COP) of the UNFCCC. This ministry works in synergy with other sectoral structures to integrate the climate dimension into national public policies. Among the technical structures, the National REDD+ Coordination (CN-REDD), under the joint supervision of the MEDDBC and the Ministry of Forest Economy, plays a strategic role in the implementation of initiatives to reduce emissions from deforestation and forest degradation. It notably manages the Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) tools, the REDD+ pilot projects and the integration of environmental and social standards into forestry programmes. The National Technical Climate Committee (CTNC), for its part, supports the work of quantifying greenhouse gas emissions, evaluating adaptation and mitigation policies, and producing technical reports.

To foster the integration of climate into the various development sectors, each ministry has NDC focal points responsible for ensuring the collection and validation of climate-related sectoral data, the alignment of sectoral policies with the objectives of the NDC, as well as coordination with the MEDDBC, the national focal point for climate issues. At the deconcentrated level, the Departmental Directorates of the Environment are tasked with relaying national climate policies at the local level, ensuring coordination with local authorities and local partners. Nevertheless, these structures remain limited in human and logistical resources, which constitutes a challenge for effective climate action at the territorial level. Civil society, local communities and indigenous populations also participate, increasingly, in climate governance through consultation platforms, community projects and capacity-building programmes. Their involvement is essential to guarantee the effectiveness, ownership and sustainability of climate actions.

In terms of gender and social inclusion, the presence of gender focal points, the Youth Council, as well as representatives of associations of women, older persons, Indigenous Peoples, children, adolescents and persons living with a disability, fosters the consideration of their specific needs. This inclusive approach aims to reduce their vulnerabilities to climate shocks and strengthen their resilience.

3 NDC3.0 DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

The process of developing the third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0) of the Republic of Congo followed a rigorous, inclusive and participatory approach, in accordance with the requirements of the Paris Agreement and in line with national sustainable development priorities. It built on the achievements and lessons learned from the implementation of NDC2.0, while integrating the most recent methodological requirements for strengthening climate ambition.

This new NDC integrates the lessons learned from the implementation of NDC2.0, while responding to new national priorities, notably in terms of sustainable development, gender equity, consideration of Indigenous Peoples, children, youth, adolescents, older persons, persons living with a disability as well as climate justice. In addition, the approach is based on co-development of the said NDC with stakeholders so as to facilitate ownership and implementation. This approach was materialised through series of stakeholder engagements via sectoral consultations and both technical and political workshops.

3.1 Stakeholder mapping and engagement process

3.1.1 Stakeholder mapping

Stakeholder mapping was a crucial step to ensure that the actors to be engaged were identified and the way to engage them was known. It was carried out in order to identify, classify and mobilise all key stakeholders, directly or indirectly concerned by the implementation of climate change, action planning and financing. This approach made it possible to ensure an inclusive and representative approach. The categories of stakeholders identified include:

  • Sectoral ministries and government institutions, responsible for the planning, regulation and monitoring of public policies;
  • Specialised technical agencies, responsible for producing and managing the data needed for inventories and projections;
  • Institutions in charge of local authorities, essential local actors for the implementation and adaptation of actions at the local level;
  • Civil society and community organisations, vehicles of citizen expression and relays to the populations;
  • Associations in charge of IP;
  • Women’s associations working in the various development sectors (energy, agriculture, forestry, environmental protection, education etc.)
  • Associations of PWD (blind persons, persons with sensory impairments, deaf-mute persons, persons with reduced mobility or multiple disabilities, mental);
  • Structures in charge of the social protection of children, adolescents and young people
  • The private sector, a driver of innovation, investment and technological solutions;
  • Technical and financial partners;
  • Research institutions and universities, guarantors of scientific expertise.

3.1.2 Engagement process

The engagement process was based on a series of multi-level consultations (national, sectoral and territorial), organised in the form of workshops, thematic working groups and bilateral dialogues. These consultations made it possible to integrate the contributions of all stakeholders, strengthen transparency and foster collective ownership of the NDC3.0.

An initial scoping workshop, held at the start of the process, made it possible to present the methodology, align expectations and agree on a shared work schedule. Technical working sessions in the form of sectoral workshops and bilateral discussions made it possible to gather contributions from both the sectoral ministries and the various stakeholders, ensuring the co-construction of the analyses and strategic orientations. Particular attention was paid to the integration of gender, youth, Indigenous Peoples and people in vulnerable situations. Sectoral consultations followed by a technical workshop for discussions on sectoral priorities made it possible not only to engage planning actors but also to consolidate the priority measures/projects to be included in the NDC3.0. A final validation workshop was subsequently organised with all stakeholders.

To ensure that the concerns of women, young people, children and persons with disabilities were effectively taken into account, specific consultations were conducted with these groups. These exchanges made it possible to analyse, in a participatory manner, their vulnerabilities to climate risks and to propose appropriate adaptation and mitigation measures. In addition, for Persons living with disabilities, Older persons and Indigenous Peoples, perceptions, obstacles, threats and opportunities were examined in order to formulate actions aimed at better taking into account these groups that are often marginalised by society.

3.2 Mapping of sectors

The sectors covered by the NDC3.0 were selected on the basis of their potential contribution to reducing greenhouse gas emissions and to adapting to climate impacts:

For mitigation

These are the Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) sector, the Energy sector including transport, the Waste sector and the Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) sector.

For mitigation

The sectors of Agriculture, forestry, Water Resources, Human settlements (taking into account floods and health), Disaster risk reduction, Coastal zone and Tourism were highlighted

For cross-cutting sectors

This involves taking into account gender and social inclusion, including Indigenous Peoples, young people, children, older persons, PWD and refugees. In addition, emphasis is placed on the private sector in view of its role not only in channelling financial flows but also in managing climate change through emission reduction and resilience-building initiatives

3.3 Methodology for estimating reductions and gases covered

3.3.1 Alignment with the IPCC guidelines

Methodologically, the development of the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo was based mainly on the IPCC guidelines (IPCC, 2006; Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories), taking care to ensure the comparability and transparency of the results. Where possible, the 2019 updates were incorporated, in particular to improve the accuracy of emission factors, better account for uncertainties and broaden sectoral coverage.

The approach adopted combines national statistical data (energy inventories, forest balances, agricultural statistics, industrial and waste management data) with default estimates from international databases where local information was insufficient. This method made it possible to ensure consistency between the national greenhouse gas inventories and the projections used for planning the NDC3.0.

The gases covered include the main greenhouse gases regulated by the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), namely:

  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂);
  • Methane (CH₄);
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O);
  • and, where possible, certain fluorinated gases (HFCs, PFCs, SF₆), although their monitoring remains limited due to the lack of detailed sectoral data;
  • Other gases such as ozone-depleting substances (ODS Ozone Depleting Substances), CFCs (Chlorofluorocarbons) and HCFCs (Hydrochlorofluorocarbons) were considered in connection with the Kigali protocol on HFCs.

The GACMO and LEAP tools were used for estimating emission reductions and analysing scenarios in the Energy, waste and IPPU sectors. The EXACT tool, for its part, was used for the AFOLU (Agriculture, forestry and other land use) sector.

3.3.2 Alignment with the decisions of the Convention

Furthermore, the process of developing the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo complied with the provisions of the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement. In particular, it was aligned with:

  • Decision 1/CP.21 (paragraphs 22 to 35), which sets out guidance on the submission, updating and progressive enhancement of nationally determined contributions (NDCs), in accordance with the principle of increasing ambition;
  • Decision 4/CMA.1, which establishes guidelines to ensure the clarity, transparency and understanding of NDCs, with emphasis on the comparability of information, the accuracy of data and the traceability of methodological assumptions.

Thus, the NDC3.0 was designed to ensure normative compliance with international rules, while strengthening the credibility and readability of the commitments made by the Republic of Congo. It also meets the principles of transparency, comparability, consistency, completeness and accuracy recommended by the IPCC and enshrined in the decisions of the UNFCCC.

3.3.3 Alignment with the national framework

The development of the NDC3.0 was carried out in line with the strategic orientations of the National Development Plan (PND), which makes environmental protection an essential pillar of the country's sustainable development. This harmonisation makes it possible to strengthen national ownership of the NDC3.0 and to optimise synergies with existing public policies. It was also linked with the sectoral policies in force to ensure complementarity and avoid overlaps in interventions.

Careful monitoring of the priorities contained in national planning documents (policies, strategies, plans and programmes) was carried out in order to ensure that the NDC3.0 is consistent with the country's development objectives, while maximising synergies between climate action and national socio-economic priorities.

3.4 Transition from the NDC2.0 to the NDC3.0

The development of the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo was informed by the analysis of the status of implementation of the NDC2.05. This analysis made it possible to identify the successes but also the gaps, challenges and opportunities for raising ambition. The lessons learned constituted an essential starting point for defining updated targets and realistic measures.

In terms of process: the development of the NDC3.0 proved to be inclusive, with actors from the sectoral ministries placed at the centre of defining the ambitions in order to ensure that these are achievable and aligned with sectoral priorities, while integrating the current methodological requirements related to enhancing climate ambition.

In terms of scope: In addition to strengthening the adaptation component, the NDC3.0 has been broadened to take into account cross-cutting aspects: Gender, Social inclusion, youth/children, Indigenous Peoples and Persons living with disabilities (PWD). The NDC3.0 also places research, technological innovation and digital technology as strategic elements to support the implementation of climate actions. The mobilisation and engagement of the private sector, regional/international carbon markets and Article 6, the funds for responding to loss and damage (FRLD), and the establishment of a country climate finance platform occupy an important place in the NDC3.0 in order to accelerate the mobilisation of finance for the implementation of the selected actions.

4 MITIGATION CONTRIBUTION

By initiating the 3rd cycle of NDCs, the Republic of Congo reaffirms its commitment to contribute to the global effort to combat climate change through the reduction of GHG emissions. With this in mind, the country has identified the priority sectors that have the greatest emission reduction potential and in which the country has the necessary instruments, including data. These include: energy, including transport; agriculture; forestry and other land use (FOLU); Industrial processes and product use (IPPU) as well as the waste sector.

4.1 National emission reduction ambition

The Republic of Congo has committed to significantly strengthening its climate ambition, in line with the objectives of the Paris Agreement and the principles of sustainable development. This commitment is based on a national trajectory aimed at reducing net greenhouse gas emissions while consolidating the country's status as a net carbon sink.

The national emission projections (Table 2) reflect the efforts envisaged through three scenarios:

  • a BAU (Business as Usual) scenario, describing the trend evolution without new measures;
  • an unconditional scenario, incorporating measures already undertaken with available national resources;
  • a combined conditional + unconditional scenario, reflecting the implementation of policy measures and additional measures planned subject to additional international support (finance, technology transfer, capacity building).

In the reference scenario (BAU), net removals follow a downward trend, from -22 852 ktCO₂eq in 2020 to -20 138,14 ktCO2eq in 2030 and -17 974 ktCO₂eq in 2035, i.e. a reduction in removal potential of nearly 21 % over the period. This evolution reflects the combined impact of deforestation, land degradation, population growth and extractive activities, which exert increasing pressure on natural resources. Without additional actions, Congo would see its national carbon sink weaken, compromising its role as a central actor in regional climate neutrality.

The unconditional scenario takes into account current low-carbon measures in the sectors. Thanks to these efforts, the country progressively strengthens its performance to –21 717 ktCO₂eq in 2030, i.e. an improvement of 7,8 % compared with the BAU scenario for the same year. By 2035, removals reach –21 932 ktCO₂eq, i.e. a 22 % improvement compared with BAU, reflecting the consolidation of the country's role as a carbon sink through programmes that are already operational.

By adding additional measures that require external support (combined Unconditional + Conditional scenario), net removals go from –22 033 ktCO₂eq in 2025 to –24 254 ktCO₂eq in 2030, i.e. an improvement of 20,4 % compared with BAU. By 2035, removals reach –26 569 ktCO₂eq, i.e. an increase of 47,8 % compared with BAU.

4.2 Sectoral contribution to mitigation

The implementation of sectoral mitigation measures constitutes the main lever for achieving the emission reduction commitments set by the Republic of Congo. These measures are structured around several strategic axes: strengthening nature-based approaches in the agriculture and forestry sectors, developing clean energy, the valorisation of gas into LNG through a zero open flaring approach; promoting the sustainability of transport systems, as well as the progressive decarbonisation of industries. All of these actions will contribute decisively to achieving the country's climate objectives. Details of the mitigation targets and the measures planned by sector are presented in the following sections.

4.2.1 Energy

The energy sector is at the heart of the national emission mitigation strategy of the Republic of Congo. Highly dependent on fossil fuels6, it represents a key sector for the transition towards a low-carbon energy model, through the development of renewable energies (hydroelectricity, solar, etc.), the modernisation of the electricity grid and the promotion of energy efficiency in households, services and transport.

In the BAU scenario, emissions from the energy sector increase steadily as a result of population growth and energy demand. They rise from 5 406,6 ktCO₂eq in 2020 to 6 520,9 ktCO₂eq in 2030 and then to 7 691,9 ktCO₂eq in 2035, i.e. an increase of 42 % over the period.

The unconditional scenario takes into account measures already being implemented with national resources or for which financing has been secured. These measures include7:

  • the rehabilitation and construction of small hydropower plants (MCHE) at Itsibou (1,5 MW), Lébama (28 MW), Foula (0,8 MW) and Dimani (23,2 MW);
  • the Maloukou solar photovoltaic plant (50 MW);
  • the first energy efficiency actions in households and services (LED lamps, improved stoves, high-performance refrigerators, grid modernisation) as provided for in the National Energy Pact (PNE)

Under this scenario, emissions fall from 6 520,9 ktCO₂eq in 2030 (BAU) to 5 904,0 ktCO₂eq, i.e. a reduction of 9,46 %. By 2035, emissions reach 5 810,8 ktCO₂eq, i.e. a decrease of 24,46 % compared with BAU (7 691,9 ktCO₂eq). These reductions result mainly from the deployment of local renewable capacities and the widespread adoption of energy-efficient equipment in urban households.

The combined scenario (Conditional and unconditional) reflects Congo's highest ambition for the energy sector. It combines efforts already undertaken at national level with additional measures dependent on international support in terms of finance, technology transfer and capacity building.

The conditional investments mainly concern the construction and modernisation of small and large hydroelectric and solar power plants, representing a total of 2057,5 MW of new capacity8, in particular:

  • The expansion of the Centrale Électrique du Congo (CEC) with the establishment of the full combined cycle (470 MW) through the establishment of a gas-fired electricity generation infrastructure, aimed at strengthening the energy autonomy of industrial facilities and the country's domestic consumption (around 70% of national electricity production);
  • the commissioning of the Centrale Electrique de Djeno (CED, 40 MW) through the valorisation of associated gas via its conversion into electricity, contributing to the reduction of flaring and the improvement of the energy efficiency of operations;
  • The extension of the Imboulou plant (60 MW) and the rehabilitation of the Liouesso (6,6 MW) and Djoué (18 MW) hydroelectric plants;
  • The development of the Mbama small power plant (8,4 MW);
  • The construction of the large hydroelectric plants of Mbandza Ndounga (630 MW), Linzolo (555 MW) and Nyanga (230 MW);
  • The installation of solar photovoltaic plants totalling 39,5 MW.

The investments also concern the implementation of strategic and ambitious measures in the hydrocarbons sector, in particular the valorisation of gas through a zero flaring approach:

  • Eni Congo's Gas-to-LNG Valorisation project, through the establishment offshore of an LNG hub composed of 2 floating gas liquefaction plants (a liquefaction unit named FLNG Tango, with a capacity of around 600.000 metric tonnes per year, and the liquefaction unit (launched in 2023), FLNG Nguya, with a capacity of 2.400.000 metric tonnes per year (December 2025). This project should make it possible to export up to 4,5 million m3 of LNG per year9.
  • WING WAH's Associated Gas Valorisation project, which provides for the production of dry gas, propane, butane as well as a significant volume of LNG intended for the national and international market. This gas valorisation plant will process 5 million cubic metres of associated gas per day, thus producing more than 219 000 tonnes of propane, 151 000 tonnes of butane, 88 000 tonnes and 250 000 tonnes of LNG, as well as a significant quantity of dry gas10.

In addition, there are measures in the transport, residential/domestic, services and industry sectors, in particular:

  • the promotion of electric mobility, with the progressive introduction of 3 000 electric vehicles and 1 000 buses for public transport by 2035;
  • the strengthening of energy efficiency programmes in households and services, through the dissemination of LED lamps, high-efficiency stoves and high-performance refrigerators, and the improvement of energy efficiency in buildings (households, industry, shopping centres, etc.) and equipment. 4.2.2 Contribution of the Agriculture, forestry and other land use (AFOLU) sector

The Agriculture, Forestry and other Land Use (AFOLU) sector remains the foundation of the mitigation strategy of the Republic of Congo, owing to its predominant role in carbon sequestration and in reducing emissions from land-use change. 4.2.3 Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU)

The Republic of Congo aims to progressively decarbonise its industrial fabric by promoting the use of clean and sustainable technologies. 4.2.4 Waste

Waste management is a major environmental and climate challenge in the Republic of Congo, in a context of population growth and rapid urbanisation. The national waste management system remains poorly structured and is marked by an absence of up-to-date data with national coverage.

According to available data, in Brazzaville, for example, only 5%13 of the waste produced daily is collected and transported to uncontrolled landfills or open dumps, without biogas capture or soil cover, resulting in significant emissions of methane (CH₄) and nitrous dioxide (NO2). The city generates around 1 200 tonnes of waste per day (i.e. 372 482 tonnes per year), mainly from households, offices, shops and restaurants, with an average production of 0,65 kg/inhabitant/day. This waste is composed of 32 % organic matter, 10 % plastics, 7 % paper, 6 % glass, 5 % each of wood and textiles, 3 % metals, 2 % rubber and leather, 1 % ceramics, and 29 % miscellaneous waste (incombustible 6 %, fine elements 12 %, and other)14.

With regard to sanitation, the country has structural shortcomings. In Brazzaville, available data indicate that 97,8 % of households have a sanitation service, but this coverage remains largely precarious and uneven across neighbourhoods15. The majority of households (54 %) use simple latrines, while 18 % make do with pits without a slab, which poses significant health risks. Only 27 % of households have access to improved facilities, such as ventilated dry latrines or pour-flush latrines16.

Added to this is biomedical waste, which is disposed of by incineration using unsuitable methods. These different components of waste management (solid and liquid) generate GHG emissions.

In the reference scenario (BAU) and on the basis of available data, projections of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the waste sector show an upward trend. In the absence of specific interventions, emissions would rise from 838 ktCO₂eq in 2020 to 1 280,84 ktCO₂eq in 2035, an increase of more than 52 % over the period.

In the conditional scenario, however, implementation of the planned measures would significantly reverse this trend. In 2030, emissions would be reduced to 989,31 ktCO₂eq, corresponding to a decrease of 10,8 % compared with the BAU scenario. This reduction would become more pronounced by 2035, with emissions estimated at 981,50 ktCO₂eq, a reduction of 23,37 % compared with the level projected without intervention.

5 ADAPTATION CONTRIBUTION

The Republic of Congo, which is highly exposed to the impacts of climate change, places adaptation at the heart of its NDC3.0. This priority responds to the need to protect populations, ecosystems and infrastructure in the face of increased risks, while consolidating the gains of socio-economic development. The adaptation contribution thus aims to strengthen the resilience of key sectors of the national economy, to anticipate future climate shocks and to promote sustainable and inclusive development. It is based on the vulnerabilities identified, the priorities expressed in national policies and the contributions of stakeholders during the process, and defines concrete actions to integrate adaptation into planning at all levels, taking into account the specific vulnerabilities of children, adolescents, young people, women, persons living with disabilities, older persons and Indigenous Peoples.

5.1 Adaptation objectives

In connection with its vulnerability, the main adaptation objectives within the framework of the NDC include:

  • Strengthen the resilience of populations (including young people, children, women, persons with disabilities, Indigenous Peoples) and ecosystems, by improving capacities for anticipation, prevention and response to climate hazards (floods, droughts, coastal erosion, water erosion, climate-related diseases, bush fires, etc.);
  • Reduce the vulnerabilities of sensitive sectors, in particular agriculture, forestry, water resources, health, tourism, coastal zones and human settlements, through structural and non-structural measures integrated into national and local policies.

These objectives constitute the guiding framework for the implementation of adaptation actions, in line with the country's national development priorities and international commitments. They will be achieved through measures carried out in the various sectors identified as vulnerable to climate change.

5.2 Sectoral contribution to adaptation

5.2.1 Agriculture

Agriculture in the Republic of Congo is an essential pillar for food security and poverty reduction, accounting for around 10 % of GDP and employing nearly 40 % of the working population (World Bank, 2022; FAO, 2021). Predominantly family-based and subsistence, it remains highly dependent on rainfall, which makes it particularly vulnerable to climate hazards.

5.2.6 Coastal zone

The Republic of Congo has around 169 km of Atlantic coastline, home to sensitive ecosystems (mangroves, lagoons, wetlands), strategic infrastructure (ports of Pointe-Noire and Brazzaville, industrial zones, oil installations) and a large share of the urban population (UNESCO, 2022; World Bank, 2022). This coastline offers a continental shelf of 10 000 km² and an Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) rich in fishery and energy resources, constituting an essential hub of national development.

However, the coastal zone is highly vulnerable to the effects of climate change and to human pressures. The retreat of the shoreline, reaching several hundred metres in 40 years in the bays of Loango and Pointe-Noire, threatens populations, infrastructure and biodiversity. The historic cemetery of Loango is already submerged. Sea-level rise and the salinisation of groundwater aquifers are aggravating the loss of fresh water and weakening ecosystems.

The socio-economic consequences are major: loss of housing, decline in artisanal fishing, degradation of infrastructure and increased vulnerability of coastal communities dependent on petty trade (UNICEF, 2022, UNDP, 2021). Protection structures remain insufficient, while urban and port planning still only weakly integrates climate resilience. In order to strengthen the resilience of the coastal territory, several priority orientations are set out in the National Strategy for the Sea and Inland Waters18: (i) develop a holistic approach to erosion for appropriate solutions; (ii) carry out an in-depth study on saline intrusion in Pointe-Noire; (iii) develop a coastal space management policy.

Sea-level rise, combined with coastal erosion and marine flooding, constitutes the main threat. It endangers natural habitats (mangroves, beaches, lagoons) and increases the salinisation of soils and groundwater aquifers, affecting agriculture and access to fresh water (IPCC, 2022). Mangroves, which protect coastlines and are home to rich biodiversity, risk significant decline under the combined pressure of climate change and human exploitation (UNEP, 2020).

6 CROSS-CUTTING ISSUES AND OTHER STRATEGIC CONSIDERATIONS

The successful implementation of NDC3.0 depends not only on sectoral mitigation and adaptation actions, but also on taking into account essential cross-cutting dimensions. These guarantee the effectiveness, equity and sustainability of the country's climate efforts. They include the involvement of the private sector, the integration of gender and social inclusion, the active participation of youth and children, the promotion of a just transition and taking into account issues related to loss and damage. The integration of these cross-cutting dimensions aims to strengthen Congo's resilience while ensuring climate action that is inclusive and beneficial for society as a whole.

6.2 Gender and social inclusion, children, youth and Indigenous Peoples

Aware that the inclusion of children, youth and women is an integral part and a requirement of climate justice, social cohesion and sustainability, the Republic of Congo makes this theme an important pillar of its NDC3.0.

Indeed, the climate hazards and their impacts (floods, droughts, epidemics, food insecurity and ecosystem degradation)19 that the country faces disproportionately affect vulnerable groups, in particular children, youth and women, who represent more than 70 % of the national population (UNICEF, 2025 ; UNDP, 2024 ; UNDP, 2022). This increased vulnerability results from structural factors, restrictive sociocultural norms and limited access to resources, essential services and decision-making bodies. The situation is much more severe for persons living with disabilities, older persons and Indigenous Peoples who, in addition to being sensitive to climate hazards, face major obstacles in accessing water, hygiene and sanitation services, particularly in times of crisis (Handicap International, 2022).

Children, who make up nearly half of the Congolese population, are the most exposed to the effects of climate change. The Children's Climate Risk Index (CCRI) ranks Congo among the high-risk countries. The 2023 floods interrupted the schooling of more than 43 000 children, destroyed schools, contaminated water sources and worsened child malnutrition (UNICEF, 2024). Young people, girls and boys, although drivers of innovation, social mobilisation and community resilience, are often excluded from decision-making processes, climate finance mechanisms and governance spaces (UNDP, 2022).

Rural women are particularly affected by the effects of climate change, as they manage water, food and family hygiene in increasingly difficult conditions. The growing scarcity of resources increases their workload and weakens their incomes, while leading to girls dropping out of school, exposing them to increased risks of abuse and early marriage. In precarious urban neighbourhoods, children, youth and women live in areas at high climate risk: unstable slopes, flood-prone riverbanks, lack of basic services. In displacement camps, girls and women are exposed to increased risks of violence, lack of privacy and poor menstrual hygiene management. Although they are at the heart of climate problems, they remain under-represented in climate-related decision-making bodies.

Social and structural inequalities amplify the vulnerability of children, youth, women, older persons, persons living with disabilities (PWD) and Indigenous Peoples (IP) to climate change. These challenges particularly affect: (i) education, notably the absence of climate content in curricula and the weak training of teachers, limiting the awareness and participation of young people, especially rural girls, in the ecological transition and green jobs; (ii) access to water and sanitation (WASH), notably insufficient and inadequate infrastructure that increases the burden on women, exposes children to health risks and excludes persons with disabilities from essential services; (iii) participatory governance, notably the low representation of women, youth and Indigenous Peoples in decision-making bodies, which hinders inclusive climate governance and the valorisation of local knowledge.

To respond to these challenges, three priority areas of action are proposed in order to ensure an inclusive, equitable and sustainable climate response.

6.3 Just transition

The Republic of Congo considers just transition as a strategic pillar of its response to climate change. It aims to ensure that the transformation towards a low-carbon and resilient economy is equitable, inclusive and socially just. In a context of dependence on extractive industries, vulnerability of rural and Indigenous populations, and the need for economic diversification, just transition represents an opportunity for sustainable development and the reduction of inequalities. The country recognises that climate impacts and transition measures affect social groups differently, in particular women, youth, informal workers and rural or forest communities. The integration of the principle of climate justice into public policies aims to prevent the worsening of inequalities while ensuring the active participation of all actors in the low-carbon transformation.

Just transition is understood as a transformation process reconciling equity, social, economic sustainability and citizen participation. It relies on the commitment of the State to promote social protection, job security, access to modern and affordable energy services, and the development of low-carbon economic opportunities. It also requires the creation of institutional frameworks guaranteeing transparency and consultation between trade unions, the private sector, civil society and local authorities.

The Republic of Congo envisages just transition as a vector of social cohesion and shared prosperity, aligned with the African Union's Agenda 2063, the 2030 Agenda and the Paris Agreement, to strengthen collective resilience and promote inclusive development.

6.4 Loss and damage

The Republic of Congo, although a low emitter of greenhouse gases, remains highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, generating significant economic, social and environmental loss and damage. In recent years, the intensification of extreme precipitation has caused major floods: those of 2019 and 2020 affected around 170 000 people in Sangha, 200 000 in Likouala, as well as a large number of inhabitants of Cuvette and Plateaux. In Likouala, rainfall was twice the seasonal average, and the level of the Oubangui River reached a record since 2009. These disasters devastated crops, caused a surge in food prices and required a humanitarian intervention by the United Nations. The 2022–2023 period was even more critical, affecting 320 891 people and causing 1720 deaths, while 2 292 hectares of cultivated land were submerged, aggravating food insecurity and population displacement.

In parallel, the country suffers severe coastal erosion linked to sea level rise, leading to the disappearance of villages, the destruction of infrastructure and the degradation of coastal ecosystems. On the environmental front, deforestation results mainly from shifting slash-and-burn agriculture, unsustainable consumption of biomass and forestry and mining exploitation. However, Congo has a low deforestation rate (0,10 %/year, i.e. 25 886 ha) (Republic of Congo, 2024).

Taking loss and damage into account in its climate commitment aims to strengthen national resilience, improve early warning mechanisms, and develop insurance, recovery and compensation systems adapted to the most vulnerable populations.

6.5 Other strategic considerations

Holistically, the NDC3.0 integrates dimensions that are not negligible but extremely important for the success of its implementation. These aspects include, among others: Peace and security, Cross-border initiatives and other strategic issues.

  • Peace and Security

Climate change amplifies social, economic and security risks. In the Republic of Congo, pressure on resources (land, forests, water, fisheries) exacerbates community tensions, threatens livelihoods and silently weakens certain social cohesion in the affected communities. Extreme phenomena (floods, droughts) can cause population displacement and increase vulnerability. Climate action is therefore a lever for peace. The NDC 3.0 promotes sustainable resource management, strengthens community dialogue and integrates conflict prevention into adaptation. This climate-peace-security link is a strategic axis for preserving national stability and strengthening resilience.

  • Cross-border cooperation

Climate change requires a regional response. The Republic of Congo shares with its neighbours strategic ecosystems (forests, watersheds, fishery resources) whose preservation requires concerted management. The NDC 3.0 promotes the harmonisation of policies on forest management, conservation and the development of resilient infrastructure. The country relies on the frameworks of CEMAC, COMIFAC and ECCAS to implement joint protection and sustainable management projects. This renewed commitment to regional cooperation is essential to address common climate challenges and maximise shared benefits.

  • Other issues

The climate trajectory of the Republic of Congo integrates several cross-cutting dimensions set to become increasingly important: (i) Blue economy: the coastline and marine resources represent a strategic potential to diversify the economy, create sustainable jobs and strengthen food security. Its development must be based on responsible management of marine ecosystems, sustainable fisheries, resilient aquaculture and the protection of coastal areas; (ii) Freshwater management: water security, essential for agriculture, hydropower and biodiversity, depends on the restoration and protection of aquatic ecosystems, contributing directly to adaptation and resilience; (iii) Traditional knowledge and nature-based solutions: the valorisation of local knowledge and ancestral practices is an asset for community resilience, particularly in rural and forest areas. Their integration into climate policies fosters local ownership, improves the effectiveness of adaptation measures and preserves cultural and environmental heritage.

7 MEANS OF IMPLEMENTATION AND GOVERNANCE

Over recent years, the Republic of Congo has undertaken significant efforts to stimulate its economic growth, improve the well-being of its population and strengthen the stability of its socio-economic structures. 7.1.2 Finance mobilisation mechanisms

Climate finance is indeed a central determinant of the ambition and effectiveness of the envisaged actions, both for mitigation and for adaptation, as well as for capacity building, research and technology transfer. In this context, an integrated approach seems to be a better option for the Republic of Congo.

At the national level, the Congolese State could strengthen the strategic orientation of its domestic resources in favour of climate action. Climate-sensitive budgeting, and even more so gender-sensitive budgeting, should constitute a major innovation allowing climate and social issues to be systematically integrated into sectoral budget allocations. At the same time, the Environmental Protection Fund will play a leveraging and catalysing role in mobilising national and international resources in favour of priority programmes, notably in the sectors of renewable energy, forestry, sustainable agriculture and adaptation of coastal zones. Alongside national public finance, national private finance will be an important lever in the implementation of the NDC. National banks and financial institutions, insurance companies, microfinance companies, extractive, agro-industrial and forestry companies are key partners to fill public financing gaps. The development of incentive mechanisms (ecotaxes, carbon taxation, public guarantees, climate PPPs) will encourage their engagement. Public-private partnerships appear to be essential levers for stimulating private investment in strategic low-carbon value chains.

At the international level, there are multilateral and bilateral climate finance instruments that the country could take advantage of. The Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and the Adaptation Fund are major levers to support the implementation of structuring projects, in particular in the field of agricultural resilience, sustainable land management and the protection of forest and coastal ecosystems. Resource mobilisation opportunities will increase with the New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG), which aims to mobilise 300 billion US dollars per year by 2035. This endorsed decision calls for the mobilisation of 1,300 billion dollars by 2035. The tripling of finance dedicated to adaptation by 2035, agreed at COP30, also constitutes an opportunity to accelerate the implementation of adaptation projects and the building of the country's resilience.

In addition, the operationalisation of the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD)21 represents an important opportunity to address economic and non-economic loss and damage associated with the adverse effects of climate change. The official launch of the Barbados Implementation Modalities (BIM) thus triggers this window of resource mobilisation that Congo can seize. However, the country should take action to meet the prerequisites for accessing this funding, notably the appointment of a focal point, national scoping studies and the establishment of a national loss and damage mechanism.

At the regional level, institutions such as the African Development Bank (AfDB), the Development Bank of Central African States (BDEAC), as well as bilateral cooperation, also offer complementary opportunities. Congo will also rely on the regional frameworks, namely the Congo Basin Climate Commission (CCBC), the Central African Forests Commission (COMIFAC), the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Congo Basin Forest Partnership (PFBC), in the implementation of its NDC. The CCBC offers a climate cooperation platform enabling the mobilisation of technical support and joint advocacy for increased access to climate finance. Meanwhile, COMIFAC is an essential lever for the harmonisation of forest policies, capacity building and the implementation of the AFOLU actions planned in the NDC. ECCAS, for its part, by fostering the integration of climate priorities into regional development, resilience and energy strategies, will be an asset or a platform for the mobilisation of regional programmes and initiatives beneficial to Congo. Finally, through the PFBC, Congo will be able to further strengthen cooperation with technical and financial partners engaged in the conservation and sustainable management of the Congo Basin forests. Together, these institutions offer Congo a structuring framework to support and accelerate the effective implementation of its NDC.

In addition to these traditional sources, there are innovative financing mechanisms, notably green bonds, climate insurance instruments, blue funds, windows dedicated to resilient finance and philanthropic funds, which Congo intends to explore in order to diversify its financing channels and reduce its dependence on official development assistance.

7.1.3 Opportunities offered by Article 6 of the Paris Agreement and access approaches

a) Article 6 and opportunities for Congo

In addition to traditional climate finance mobilisation mechanisms, Article 6 of the Paris Agreement offers an opportunity for Congo in the implementation of the NDC3.0. For Congo, the use of Article 6 will be primarily directed towards conditional actions, in order to attract international financing and private investment that will complement domestic efforts. This approach will ensure that international cooperation does not affect the achievement of the unconditional targets, while providing a strategic lever to accelerate the implementation of the priority projects identified in the NDC3.0.

Article 6.2 – Bilateral cooperation: Congo may conclude bilateral agreements with other Parties to transfer mitigation outcomes in the form of Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs). These mechanisms will make it possible to finance low-carbon projects in key sectors such as energy, forestry, transport and agriculture, while ensuring environmental integrity and avoiding double counting.

Article 6.4 – Multilateral market mechanism supervised by the UNFCCC: Congo could consider developing projects certified under this mechanism, in particular in sustainable forest management, agroforestry, waste-to-energy and renewable energy. The carbon credits generated could be traded on international markets in order to mobilise additional financing. The mechanism is supervised by the UN through the UNFCCC. However, it requires the country to have a Designated National Authority (DNA), whose role will be to approve projects and interact with the system.

Article 6.8 – Non-market approaches: Congo could take advantage of the opportunities offered by this mechanism of non-market cooperation, focused on capacity building, technology transfer, South-South cooperation and technical support, in particular in the management of forest ecosystems, agricultural resilience and food security.

b) Approach to accessing climate finance under Article 6 for Congo

As a starting point, the Republic of Congo will consider developing a national strategy document on Article 6 and carbon pricing that will assess the country's readiness to take advantage of these instruments for the implementation of its future NDCs and climate plans, including the development of its long-term low GHG emission development strategy under the guidance of the UNFCCC. This process will also include stakeholder awareness-raising and engagement through a participatory process ensuring national ownership, transparency and policy coherence. Overall, the process will be structured around the following points:

Define a clear national framework: Congo will put in place a legal and institutional framework to govern Article 6 projects, including project eligibility criteria; an authorisation and monitoring mechanism; a national carbon credit registry. Congo will need to establish a designated national authority (DNA), or strengthen the existing structure, responsible for coordinating transactions under Article 6. This body will be responsible for approving activities, supervising exchanges of emission reduction units, and ensuring compliance with the requirements of transparency, environmental integrity and sustainability. The operationalisation of a reliable national measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) system will also be essential to attest emission reductions and avoid double counting.

Strengthen local capacities: Training will be organised for ministries, local authorities and project developers in order to: (i) understand the technical requirements of the Article 6 mechanisms, (ii) repair projects compliant with international standards. Particular attention will need to be paid to the transfer of low-carbon technologies, training of public and private actors, and networking with other African countries in order to share good practices.

Identification and prioritisation of promising sectors: Target as a priority sectors with high mitigation potential and co-benefits for adaptation and sustainable development, such as forestry and land use, renewable energy, energy efficiency, waste and sustainable agriculture. These sectors offer significant opportunities to generate transferable emission reductions (ITMOs) while contributing to national development priorities. The country will then propose pilot projects in the identified priority sectors.

Development of strategic partnerships and private sector mobilisation: Access to Article 6 financing will require the establishment of solid partnerships with buyer countries, institutional investors, and national and international private actors to co-develop projects, access carbon finance platforms, and ensure the transparency and traceability of results. Congo will need to develop clear and incentivising frameworks to attract private financing, while ensuring an equitable sharing of benefits and maximising local socio-economic spin-offs.

The systematic integration of science, technology and innovation (STI) into public policies is an imperative for achieving the sustainable development goals. 7.1.5 Technology transfer

Technology transfer constitutes a central lever to enable the Republic of Congo to implement its climate commitments. The country has significant potential in the areas of renewable energy, sustainable forest management, resilient agriculture and waste valorisation, but remains limited by access to suitable technologies, which are often costly and require specific know-how.

7.1.6 Capacity building, communication and awareness-raising for ownership of the NDC3.0

Capacity building, communication and awareness-raising constitute indispensable pillars for the effective and inclusive implementation of the NDC3.0 in the Republic of Congo. Capacity building and communication needs The capacity building and communication priorities in the context of the implementation of the NDC are structured around the following axes:

7.2 Governance framework and institutional arrangements

The governance framework and institutional arrangements for the implementation of the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) of the Republic of Congo are based on an integrated architecture fostering coordination, transparency and the inclusive participation of all actors.

At the strategic level, the mechanism is steered by the Strategic Orientation Committee (COS), chaired by the Prime Minister. This body ensures political oversight, defines the main orientations and ensures the integration of climate priorities into national development policies. It meets every year. It is made up of the CNCC and representatives of governmental political institutions.

A Monitoring-Evaluation & MRV Committee ensures operational monitoring, the consolidation of performance indicators and the management of the national mechanisms for measurement, reporting and verification of actions. This committee is responsible for developing dashboards, steering periodic evaluations and feeding accountability and transparency processes. This committee manages the MRV system, the Biennial Transport Reports and the National Communications. It is composed of the Monitoring-Evaluation Directorates of the various ministries, national statistics institutions, and GHG inventory institutions. It will be steered by the National Environment Agency (ANE)24 and will meet on an annual basis. The ANE will have within it a Climate Directorate that will ensure the coordination of these activities.

The Technical Orientation Committee (COT), chaired by the Minister in charge of climate, ensures intersectoral technical coordination. It ensures the coherence of mitigation, adaptation and means of implementation mobilisation actions. It meets once a year, bringing together the Technical Units (CT)25 and the Monitoring-Evaluation & MRV Committee. In addition, the COT includes representatives of the governance bodies of the private sector, local and regional authorities, CSOs/NGOs, and the Focal Points of the various funds and conventions. The COT may consult the advisory bodies and/or invite ad hoc bodies on specific issues. The COT produces annual reports resulting from the compilation of the reports of the CTs and of the monitoring-evaluation & MRV committee and transmits them to the COS.

Three specialised Technical Units (CT) are established according to the 3 main components of the NDC (mitigation, adaptation and means of implementation) and support the operational functioning of the framework:

  • the Mitigation Unit, responsible for the planning, monitoring and evaluation of emission reduction actions in key sectors (energy, AFOLU, waste, IPPU, etc.);
  • the Adaptation Unit, which coordinates the implementation of measures relating to the resilience of vulnerable sectors (water resources, biodiversity, coastal zone, health, infrastructure, etc.);
  • the Means of Implementation Unit, responsible for the mobilisation of financing, capacity building, technology transfer and communication.

These units rely on several sectoral Working Groups (GT) according to their fields of intervention. The Unit is made up of representatives of each working group. They meet every six months and produce their report on the basis of those of the GTs. The GTs include energy, agriculture, forestry and land use (AFOLU), waste, and industrial processes with regard to Mitigation. Water resources, the coastal zone, Human settlements and health, Infrastructure, Biodiversity and ecosystems, tourism, in addition to AFOLU for Adaptation. As regards the Means of Implementation Unit, it has under it the Planning & finance, Capacity building and Communication, and Research & Technologies groups. The organisations/associations in charge of gender, youth, indigenous peoples and local authorities, and the private sector are integrated directly into the various groups according to their area of intervention in order to ensure inclusiveness and the participation of all on the various themes relating to climate change.

The Technical Units are headed by a Chair, a Vice-Chair and a Secretary. The chairmanship rotates annually within the CTs, guaranteeing inclusive participation.

The advisory bodies: the National Assembly, the Senate and the Economic, Social and Environmental Council (CESE) contribute to the institutional dialogue and democratic accountability of the process.

In addition, ad hoc bodies composed of technical and financial partners, regional institutions and resource persons provide occasional support for expertise, resource mobilisation and the concerted implementation of climate actions.

Finally, the Climate Finance Unit (UFC) plays a key role in the coordination and monitoring of financial flows related to the implementation of the NDC. Its role is to accelerate the mobilisation of resources for the implementation of the NDCs. It brings together experts from the Ministry in charge of climate (notably the ANE), the Ministry of Forest Economy (notably the Carbon Task Force), the Ministries in charge of Finance and Budget (Directorates in charge of finance / resource mobilisation), the Ministry in charge of the economy and planning (Planning Directorate), and the Ministry in charge of Cooperation (notably the Directorate in charge of bilateral cooperation).

This mechanism aims to ensure effective, participatory and results-oriented climate governance, thus guaranteeing the coherence and sustainability of the actions carried out by Congo by 2035. Its operationalisation will be financially supported by sectoral budgets and the national budget. Meetings of the groups and technical units will be held at the ministerial level and will not require significant financial resources. The institution chairing the technical unit will be responsible for convening these meetings, organised twice a year. As for the technical groups, their meetings will take place within the framework of the usual functioning of the ministries concerned. In addition, certain technical and financial institutions may support the organisation and holding of these meetings.

Regarding the establishment of the mechanism, it is planned that the groups and technical units will be formalised quickly, within three months following the official submission of the NDC, by a decree on the implementation of Congo's NDCs. This decree will officially establish the planned structures and will also define the modalities for creating the Climate Finance Unit, whose operationalisation will require specific technical assistance. The effective establishment of this unit will take place during the second or third year of implementation of the NDC, given the prior studies required.

7.3 Measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) system

The Republic of Congo has committed to putting in place a national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system in order to guarantee rigorous, transparent and harmonised monitoring of the implementation of its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). This mechanism falls within the framework of the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) provided for by the Paris Agreement and aims to ensure the country's accountability to the international community, while strengthening the planning and steering of climate policies at the national level.

The purpose of the MRV system is (i) to ensure accurate measurement of progress made in the implementation of mitigation and adaptation actions, (ii) to produce reliable and regular reporting at the national and international levels, (iii) to guarantee verification of the quality and consistency of the information generated. As such, it will feed not only the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR) and the Global Stocktake, but also national processes such as budget planning, project monitoring and accountability to technical and financial partners.

The Congolese mechanism is based on several complementary components. Data collection and management constitute the basis of the system, with mechanisms to ensure enhanced traceability and reliability. A national registry will centralise mitigation and adaptation actions and cross-cutting measures, in order to ensure their monitoring and evaluation. This registry will be supplemented by specific tools for tracking projects and financing, making it possible to document the implementation status of initiatives, the financing mobilised, the funds actually received and disbursed, as well as the financial needs not yet covered. This information will be aggregated through an Implementation Progress Tracker, which will measure the country's overall progress in achieving the targets of its NDC and provide a consolidated view for the preparation of transparency reports.

The MRV system will also constitute a coordination framework between the actors involved in the implementation of climate policies. It will make it possible to clarify the roles and responsibilities of the various institutions, ensure coherence between actions carried out at the sectoral and national levels, and strengthen the effectiveness of climate governance. In addition, it will play an essential role in Climate Budget Tagging, in order to strengthen the alignment of financial resources with national climate priorities and improve the integration of climate issues into the country's fiscal and economic policy.

In the coming years, the Republic of Congo intends to progressively consolidate and operationalise this mechanism. Priorities will focus on strengthening institutional and technical capacities for data collection and verification, the introduction of digital tools suited to the monitoring and management of information, and the establishment of multisectoral and inclusive coordination. These efforts will make it possible to ensure greater credibility of the information transmitted, strengthen the confidence of partners and guarantee greater effectiveness in mobilising the resources needed to implement the NDC.

8 CONTRIBUTION TO DEVELOPMENT AND ALIGNMENT WITH THE SDGS

The implementation of the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo is not limited to reducing greenhouse gas emissions and strengthening climate resilience; it also constitutes a lever for sustainable development. By following an integrated approach, the NDC3.0 contributes not only to the achievement of several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), but is also aligned with the African Union's Agenda 2063. This section highlights the synergies between Congo's climate priorities and regional and global objectives, thus underlining the dual ambition of the NDC3.0: combating climate change while accelerating the country's socio-economic development.

8.1 Socio-economic co-benefits and alignment with Congo's socio-economic development policies.

The implementation of the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo generates significant socio-economic co-benefits that go beyond combating climate change. Indeed, the mitigation and adaptation measures adopted foster the creation of green jobs, in particular in the sectors of sustainable agriculture, forest management, renewable energy and waste valorisation. These jobs help to boost the local economy, in particular for young people and women, and support the economic diversification advocated by national policies.

At the social level, the planned actions strengthen food security, improve access to clean and affordable energy, reduce air and water pollution, and foster better access to basic services for vulnerable populations. They thus contribute to improving quality of life and reducing inequalities, consistent with national priorities for combating poverty and social inclusion.

Furthermore, the NDC3.0 is closely aligned with the main development orientations of the Republic of Congo, notably the National Development Plan (PND) and Vision 2030, which aim to diversify the economy, promote inclusive and sustainable growth, and strengthen the resilience of populations to crises. This convergence between climate action and development demonstrates that the NDC3.0 constitutes not only a climate policy instrument, but also a catalyst for economic and social transformation.

8.2 Alignment of the NDC3.0 objectives with the sustainable development goals (SDGs)

The link between the NDC3.0 and the SDGs of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development translates into multiple synergies. These goals constitute a common roadmap for all countries and peoples. Their aim is to ensure peace, prosperity and sustainable development for people and the planet. Thus, actions in favour of the energy transition contribute directly to SDG 7 (affordable and clean energy) and SDG 13 (climate action), while the promotion of sustainable and resilient agriculture strengthens SDG 2 (zero hunger) and SDG 13 (climate action). Similarly, initiatives relating to the sustainable management of water and forest ecosystems support SDGs 6 (clean water and sanitation), 15 (life on land) and 14 (life below water) respectively. These synergies contribute to achieving the targets of the 2030 Agenda while consolidating the convergence between climate action and national development.

Beyond 2030, the NDC3.0 also projects a vision aligned with sustainable development prospects for 2035. The structuring measures implemented today, notably in the sectors of energy, agriculture, infrastructure and natural resource management, will lay the foundations for a resilient, low-carbon and socially inclusive Congolese economy. These efforts are linked to the emerging post-2030 priorities, helping to prepare the country to meet long-term challenges and to take advantage of the opportunities of more transformative sustainable development.

8.3 Alignment of the NDC3.0 actions with the African Union's Agenda 2063 (according to the country context)

The African Union's Agenda 2063 constitutes the strategic roadmap for a prosperous, inclusive, resilient and sustainable Africa. The actions planned under the NDC3.0 of the Republic of Congo are fully in line with this dynamic by contributing to the achievement of several flagship aspirations of this Agenda.

First, the mitigation and adaptation measures adopted support Aspiration 1 relating to a prosperous Africa based on inclusive growth and sustainable development. Through the promotion of renewable energy, energy efficiency, resilient agriculture and the sustainable valorisation of forest resources, the NDC3.0 supports economic diversification, energy and food security, as well as the creation of green jobs, in particular for young people and women.

Next, the NDC3.0 is aligned with Aspiration 3, which envisions an Africa characterised by good governance, respect for human rights and justice. The integration of transparency, stakeholder participation, and the consideration of gender and social inclusion dimensions in the implementation of the NDC reflect this commitment.

Furthermore, by strengthening the resilience of populations and ecosystems to the impacts of climate change, NDC3.0 contributes to Aspiration 6, focused on an Africa whose development is people-driven, ensuring health, well-being, education and sustainability for all.

Finally, the mobilisation of regional and international partnerships around climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building enables Congo to align its climate actions with the logic of Aspiration 7, which promotes Africa as a strong, united and influential player on the global stage.

9 CONCLUSION

The Republic of Congo, through this third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0), consolidates the achievements and lessons learned from the implementation of NDC2.0. NDC3.0 stands out through strengthened climate ambition, broader sectoral coverage and an integrated approach to low-carbon and resilient development. It covers the energy, agriculture, forestry, industrial processes, waste and land-use change sectors, while integrating the cross-cutting dimensions of gender, research, technological innovation and social inclusion.

In terms of mitigation, the Republic of Congo remains a net carbon sink and aims to strengthen this positive contribution to the global climate by 2035. It thus commits to reducing its emissions by 22,02 % compared with the reference scenario (BAU), i.e. the equivalent of 21 932,31 ktCO₂eq of emissions avoided or sequestered in an unconditional scenario. With international technical, financial and technological support, the country could achieve a total reduction of 47,82 %, corresponding to 26 569,47 ktCO₂eq. In adaptation, the country aims to strengthen resilience in the key sectors covered.

The overall financial need for the implementation of NDC3.0 is estimated at approximately 8 616,33 million USD, of which 5 814,32 million USD for the mitigation component, 2 505,68 million USD for the adaptation component, as well as an amount of 296,33 million USD intended to support cross-cutting measures. These needs will be covered through blended financing combining national public resources, private sector contributions, support from technical and financial partners, as well as innovative climate finance mechanisms, notably the Green Climate Fund, the Global Environment Facility, and carbon markets, the fund for responding to loss and damage. Existing regional frameworks such as the Congo Basin Climate Commission (CCBC), the Central African Forest Commission (COMIFAC), the Economic Community of Central African States (ECCAS) and the Congo Basin Forest Partnership (CBFP) will be leveraged to accelerate the implementation of its NDC. Philanthropic funds will also be explored for the implementation of the NDC.

The governance framework established for the implementation of NDC3.0 is based on national coordination led by the Ministry in charge of climate, in close collaboration with sectoral departments, local authorities, the private sector and civil society. This mechanism is supported by a strengthened Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system, ensuring transparency, traceability and accountability for the actions undertaken and the finance mobilised.

NDC3.0 gives a central place to the private sector and highlights the role of scientific research, technological development and innovation, considered essential levers for increasing the effectiveness of climate policies and stimulating endogenous solutions adapted to the Congolese context. Furthermore, Congo reaffirms its commitment to social inclusion and respect for gender, ensuring that women, youth, children, girls, Indigenous Peoples and persons living with disabilities are fully involved in the design, implementation and monitoring of climate actions. This inclusive approach ensures that the climate transition contributes to social justice, the reduction of inequalities and the consolidation of human development.

This process, which embodies an ambitious vision, paves the way for a resilient and low-carbon Congo, where the fight against climate change becomes a driver of sustainable prosperity and inclusiveness. Its success will depend on continued national mobilisation and a solid international partnership, founded on shared responsibility and global climate solidarity.
