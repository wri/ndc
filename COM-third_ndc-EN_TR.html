PREFACE

Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges facing humanity, and the Union of the Comoros is no exception. This phenomenon, marked by global warming, is already causing worrying impacts that threaten our populations, our ecosystems and our infrastructure. As an island developing State, our country is disproportionately exposed to these climate risks, in particular sea-level rise, coastal erosion, agricultural disruption and increased rainfall variability. In accordance with the Paris Agreement, to which the Union of the Comoros has been a party since 2016, our country reaffirms its commitment to contributing to the global effort to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and to strengthening its climate resilience. The Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0) reflects this renewed ambition, by integrating all the national mitigation and adaptation priorities for the period up to 2035, with a long-term vision consistent with the Plan Comores Émergent. The CDN 3.0 places the emphasis on reducing GES emissions through targeted actions in key sectors such as energy, transport, waste, agriculture and forestry, while integrating a gradual transition towards clean energy and improved energy efficiency. On the adaptation side, it strengthens actions to increase the resilience of communities in the face of climate shocks, particularly in the areas of agriculture, water management, health, infrastructure, ecosystems and disaster risk reduction.

INTRODUCTION

The Union of the Comoros, an island developing State located in the Indian Ocean, is particularly vulnerable to the effects of climate change. Made up of four main islands, Grande Comores, Anjouan, Mohéli and Mayotte, which has remained under French administration, the country is exposed to growing risks such as sea-level rise, coastal erosion, flooding, temperature increases, droughts, tropical cyclones and loss of biodiversity. These phenomena directly threaten food security, access to drinking water, public health, infrastructure and people's livelihoods, in particular those living in rural and coastal areas.

Aware of these challenges, the Union of the Comoros has developed its Third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0), which represents a major step forward compared with the previous versions. This revision strengthens national mitigation and adaptation commitments, aligning them more closely with the Paris Agreement, the ODD 2030 agenda and the African Union's Agenda 2063. It reflects the country's desire to reconcile energy transition, climate resilience and economic growth, despite limited capacities and persistent socio-economic constraints.

In terms of mitigation, the CDN 3.0 provides for an unconditional reduction of 14 % in greenhouse gas emissions by 20351 and a conditional reduction of 43 %2 thanks to international support. These ambitions are based on the development of renewable energies, energy efficiency, the promotion of clean transport and the sustainable management of natural resources, contributing to the construction of a low-carbon energy model.

Adaptation constitutes a central pillar of the national strategy. The priorities identified concern resilient agriculture and food security, integrated water resources management and sustainable access to drinking water, the protection of coastal and marine areas, the restoration of mangroves and the sustainable use of fishery resources, the strengthening of public health in the face of climate risks, as well as the preservation of biodiversity and the restoration of forest ecosystems. These measures aim to reduce the vulnerability of populations and to ensure the sustainability of ecosystems, while supporting livelihoods and local economic development. The CDN 3.0 also promotes inclusive insurance and climate risk financing

as an essential tool to strengthen the resilience of populations and support recovery after disasters. The implementation of the CDN 3.0 requires investments estimated at approximately 6,6 billion USD3 by 2030, which can be mobilised through international cooperation, technology transfer and capacity building. The success of this approach rests on an inclusive and participatory approach, involving public institutions, civil society, the private sector, local authorities and technical and financial partners, in order to guarantee a climate response that is equitable and adapted to national realities. Beyond 2030, the CDN 3.0 embodies the strategic vision of the Union of the Comoros: to build a country that is resilient to climate change, to protect its populations and its ecosystems, to promote a sustainable and inclusive energy transition, and to contribute actively to global efforts to combat climate change by reconciling adaptation, mitigation and sustainable development. The question of the carbon market in the Comoros is taking on a growing place in the national climate strategy, but today it relies more on the potential of natural sinks (in particular land and mangroves) than on a structured domestic market. The 3rd CDN and the national inventories show that, in the scenarios selected, the Union of the Comoros can present a strongly negative net balance (net sink) by 2030 as a result of actions in the AFAT sector and the restoration of mangroves, which opens up opportunities for carbon credits based on removals (blue carbon, reforestation, sustainable agricultural practices). The BUR1 and the inventories nevertheless highlight gaps in MRV (measurement, monitoring and verification), in the baseline reference and in institutional architecture, prerequisites needed to generate credible credits and to participate transparently in international markets (Article 6). In the short term, the priorities are therefore: (i) to strengthen the inventories and MRV capacities to this end, a roadmap was drawn up in 2020 for the establishment of a national MRV system, (ii) to define a national framework for governance and benefit-sharing for credits (safeguards, transparency, inclusion of local communities), and (iii) to target mechanisms compatible with the long-term vision (credits for coastal/mangrove restoration, carbon conservation, and renewable energy projects) in order to attract financial support and technology transfer. If well used and properly regulated, the valorisation of the climate service of ecosystems could become a complementary source of financing for adaptation and resilience, to

condition that strict social and environmental safeguards are ensured and that every action is placed within the mitigation and adaptation trajectory defined by the 3ᵉ CDN.

1. GUIDING PRINCIPLES

The Union of the Comoros ratified the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (CCNUCC) on 31 October 1994 as a non-Annex 1 Party, the Kyoto Protocol on 10 April 2008, and the Paris Agreement on 23 November 2016. In doing so, the Comoros affirmed its commitment to the global programme aimed at achieving the objective of Article 2, paragraphs 1(a) and 1(b) of the AP.

| Article 2, paragraphs 1 a) and 1 b) of the Paris Agreement

  • Holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1,5 °C above pre-industrial levels, recognizing that this would significantly reduce the risks and impacts of climate change.

  • Increasing the ability to adapt to the adverse effects of climate change and fostering climate resilience and low greenhouse gas emissions development, in a manner that does not threaten food production.

The Comoros submitted its intended nationally determined contribution (CPDN) in 2015.

The Paris Agreement (Article 4, paragraph 2) requires each Party to prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions (CDN) that it intends to achieve.

Thus, in accordance with decisions 1/CP.19 and 1/CP.20 and with its Plan Comores Emergent 2030, the Comoros revised its CPDN to produce its CDN 2.0 in 2021, and communicates through this document its Third Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.O) in order to reflect its commitment and its contribution to the fight against climate change.

The elements set out in the Nationally Determined Contribution of the Comoros are a synthesis of the country's ambitions and public policies which, at a time of a turning point in its development, chooses to commit resolutely to sustainable development based on a low level of greenhouse gas (GES) emissions. The country thus wishes to contribute to international efforts to combat global warming by pursuing the objective of being a carbon sink by 2030.

The country also prepared and submitted its Third National Communication on climate change in 2023 and an updated biennial report (BUR) in 2023, and is preparing its first biennial transparency report (BTR1) to inform the Convention of its emissions, of the progress made in implementing mitigation and adaptation, and of the support received and needed.

2.3. Economy

The Comorian economy is little diversified and is essentially centred on the primary sector. Indeed, the latter, made up of agriculture and fisheries, still represents a large and growing share of the PIB of the Comoros, which highlights the potential for future structural transformation. The primary sector contributed 36,4% of PIB in 2022, compared with about 30% in 2010. This increase mainly results from a rise in the contribution of the fisheries sector, which went from 7,3% of PIB in 2010 to 13,1% in 2022. In parallel, the agricultural sector experienced a rebound, with growth of 3% over the period 2021-2022, compared with -0,4% between 2016 and 20195. Thus, PIB grew by 1,9 % in 2021 compared with 0,2 % in 2020.

The primary sector provides 57 % of total employment, of which 62,7 % is held by women, and 90 % of operating revenue.

The economy also relies largely on consumption, driven by remittances from the diaspora, and lacks diversity, the private sector being small and essentially informal, contributing very little to value added. Foreign grants have only worsened the absence of incentives to invest in human capital and in the investment stock. Unemployment rates are relatively high, especially among young people. Poverty is a real challenge: according to estimates, 39% of the population lived below the poverty line in 2022. This situation is aggravated by high inflation rates, peaking at 12,4% in 2022 before falling back to 9,2% in 2023 and then to 2,2 per cent in 2024.

With a gross national income per capita of 1360 $, the Union of the Comoros has just been classified among lower middle-income countries. The ambition is to move into the upper bracket, at more than 4000 $ per capita by 2030, drawing on the successful practices and models of other emerging countries to put the Union of the Comoros on the path of economic expansion, break with cycles of weak and volatile growth and create growth momentum that should reach an average of more than 7,5 % in 20306.

2.4. Climate

The climate of the Comoros is determined by the maritime tropical influence of the Indian Ocean and the south-east trade winds. Its geographical location, north-west of Madagascar, gives it a largely tropical climate, with small seasonal variations in temperature. However, the altitude of

certain parts of the islands, in particular on the main island of Grande Comore (Ngazidja), considerably influences the regional microclimate.

Two seasons mark the year: the rainy season, hot and humid, from November to April, and the dry season, cooler, from May to October. During the rainy season, temperatures often reach 31 °C and are accompanied by heavy rainfall. During the dry season, temperatures fall slightly and average between 23 and 27 °C. The trade winds moderate the heat and ensure a thermal balance. In particular, the western coasts of the islands, exposed to the winds, are drier than the eastern coasts, exposed to the humid trade winds. Each of the islands of the archipelago has its own climatic characteristics:

On Grande Comore, the Karthala volcano determines the climate. At altitude, from about 800 m upwards, rainfall is more frequent and temperatures cooler, with daily averages of 18 to 22 °C. In contrast, the coastal areas are markedly warmer, with daily temperatures between 25 and 30 °C and a high humidity level.

Anjouan has a rugged topography with densely vegetated ridges. Humidity there is constantly high and up to 2 000 mm of rain falls per year. The rainy season runs from November to April. The climate is cooler in the mountains of Anjouan than in the flat coastal areas.

Mohéli is the smallest of the three main islands and has a more uniform climatic profile, with a distribution of temperatures and rainfall similar to that of Anjouan, but with a downward trend in extreme values owing to the lower relief.

2.5. Climate Change Vulnerability

The Union of the Comoros is particularly vulnerable to climate risks. According to the Notre Dame global adaptation index, the Comoros is the 29th most vulnerable country to climate change and ranks 163rd in terms of adaptation readiness, out of 191 countries assessed.

The archipelago is exposed to numerous natural disasters that seriously affect natural capital, the population and physical infrastructure. Between 2018 and 2023, the country suffered 11 tropical depressions or cyclones, with cyclone Kenneth causing the heaviest damage. This damage represented 14% of PIS, leading to a drop in economic growth from 3,6% in 2018 to 1,9% in 2019. More than 345 000 people, or 40% of the population, were affected

by the cyclone, of whom 185 000 severely affected and 12 0007 displaced. Despite these challenges, the Comoros has significant opportunities to strengthen its resilience in the face of climate crises.

The main lines of intervention for the Comoros in the face of climate change are adaptation and resilient development, the country having to face additional costs of 836 million USD8 by 2050 because of climate impacts. Developing an adaptation plan would make it possible to improve water management, strengthen coastal protection and develop climate-smart agricultural practices, and to strengthen the adaptive capacity of sectors linked to natural resources. In addition, it will be essential to implement strategic economic diversification in order to limit future climate impacts for low-carbon development.

3.1. EMISSIONS PROFILE

3.1.1. AFAT sector for the 2010-2024 time series

The greenhouse gas inventory for the AFAT sector in the Comoros over the period 2010-2024 highlights a particularly favourable dynamic, with a net carbon sink that has more than doubled in fifteen years, reaching about -910 Gg CO2éq in 2024. This exceptional performance is the result of still extensive land management, positive land conversion, and the maintenance of the natural sinks that are forests, grasslands and wetlands.

Emissions from livestock, manure management and urea use remain limited, in the absence of any notable intensification of the agricultural sector. However, this favourable situation could change with demographic pressure, the expansion of settlements and agricultural changes. It is therefore crucial to institutionalise monitoring, to strengthen methodological transparency, and to preserve key ecosystems, in order to guarantee in the long term the strategic role of the AFAT sector in climate change mitigation in the Comoros.

3.1.2. Energy sector for the 2010-2024 time series

Total emissions from the energy sector rose from about 133 Gg CO2éq in 2010 to 320 Gg CO2éq in 2024, an increase of more than 140 % in fifteen years. This increase reflects the growing dependence of the Comorian economy on fossil fuels, particularly in the electricity generation, land transport, fisheries and residential sectors. A major peak was recorded in 2022, reaching about 322 Gg CO2éq, followed by a decline in 2023 to 308 Gg CO2éq, before a slight upturn in 2024. This trend is largely influenced by the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, marked by a significant reduction in economic activity in 2020, then by a rapid recovery between 2021 and 2022.

5.1.1 Context

The forest sector (Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry - UTCF) of the Union of the Comoros plays a crucial role in carbon storage and climate regulation. The recent greenhouse gas inventories (2024) confirm that this sector constitutes the country's main carbon sink, largely offsetting the emissions of the other sectors. The objective of this section is to present the trajectories of the evolution of this carbon sink for the period 2024-2035. The data are based on the 2025 national inventory, which establishes a reference net sequestration level of -910 Gg CO2eq in 2024.

5.1.2. Methodology for developing the scenarios

The scenarios aim to assess how forest management policies can increase the country's sequestration capacity. The projections incorporate the most recent inventory data (FAT 5) and the ambitions of the 3rd National Communication.

5.1.3. Types of scenarios

Three scenarios are presented:

Scenario 1 - Baseline Trend (Business As Usual, BAU): Continuation of current dynamics. Although the forest is under pressure, the net balance remains negative (sink) and increases naturally because of the growth of the existing biomass and of the accounting methodologies, reaching -1 260 Gg CO2eq in 2035.

Scenario 2 - Unconditional Measures: National reforestation programme and fire control funded from the country's own resources. It aims to moderately strengthen the carbon sink beyond the BAU.

Scenario 3 - Conditional Measures: Very ambitious scenario incorporating massive international support for widespread agroforestry, watershed restoration and strict protection. This scenario aims to maximise the carbon sink in order to reach -1437 Gg CO2eq in 2035.

5.1.11. Conclusions and recommendations

The BAU scenario shows a significant loss of forest carbon if no measure is taken. The mitigation scenarios demonstrate the potential for emission reductions through the combination of sustainable management, reforestation and protection of critical areas. Thus, the scenario with conditional measures constitutes the most effective strategy for achieving the national GES reduction objectives. This requires the mobilisation of the resources needed for its implementation. However, limitations are observed with the uncertainties regarding the sequestration rate and the carbon emission factors, natural regeneration and future climate impacts.

To achieve these objectives, it is recommended to:

  • Establish a national sustainable forest management plan including all stakeholders.

  • Strengthen institutional and technical capacities for the implementation of restoration programmes.

  • Integrate the monitoring of carbon stocks into national forest policies.

  • Promote agroforestry and community management of resources.

  • Develop an incentive framework for local reforestation initiatives.

  • Mobilise climate finance to support pilot projects.

  • Implement annual spatial monitoring of forest cover.

  • Strengthen forest monitoring and data collection to improve the accuracy of the models.

  • Introduce concrete measures for the storage and availability of forest data.

  • Couple the mitigation measures with socio-economic programmes (green jobs, local communities).

  • Integrate the results into the potential national REDD+ strategy and the NDC in order to align policies and international objectives.

  • Encourage participatory restoration and reforestation with monitoring of carbon sequestration by Island

Thus, the scenario with conditional measures, although more costly, is economically the most advantageous in terms of emission reductions and ecosystem services. The adoption of the scenario with unconditional measures may constitute a transitional step while awaiting additional funding. The mobilisation of carbon credits, subsidies and international partnerships is essential to guarantee financial viability.

5.2. Mitigation Agriculture Sector

5.2.1. Trend analysis

The Agriculture sector (excluding forestry) represents a major issue for the Union of the Comoros, both for food security and for the national carbon footprint. According to the data of the AFAT 5 Sectoral Report (2025), the emissions of this sector are dominated by livestock, which constitutes a significant share of methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N20) emissions.

The analysis of the sub-categories reveals that emissions are driven by three main sources:

1) Enteric fermentation (Livestock): This is the predominant source. The herd (cattle, goats), often managed extensively with nutrient-poor feed, generates large quantities of methane during digestion. 2) Agricultural soil management: Direct and indirect N20 emissions linked to the spreading of synthetic (urea) and organic fertilisers, as well as to soil leaching. 3) Manure management: Although more modest, this source contributes to emissions through the storage and treatment of animal waste without biogas recovery.

The historical trend shows a direct correlation between population growth and the increase in livestock numbers, leading to a mechanical rise in emissions in the absence of an improvement in productivity per head

5.3. Mitigation in the Energy sector

5.3.1 Trend analysis

The Energy sector is a priority sector for the Union of the Comoros, with a share above 60 % of the country's overall emissions according to the data of the latest Greenhouse Gas Emissions Inventory Report for the Energy Sector of the Comoros 2025.

The analysis of trends by sub-sector of the GES inventories of the Energy sector shows a continuous rise in emissions between 2010 and 2024 with an average annual rate of about 7 % for a sector that is the leading GES emitter and one of the key categories.

Indeed, a few endogenous driving factors explain this rise, namely:

  • Population growth with more than 140 000 inhabitants between 2010 and 2024.

  • Urbanisation with an increase in demand for electricity and fuels;

Dependence on diesel for electricity generation, whose main sources are still diesel thermal generating sets;

  • The low penetration of renewable energy, below 5 % in 2024. However, a relative stabilisation of emissions post-2020 can be observed, notably from 2021 to 2024 at around 300 GgCO2eq, essentially due to:

  • The effects of the first pilot solar projects and better management of grid losses;

  • The (admittedly slow) scaling up of climate policies (CDN, GCF projects, etc.).

5.3.2. Definition of the base year (2023).

The year 2023 is indeed a relevant reference year for the GES mitigation scenario of the energy sector in the Comoros, as it marks a strategic, statistical and institutional turning point.

The justifying factors in this regard are therefore multiple, notably:

1. The consolidation of sectoral data thanks to the improved monitoring of its energy system, which goes through the strengthening of its data collection capacities in 2022-2023, notably via projects supported by PNUD and the World Bank, 2. An updated national inventory with the production of several climate-energy reports in 2023, allowing a better estimation of emissions by source (diesel, biomass, solar) but above all, 3. The establishment of a reliable statistical base with consumption, production and energy import data that are more complete than in 2010-2020.

5.4. Mitigation in the Industrial Processes and Product Use Sector

5.4.1. Trend analysis

The Industrial Processes and Product Use (PIUP) sector in the Comoros is still embryonic, with industry contributing only about 3,7 % of national PIB. It is characterised by activities that are mostly artisanal or small-scale processing (agri-food, ylang-ylang distillation, bakeries).

Analysis of the GES inventories shows that this sector's emissions come exclusively from the use of imported products, as the country has no heavy industry. The two main emitting categories identified in the April 2025 inventory report are:

1) The use of fluorinated substitutes (HFC-134a): Mainly for refrigeration and air conditioning.

2) The use of non-energy products: In particular lubricants and paraffin waxes.

Although total emissions are low compared with energy, the trend is upward. Emissions went from a stable level of around 6-7 GgCO2eq between 2017 and 2021 to a peak in 2022, linked to an abrupt rebound in imports post-COVID-19. In 2024, reference emissions stand at 5,56 GgCO2eq. The drivers of this structural increase are:

  • Growing demand for cooling (residential and commercial air conditioning, food cold chain) driving up imports of HFC gases.

  • The increase in the fleet of vehicles and motorised equipment, stimulating lubricant consumption.

5.5. MITIGATION IN THE WASTE SECTOR

5.5.1. Trend analysis

The waste sector in the Comoros, although representing a modest share of total emissions compared with the energy or agriculture sector, shows a worrying growth dynamic linked to demographic pressure and urbanisation.

According to the Greenhouse Gas Emissions Inventory Report: Waste Sector (2025), the sector's emissions increased by around 38 % between 2010 and 2024, rising from 30,5 GgCO2eq to 42,1 GgCO2eq. This increase is linear and shows no sign of structural slowdown.

Analysis of the sub-sectors reveals that emissions are dominated by:

1) Wastewater treatment and discharge: a source generating mainly methane (CH4) via septic tanks and rudimentary latrines.

2) Uncontrolled landfills: Although theoretically significant, their modelled emissions remain low for the time being because only a small fraction of waste is actually collected and stored in bulk.

The drivers of these emissions are structural: rising waste production (estimated at 0,3 kg/inhabitant/day), a composition dominated 50 % by fermentable organic matter, and an almost total absence of sorting or recovery (composting) infrastructure.

It is a pivotal year marking the end of a period of informal management and the beginning of structured planning (National Waste Management Plan).

Likewise, persons with disabilities are rarely taken into account in energy infrastructure projects, which limits their access to adapted services. The integration of specific inclusion and capacity-building measures would help to guarantee equitable access to these benefits.

  • In the agricultural sector, the modernisation of practices and the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions must also incorporate social dynamics. Women, who are predominantly active in food crops, and young people, who struggle to access land and credit, risk being marginalised if mitigation policies do not provide targeted support. 8.3.2. Just transition: a lever for equity, inclusion and social sustainability

In accordance with the 2015 Paris Agreement and in line with the OIT guiding principles on just transition, the Union of the Comoros establishes just transition as a cross-cutting and strategic component of its new Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN 3.0). This approach aims to ensure that the country's ecological transformation is accompanied by social justice, the creation of decent jobs and sustainable human development, while ensuring that women, young people and persons with disabilities participate fully in the benefits of the transition and in the decisions that shape it.

The national approach to just transition is thus based on an articulation between climate innovation, social inclusion and structural reforms. It aims to reconcile climate change mitigation and adaptation with social justice, gender equity and the reduction of inequalities.

The Union of the Comoros indeed recognises that the transition towards a green and resilient economy must be conducted in a fair and inclusive manner, by ensuring that the benefits of this transformation benefit everyone, leaving no one behind. It is within this framework that the State plans to adopt a regulatory framework for just transition in order to respond to the need for a structuring tool that guides the integration of social justice and equity considerations into the planning, implementation and monitoring of climate change mitigation and adaptation measures. To do so, it would be worthwhile to put in place clear and measurable social indicators (rate of green jobs created, share of women in jobs, access of informal workers to social protection, training in green skills) and to develop a diversified financing framework. The country thus plans to implement a just transition based on:

  • Training and capacity building, through the development of professional retraining programmes, green skills training in priority areas (renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, water and waste management, ecological tourism) and support to SMEs to foster innovation, sustainable entrepreneurship and the transfer of clean technologies. The vocational training system will be adapted to anticipate the labour market transformations linked to the ecological transition.

  • The creation of decent jobs, through the promotion of green investments, the development of sustainable entrepreneurship and the professional retraining of workers from sectors that could be negatively impacted, while seizing the new opportunities for green job creation. Public employment services will be strengthened to support occupational transitions and to facilitate the match between skills and new economic needs.

  • Equity and social inclusion, by ensuring that the benefits of the transition reach all categories of the population, in particular women, young people and persons with disabilities, through active policies of economic empowerment, participation in climate governance and equitable access to green jobs and training;

  • Universal social protection, including the extension of coverage to workers in the informal economy, the establishment of insurance and compensation mechanisms for vulnerable populations affected by transition policies, and the promotion of a social, solidarity-based and inclusive economy. Specific social indicators (e.g. green jobs created, share of women and young people in green jobs, rate of access to social protection, number of retrained workers) will make it possible to monitor the progress achieved. Respect for fundamental rights at work and the strengthening of occupational health and safety in order to anticipate climate risks and guarantee a safe and healthy working environment; Tripartite social dialogue, through the creation of a regulatory framework for the just transition, bringing together social partners, civil society, local authorities and representatives of vulnerable groups, in order to ensure participatory, transparent and inclusive governance. This framework will make it possible to structure the integration of social and economic dimensions into the planning, implementation and monitoring of climate policies. This approach will foster coherence between mitigation, adaptation and sustainable human development objectives.

Ultimately, the integration of the just transition into the CDN will make it possible to turn environmental challenges into development opportunities, to strengthen national resilience and to promote a more inclusive economic model. This strategic choice reflects the will of the Union of the Comoros to make the fight against climate change a vehicle for social progress and shared prosperity.

The sixth assessment report of the GIEC highlighted the link between climate change and health throughout the world. Recognised as a fundamental right in the Constitution of the Union of the Comoros of 23 December 2003, the health sector is subject to deep structural constraints which inhibit its capacities for resilience and adaptation. The Comorian State has little room for manoeuvre to invest in health care (the share of public health expenditure in the State budget fell from 8,6% in 1998 to about 8,09% in 2010, far behind the 15% recommended by the Summit of Heads of State in Abuja).

The country's health system is particularly exposed to a plurality of direct and indirect threats. These include:

The resurgence of waterborne diseases, favoured both by the growing scarcity of drinking water and by the contamination of water resources;

The spread of vector-borne diseases whose epidemiological dynamics are altered by changing temperature and rainfall patterns

The worsening of respiratory disorders, linked to the deterioration of air quality under the combined effect of polluting emissions and suspended particles;

The increase in cases of malnutrition, resulting from the decline in agricultural yields and fisheries production

The emergence of mental health issues and psychological trauma, often following the recurrent climate disasters that are cyclones, floods and landslides. These vulnerabilities compromise socio-health determinants (health equity, access to care), amplify pandemic risks and hinder the achievement of the sustainable development goals.

This precariousness further weakens the country's capacity to cope with the usual disease burden and makes it vulnerable to any additional shock.

However, the initiatives under way, strong in their anchoring in multisectoral projects and in their mobilisation of active technical and financial partnerships, demonstrate the sector's capacity to attract international funding oriented towards health, nutrition and water security objectives.

These initiatives recognise the systemic nature of the risks through objectives of strengthening the sector's governance, climate-adapted planning, community awareness-raising and regional cooperation (tele-expertise, patient transfers with Réunion to compensate for the lack of specialists).

8.5.2. Climate change, Impacts and children's rights

The Union of the Comoros concentrates most of the climate risk factors: heatwaves, flooding, sea level rise, cyclones, drought….

Its island geography, its dependence both on rain-fed subsistence agriculture and on financial, technical and technological resources coming from outside for its socio-economic development, make it a "hotspot" of climate vulnerability, where children's rights are on the front line. The country's vulnerability rate in the face of climate change is close to 82%, placing the country in the category of countries at high climate risk. The impacts of extreme climate events (cyclones and floods) translate into massive displacements of the population, the destruction of vital infrastructure, and the expansion of vector-borne diseases, such as malaria, into new areas.

The impact chain of the health sector (figure) focuses on the evolution of waterborne diseases (typhoid, cholera) and vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue, fever) induced by changes in temperature and rainfall. The most vulnerable age groups are people over 65 years of age and those under 12 years of age.

8.5.3. Engagement and participation of children and young people

With 53,8 % of its population under the age of 20, the Union of the Comoros is one of the youngest countries in the world. This youth, mostly rural and subject to precarious socio-economic conditions, bears the full brunt of the consequences of climate disruption.

It is particularly vulnerable to health risks (malnutrition, waterborne and vector-borne diseases), to shocks affecting education (destruction of school infrastructure, displacement) and to threats to its future livelihoods, in a country where the primary sector (agriculture) employs a large share of the working population.

This early and prolonged exposure makes Comorian youth a central group, not only as a highly vulnerable population, but also as an essential driver for building a resilient and low-carbon national trajectory. Their participation is a strategic imperative for an effective, sustainable and socially just climate response.

8.5.3.1. Priority areas of action for transformative youth participation

In order to move from formal participation to transformative participation, the engagement of children and young people must be embedded in the key sectors of the climate response identified for the country.

Education, awareness-raising and peer mobilisation

This will involve: - Taking into consideration, within the foundations of education, the systematic integration of climate change into school curricula, from primary school to secondary school, in connection with local realities (coastal vulnerability, food security, health). - Setting up and supporting environmental clubs in schools and universities, ensuring the funding and support for the implementation of initiatives such as the establishment of resilient school gardens and the development of reforestation campaigns and waste recycling and recovery actions.

- Ensuring the training of young climate ambassadors capable of relaying scientific information, raising awareness among their peers and their communities in local languages, and using social media to disseminate suitable messages. Innovation and green entrepreneurship Innovation and the promotion of green entrepreneurship are a means of encouraging young people to become more involved in environmental and climate issues. In concrete terms, this consists of:

- Ensuring the creation of support mechanisms for youth entrepreneurship in the green economy through incubators dedicated to adaptation solutions (climate-smart agriculture, water management, - Decentralised renewable energies) and mitigation solutions (waste recovery, energy efficiency). - Enabling facilitated access to green microcredit for projects led by young people, particularly in sustainable agricultural value chains, agroforestry or eco-responsible tourism.

Participation in decision-making and monitoring processes The participation of young people in decision-making on health and climate takes place through:

- The development and operationalisation of an institutional architecture promoting the consultation of young people in the design and monitoring of local climate policies (communal development plans) and national ones (sectoral strategies). - The creation of municipal and national youth councils for the climate, with a clear mandate and an operating budget. - The involvement of young people in community early warning and disaster risk management systems, as relays and actors of preparedness.

8.5.3.2. Implementation of integrated adaptation and mitigation actions led by young people

In order to capitalise on the innovation potential of young people and to spark a surge of collective action from this youth, it is necessary to develop and support the implementation of concrete programmes that combine ecological resilience, socio-economic security and the transfer of technical and technological skills.

These initiatives must be designed as demonstrators of sustainable solutions, generating tangible environmental benefits while offering professional prospects in the green economy.

Priority will be given in particular to labour-intensive ecological restoration programmes which, being managed by and for young people, combine carbon sequestration and coastal protection with the creation of local jobs.

In parallel, the promotion of agroecology must be operationalised through the multiplication of farm-schools and demonstration plots led by young farmers, thus serving as levers to disseminate resilient practices and reduce dependence on external inputs. Finally, the establishment of citizen science projects will make it possible to involve young people in the systematic collection of data, thereby contributing to the enrichment of national climate monitoring systems and to the territorial anchoring of adaptation policies.

8.5.4. Strategic avenues for integrating health and children into adaptation policies

8.5.4.1. Integrated approach in the face of interlocking vulnerabilities

In view of the Comoros' increased vulnerability to climate shocks – which manifests itself in a resurgence of hydrometeorological hazards, increased pressure on natural resources and a degradation of the social determinants of health – it appears imperative to rethink adaptation policies by integrating into them, in a structural and cross-cutting manner, health dimensions and the specific rights of children and young people.

This demographic group, which makes up more than half of the national population and whose health, education and development are particularly sensitive to environmental disruption, cannot be regarded as a mere passive beneficiary of adaptation measures, but indeed as a central pillar and a strategic lever of any sustainable national resilience.

Consequently, the design and implementation of climate policies must necessarily rely on strengthened governance, targeted programmes and rigorous monitoring of the differentiated impacts on this generation, a sine qua non condition for resilient and equitable development.

8.5.4.2. Strategic avenues for an integrated adaptation policy that protects health and the rights of the child

Strengthening intersectoral governance and integrated territorial planning

In order to move beyond traditional sectoral approaches, which tend to fragment the response to climate challenges, it is recommended to establish a high-level intersectoral governance framework, such as a National Health-Climate-Childhood Committee, placed under the auspices of the General Secretariat of the Government.

This committee, bringing together the relevant technical ministries as well as representatives of civil society and youth organisations, would have the mandate to harmonise strategies, to allocate resources coherently and to ensure that all public policies – whether they concern spatial planning, agriculture, water or energy – are subject to a systematic assessment of their potential impacts on child and youth health. In parallel, decentralised territorial planning must be reoriented to incorporate explicit criteria for protecting children against climate risks, in particular through fine-grained mapping of vulnerabilities and the development of Communal Adaptation Plans including a mandatory and funded component dedicated to "health-childhood".

Deployment of targeted and resilient programmes in the fundamental social sectors

The resilience of health and education systems constitutes an essential foundation of adaptation. In this respect, the capacities of the primary health system to prevent, detect and manage climate-sensitive pathologies in children should be significantly strengthened, through the training of specialised community health workers, the provision of essential inputs and the integration of environmental epidemiological surveillance.

Likewise, the education sector must be transformed into a pillar of community preparedness and resilience; this involves the generalisation of the environmental and climate education programme, guaranteeing uninterrupted access to drinking water and sanitation, the integration of education on climate and health risks into curricula, and the establishment of safe and climate-resilient school infrastructure. Finally, social protection, often neglected in adaptation strategies, must be rethought as an essential buffer against climate shocks for vulnerable households with children, through the development of climate-sensitive safety nets and innovative financial mechanisms.

Institutionalisation of youth participation and mobilisation of scientific knowledge Considering that the effectiveness and legitimacy of adaptation policies are strengthened by the involvement of those primarily concerned, it is strategic to institutionalise formal mechanisms for the participation of children and young people at all stages of the climate policy cycle, from design to monitoring and evaluation. This participation, which must go beyond the consultative stage to include an effective consideration of their contributions, can be embodied in local and national youth councils for the climate, endowed with real resources. At the same time, decision-making must be based on a solid and specific knowledge base, generated by a National Health-Childhood-Climate Observatory and by participatory action-research programmes involving young people. This production and capitalisation of knowledge are indispensable in order to ground policies that are evidence-based and adjusted to local realities.

Mobilisation of dedicated financing and building of strategic partnerships The concrete translation of these strategic orientations is conditional on substantial and lasting financial mobilisation, which requires both a priority allocation in the national budget and an increased capacity to attract international climate financing, ensuring that the latter incorporates results indicators relating to the health and well-being of children. In parallel, the building of multiform strategic partnerships – with the United Nations agencies (UNICEF, OMS), international financial institutions, civil society and the private sector – is essential in order to combine the technical expertise, financial resources and local anchoring necessary for the success of this complex undertaking.

9. ALIGNMENT OF THE CDN WITH THE 2030 AGENDA

9.1. Taking traditional knowledge and the knowledge of local communities into account in planning.

The analysis of good practices carried out in the Comoros highlights the importance of traditional knowledge and local knowledge in the planning and implementation of climate actions. These practices, often arising from community experience, illustrate community-based adaptation (ABC) approaches that are fully rooted in local socio-cultural realities. They demonstrate that communities hold valuable knowledge on the sustainable management of natural resources, the protection of biodiversity, and resilience in the face of climate hazards.

These local initiatives are consistent with national strategic frameworks, notably the Plan Comores Émergent (PCE), the National Adaptation Programme of Action (PANA) and the Nationally Determined Contribution (CDN), which recognise the central role of communities in the implementation of environmental and climate policies. They also reflect the national will to strengthen local and participatory governance in the field of climate action.

At the international level, these good practices contribute to the achievement of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (PDD-H2030) and of several Sustainable Development Goals (ODD), notably those relating to the fight against poverty (ODD1), sustainable agriculture (ODD2), gender equality (ODD5), economic growth (ODD8), the reduction of inequalities (ODD10), sustainable cities (ODD11), responsible consumption (ODD12), climate action (ODD13), the protection of marine and terrestrial ecosystems (ODD14 and ODD15), as well as partnership for the implementation of the goals (ODD17).

Thus, taking traditional knowledge and local know-how into account in national planning constitutes an essential lever for strengthening community resilience, guaranteeing the sustainability of climate interventions and ensuring effective ownership of public policies by the populations concerned.

As a developing country, the Comoros continues to enhance its mitigation efforts and is moving towards economy-wide emission reduction or limitation targets, conditional on predictable financial, technical and technological support.

7. Contribution to the objective of the Convention

(a) Contribution to Article 2 (Convention):

By preserving its forests and decarbonising its energy, the Comoros contributes to the stabilisation of GES concentrations, avoiding any dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system. By significantly increasing its forest carbon sink and undertaking a deep energy transition, the Union of the Comoros actively participates in the stabilisation of GES concentrations in the atmosphere, well beyond its historical responsibility.

(b) Contribution to the objectives of the Paris Agreement (1.5°C):

The revised CDN is fully aligned with the 1,5°C objective. It presents a deep decarbonisation trajectory of 57 % of the economy's overall emissions by 2035 (relative to BAU), while strengthening the country's net sequestration capacity (-1 437 Gg CO₂eq). By maintaining its status as a net carbon sink (removals greater than emissions), the Comoros positions itself as a model of low-carbon and resilient development.

(c) Support needs:

The total cost of implementing the CDN (2025-2035) is estimated at approximately 430 Million USD. Achieving the conditional share depends on mobilising the Green Climate Fund, the FEM and bilateral/multilateral partners.
