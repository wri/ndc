Welcome

Dear readers:

We present to you the update of our Nationally Determined Contribution, in which Costa Rica is already a country that delivers on decarbonization and, at the same time, we are not starting from zero: we defended forests when it was not popular and our electricity is already mostly renewable. The challenge now lies at the margin: each additional tonne avoided costs more and requires strong and significant coordination among different public and private actors, as well as seriously executing what we can indeed finance and manage.

Our policy is simple: greater well-being per colón invested. That is why transport and energy come first, where electromobility adds value, seeking better public transport service and where the efficient use of alternative fuels solves real problems. In industry and construction, efficiency and adaptation come first; as for the substitution of polluting fuels with renewable resources and their viable solutions, without passing unnecessary costs on to citizens and achieving a just transition.

In adaptation, we move from paper to the territory: water for people and production, better-planned coasts, cooler and safer cities with productive and resilient agro-landscapes. Biodiversity and forests —our greatest asset— remain at the centre, now with greater focus on the Sustainable Agro-landscape.

We know that additional ambition has increasing marginal costs; therefore, we will develop projects and mobilize private investment and cooperation; where appropriate, we will use market mechanisms with environmental integrity. Full transparency: we will report progress and results; if something does not work, it will be corrected.

This Nationally Determined Contribution is for real people because it is pragmatic and realistic: for those waiting for a bus that arrives on time, for the family that needs a stable electricity bill, for the producer who will face a longer drought and for the coastal communities that already see sea levels rising. A just transition is not decreed; it is built by listening and creating opportunities for all.

Thank you for joining us in this shared task. We invite you to work together: less rhetoric, more implementation and results; fewer transaction costs and more solutions.

Executive summary

Costa Rica updates its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) for the period 2025–2035 with a comprehensive vision that articulates mitigation and adaptation, under the principles of progression, environmental integrity, transparency and social justice. It consolidates progress and raises climate ambition through a multisectoral portfolio of actions, anchored in national climate governance and in commitments to human rights, gender equality, youth participation and recognition of the contribution of Indigenous peoples; aligned with a trajectory compatible with 1,5 °C and with the Global Goal on Adaptation.

The mitigation headline target is expressed in two complementary parameters: (i) an absolute maximum of net emissions in 2030 of 11 380 Gg of CO2e; and (ii) a maximum net emissions budget for 2025–2035 of 132 700 Gg of CO2e, covering all sources and sinks of the INGEI 1990–

2021. In relative terms, the target is equivalent to approximately a 53% reduction compared with the scenario without additional measures for 2030; and even under the set of unconditional actions, a reduction of around 23% relative to the same scenario is estimated.

Methodologically, the update applies the CMA guidance: 4/CMA.1 for the Information to facilitate Clarity, Transparency and Understanding (ICTU) of the mitigation contribution; and 9/CMA.1 for the Adaptation Communication. The NDC details assumptions, scope, timeframes and accounting rules; in addition, it integrates the needs, approaches and monitoring arrangements for adaptation, consistent with the Fourth National Communication, the First Biennial Transparency Report and the National Adaptation Plan 2022–2026. The economy-wide multisectoral technical modelling was based on an extension of the CLEW framework, consolidated as CLEW‑CR‑3.0 to capture interactions among energy, industrial processes and product use, agriculture, FOLU and waste, as well as synergies with oceans.

The human rights and inclusion approach cuts across the NDC. Just transition is consolidated as a principle of design and implementation, with specific governance and linkage with the National Development and Public Investment Plan. Gender equality incorporates guidelines from Decision 3/CP.25 and is operationalized through the National Action Plan on Gender Equality in Climate Action, with affirmative measures, capacity building, employability, risk management and the production of disaggregated information. Youth participation is institutionalized through the National Youth Forum on Climate Action, and dialogue with Indigenous peoples is based on the current PAFT and the General Consultation Mechanism, recognizing their contributions to conservation and to REDD+.

The financial strategy combines national instruments and external cooperation to ensure the bankability and scaling up of the priority portfolio. The portfolio includes national public resources; direct private sector investment; cooperation and multilateral banking; and market mechanisms (Article 6) —including the possibility of ITMOs under 6.2 and the channelling of projects under 6.4 with corresponding adjustments—. The purpose is to accelerate strategic measures, cover just transition costs and strengthen MRV systems. The use of markets is subject to rules and safeguards that ensure traceability, additionality and no double counting, aligned with national ambition.

In mitigation, the main signal is the transformation of the energy and mobility system, industrial efficiency and innovation, and the reduction of emissions in the supply chain and in buildings; all of this articulated with waste management, the strengthening of the FOLU sink and the integration of nature-based solutions, including blue carbon. The portfolio avoids fragmentation by sector in day-to-day implementation: it recognizes interdependencies (for example, renewable electricity and industrial thermal processes; mobility and urban planning; bioenergy and waste; construction and water/energy efficiency), maximizing co‑benefits such as health, productivity, reduction of black carbon, resilience and quality employment.

For adaptation, the NDC prioritizes reducing vulnerabilities and strengthening resilience to current and future climate risks, with a territorial and population emphasis. Institutional and financial arrangements are proposed to enable investments in resilient infrastructure, risk management, protection of ecosystems and services, access to water and food security, integrating climate information and early warning mechanisms. The linkage with national policies —National Climate Change Adaptation Plan (PNACC), performance and impact evaluation, sustainable finance taxonomy— seeks to align the allocation of resources with country targets and sustainability criteria.

Monitoring is strengthened through MRV systems, with traceability in the National Climate Change Metrics System (SINAMECC), performance indicators and impact evaluation. Consistency with the Information to facilitate Clarity, Transparency and Understanding (ICTU) ensures the comparability and international understanding of the contribution, while the use of disaggregated data —including gender and territorial data— strengthens accountability. The country will maintain iterative learning cycles to adjust implementation based on evidence and reinforce synergies between mitigation and adaptation.

The main risks to implementation include: regulatory and coordination lags; cost-of-capital barriers in clean technologies and logistics; limitations in technical and project management capacities; and exogenous shocks (climatic or economic) that put pressure on the emissions budget. To mitigate them, the NDC proposes: dynamic prioritization by marginal return; preparation of bankable portfolios with due diligence; activation of financial instruments (including PPPs and blended finance) and of carbon markets with integrity; strengthening of capacities and governance; and public communication mechanisms that sustain social support.

In sum, the 2025–2035 update offers a realistic and verifiable roadmap to sustain decarbonization and resilience, while consolidating a just and inclusive implementation framework. Success depends on executing the complete portfolio, securing timely financing and protecting critical projects, under rules of integrity and transparency. With these foundations, Costa Rica maintains its climate leadership, transforms its economy for the benefit of people and ecosystems, and contributes tangibly to the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

Opening Message

Costa Rica renews the country's commitment to low-emission and climate-resilient development. This Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) expresses a long-term vision towards decarbonization, while strengthening adaptation to reduce vulnerability and increase the resilience of people and ecosystems.

The NDC 2025–2035 adopts an intersectoral approach that articulates public and private efforts, promotes innovation and investment, and fosters synergies between mitigation and adaptation. The targets are aligned with a trajectory compatible with 1,5 °C and with the global goal on adaptation, integrating national and territorial planning.

To turn that ambition into results, the country will consolidate and scale up ongoing initiatives: forest conservation and restoration, promotion of zero- and low-emission mobility, improvements in waste management and energy efficiency, among others. The bioeconomy and the sustainable management of forest and agricultural ecosystems are pillars of this stage.

The strategy recognizes cultural and territorial diversity, and promotes the informed participation of communities, productive sectors and local governments. The just transition approach guides implementation so that the benefits of climate action translate into social well-being, quality employment and the closing of gaps.

The mobilization of resources will be decisive. The country will direct national instruments and strategic alliances to catalyse public and private investment, strengthen capacities and accelerate transformative projects. These efforts seek to expand green markets, facilitate access to technologies and increase resilience to climate risks.

Accumulated experience confirms the importance of measuring progress and adjusting implementation transparently. The NDC consolidates monitoring, reporting and verification mechanisms, and promotes continuous learning to sustain low-emission development with high social well-being, with verifiable results in the period 2025–2035.

1 National Circumstances

1.1 Geographic profile

Area

  • 51.100 km² of continental territory
  • 589.682 km² of territorial sea and exclusive economic zone

It presents a remarkable physiographic diversity: from coastal plains to volcanic mountain ranges and mountainous areas exceeding 3.800 metres above sea level. This geographic configuration, although beneficial from an ecological and tourism standpoint, exposes the country to climate hazards such as floods, droughts and landslides, which reinforces the need for comprehensive territorial planning with a risk and sustainability approach (IGN, 2022; FAO & CATIE, 2000).

Administratively, the country is divided into 7 provinces, 84 cantons and 492 districts (IGN, 2022). It borders Nicaragua to the north, the Caribbean Sea to the east, Panama to the southeast, and the Pacific Ocean to the west and south. It shares its maritime boundaries with Nicaragua, Ecuador, Colombia and Panama.

1.2 Sociodemographic profile

Population

  • 5 163 021 people according to the 2021 census
  • 2 600 654 men
  • 2 562 367 women
  • Density of 103 inhabitants per km² (INEC,2022)

Urban distribution

73 % of households and 72 % of the population reside in urban areas, with particular concentration in the Greater Metropolitan Area (GAM), which covers 3,4 % of the national territory and is home to about 55 % of the total population.

This demographic profile is especially relevant to the design and implementation of climate action: sustainable urban planning, metropolitan electric mobility, energy efficiency programmes in housing, warning systems for vulnerable populations and participatory strategies localized in the GAM are essential for effective climate governance.

1.3 Economic profile

The country's economic evolution has been marked by a significant transition: from a structure historically based on traditional agriculture (coffee, bananas, sugar), towards sectors such as tourism, the medical industry, electronics, and business and technology services. Its economy is defined as upper-middle income, with a productive model oriented towards services and advanced manufacturing. (BCCR, 2025; OCDE, 2025).

The World Bank has classified it as a high-income economy (Arrieta, 2025), with a system in a state of transition, as shown by its variability in the Gini index. Productive structure based on services (73 % of GDP), with contributions from the tourism, education, health and financial services sectors. Industry represents between 19 and 20 % of GDP, notably advanced manufacturing of medical devices, electronic products and pharmaceuticals, which are today among the main drivers of exports. Agriculture contributes between 5 and 6 % of GDP and plays a fundamental role in exports, with products such as coffee, bananas, pineapple and sugar (Banco Central de Costa Rica, 2025). GDP performance has been mixed; after a contraction of 4.1 % in 2020 as a result of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the Costa Rican economy experienced a strong recovery in 2021 with growth of 7.6 %, driven by exports and the reopening of key sectors (MIDEPLAN, 2022). The country has maintained a growth rate above 5 % per year, although a slight slowdown towards 3.8 % is projected for 2025, due to reduced external demand and new tariff pressures (OCDE, 2025).

Sectors such as agriculture and tourism face direct risks from extreme events and alterations in climate patterns, and areas such as clean technology, bioeconomy and electric mobility open pathways for a sustainable economic transformation. The penetration of renewable energy in the electricity mix positions the country as a benchmark in decarbonization (Banco Mundial, 2025; Banco Central de Costa Rica, 2025).

1.4 Climate profile

Located between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, it has a tropical climate influenced by its topography, altitude, trade winds and the interaction with the Caribbean and Pacific oceans; a combination that generates a remarkable diversity of microclimates in a small territory (Gobierno de Costa Rica, 2024).

The country is divided into two climatic slopes:

  • Pacific: seasonal regime with a dry season from December to March, a rainy season from May to October, with transitions in April and November.
  • Caribbean: wetter and less seasonal, with dry periods from February to March and from September to October (Instituto Meteorológico Nacional, 2008).

The National Meteorological Institute defines seven climatic regions: North Pacific, Central Pacific, South Pacific, Central Region, Northern Zone, North Caribbean and South Caribbean. Temperatures range between 27 °C and 32 °C in coastal areas, and can drop below 10 °C in high areas (Instituto Meteorológico Nacional, 2008).

The climate profile is conditioned by various meteorological phenomena such as cold fronts, trade winds and tropical cyclones; the Intertropical Convergence Zone, the Madden-Julian Oscillation and El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENOS). El Niño causes droughts in the Pacific; La Niña intensifies rainfall in the Caribbean and the Northern Zone (Alvarado Gamboa, 2021) , (Quesada-Hidalgo y Alfaro, 2024).

1.5 Climate governance

It operates under a multilevel approach and integrates policy stewardship and technical support: the Climate Change Directorate coordinates the policy and represents it before the UNFCCC; the IMN provides science and inventories; SEPLASA ensures coherence with sectoral planning; and MIDEPLAN mainstreams the agenda in the National Strategic Plan towards 2050. This arrangement is articulated in the National Climate Change System and is complemented by the Alliance for Climate Action for the link with the private sector; the mechanism aligns national targets with territorial capacities and social participation, creating the channels where decisions are generated and validated, combining:

I. Participation and validation

II. Technical consolidation and alignment

III. MRV and monitoring in SINAMECC for annual traceability and indicators

IV. Compliance with international guidance

a. ICTU (Decision 4/CMA.1) for mitigation

b. Adaptation Communication (Decision 9/CMA.1)

The process is updated every five years in connection with the Global Stocktake and registration with the UNFCCC; in this way, national decisions are based on evidence and participation, and international ones are formalized and reported in accordance with the agreed standards.

1.6 Climate action and Human Rights

The NDC, aligned with the motto of the 2030 Agenda “leave no one behind”, seeks the inclusion of all of the country's populations. It highlights the efforts and progress in participation, organization, advocacy and joint action in favour of human rights in climate action in four fundamental areas: just transition, gender equality, the strengthening of youth and the contributions of Indigenous peoples.

1.6.1 Just Transition

NDC 2020: defined specific contributions on just transition:

I. National Ambition Cycle as an iterative and inclusive process to monitor and update targets with social justice and just transition

II. Diagnosis of green and blue jobs

III. Just transition governance scheme led by MINAE, MDHIS and MTSS with tripartite social dialogue and participation of women and youth

IV. Just Transition Strategy, accompanied by a National Green Jobs Policy

The National Development and Public Investment Plan structures policy packages that include citizen acceptance and just transition as a condition for transformational change (MINAE-DCC, 2019). The First Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) reinforces this linkage.

NDC 2025- 2035: it is framed within a vision of just transition, social justice and human rights that understands decarbonization and adaptation as vectors of equity and well-being. This perspective builds on the NDC 2020 and reaffirms that no one should be left behind. The transformation towards a decarbonized and resilient economy must improve the well-being of people and ecosystems, with a social and climate justice approach; it positions decarbonization as a way to close social and territorial gaps and maximize collective well-being, articulating climate policy with social inclusion, circular economy and bioeconomy.

1.6.2 Gender equality

Costa Rica incorporates gender equality, intersectionality and human rights into its climate action; an orientation based on Decision 3/CP.25, which adopts the Lima Work Programme on Gender and its action plans, as well as on Articles 7.5, 11 and 12 of the Paris Agreement and on General Recommendation No. 37 of the CEDAW Committee, on disaster risk reduction from a gender perspective (CEDAW, 2018), and General Recommendation No.46 of the CEDAW Committee on the 8th periodic report of Costa Rica, which states “a) Include a gender perspective in national policies and action plans on climate change and disaster risk reduction and ensure the meaningful participation of women in the formulation and implementation of those policies;” (CEDAW, 2023).

It articulates this agenda through the National Action Plan on Gender Equality in Climate Action of Costa Rica (PGCC), declared to be of public interest. It gives concrete form to the 2020 nationally determined commitment, establishing a bipartite MINAE–INAMU governance to coordinate, monitor and report progress, and involves sectoral lead agencies and local governments. Its validity and implementation, aligned with the five-year cycles of the NDC, are based on the updating of actions and targets contained in five strategic axes:

1. Affirmative actions for equality and empowerment

2. Capacity building and innovation

3. Employability and strengthening of economic autonomy

4. Climate risk management

5. Production of information on the situation of women in their diversity in relation to the environment

It is conceived as a tool to reduce barriers and enhance differentiated opportunities for women in their diversity in climate action, under the principles of gender equality and non-discrimination, intersectionality, human rights, sustainable development, climate justice, just transition, and participation and inclusion (INAMU et al., 2023).

1.6.3 Youth

The experience and commitments established in the NDC 2020 are coherently integrated, incorporating an intersectoral perspective that recognizes the fundamental role of youth, based on the formal integration of the technical proposals presented at the National Youth Forum on Climate Action 2024, consolidated as a key space for the active and constructive participation of youth in national climate policy.

Youth is recognized as an essential driver in promoting and implementing climate actions, contributing inclusive and multisectoral approaches; rights-based approaches that enrich the design of commitments and strategies focused on mitigation, adaptation, resilience and climate governance. Youth leadership is recognized as a dynamizing and indispensable component of climate policy, facilitating the incorporation of intergenerational perspectives in the planning and execution of measures, guaranteeing the continuity and progression of national actions, and strengthening the legitimacy and effectiveness of the current NDC.

1.6.4 Indigenous peoples

In light of the update of the NDC 2025-2035, dialogue arose among the different territories that made it possible to validate the actions agreed upon in the 22 Environmental, Forestry and Territorial Plans (PAFT) in force and under implementation, with the purpose of making visible, reinforcing and recording the contributions of Indigenous peoples to national and global climate action; these arise from the sustained work of Indigenous peoples in favour of the conservation of nature, their worldview and life resources, together with the team of male and female territorial leaders, who facilitated dialogue and the validation of contributions through consensus, political advocacy and deliberate efforts to achieve institutional support commitments.

Within the framework of the National REDD+ Strategy, the Indigenous peoples of Costa Rica have carried out an extensive participatory consultation process, in accordance with the regulatory mandates that guarantee the positioning of the voices, knowledge and practices of the Indigenous population. The National Directorate for Alternative Dispute Resolution of the Ministry of Justice and Peace, in charge of the administration and execution of the General Indigenous Consultation Mechanism, confirms that “the process carried out by the REDD+ Secretariat together with the Indigenous territories since 2008 has complied with the standards of the General Mechanism for Consultation with Indigenous Peoples, having been carried out with broad participation of Indigenous peoples in a free, prior and informed manner, through appropriate procedures and through their representative institutions”. As the REDD+ process was reflected in the previous Nationally Determined Contribution, NDC 2020, and as the PAFT are the basis of the current dialogue for this NDC 2025-2035, alignment between these public policy instruments is ensured.

2 Second Nationally Determined Contribution

Costa Rica's Nationally Determined Contribution for the period 2025–2035 was developed through a comprehensive technical and participatory process, built on the headline targets for mitigation and adaptation; the analysis of reports such as the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas Emissions by Sources and Removals by Sinks 1990–2021, the First Biennial Transparency Report, the Fourth National Communication, the National Decarbonization Plan 2018–2050 and the National Adaptation Policy and its respective Plan 2022–2026; guided by the principles of progression, environmental integrity, transparency and the commitment to leave no one behind, integrating human rights, gender equality and intersectionality in accordance with international guidelines and the commitments assumed by the country.

2.1 Procedure

The two relevant CMA guidance documents were applied in full: Decision 4/CMA.1, concerning the information necessary for clarity, transparency and understanding (ICTU) of mitigation contributions, and Decision 9/CMA.1, which guides the adaptation communication. The document makes explicit the assumptions, scope, periods and accounting arrangements for mitigation in accordance with 4/CMA.1, and incorporates, in accordance with 9/CMA.1, the needs, approaches, actions, monitoring arrangements and means of implementation in adaptation, using the NDC as the communication vehicle, ensuring consistency with current international guidelines, updates to the regulatory framework (7(a)–(b) Contribution to the UNFCCC and the PA) It contributes to Art. 2 of the UNFCCC and to Arts. 2.1(a) and 4.1 of the Paris Agreement, aligning the trajectory with 1,5 °C, peaking and rapid declines, and coherence with neutrality by mid-century.

In adaptation, the state of the art was updated based on the Fourth National Communication, the BTR and the National Adaptation Plan 2022–

2026. The measures were evaluated for their contribution to the Global Goal on Adaptation, their potential to reduce vulnerability and their capacity to strengthen resilience to the effects of climate change.

The analysis made it possible to:

1. Identify measures already approved or under implementation that are suitable for scaling up.

2. Verify their institutional and regulatory feasibility.

3. Ensure their territorial alignment.

4. Consolidate enabling conditions such as just transition, effective participation, the use of disaggregated data, affirmative actions and social safeguards, in coherence with Articles 7.5, 11 and 12 of the Paris Agreement and Decision 3/CP.25 on gender.

Critical gaps were identified and a technical proposal of climate mitigation and adaptation actions was formulated, which were evaluated technically and financially using the CLEW-CR-3.0 model, estimating their aggregate impact, ensuring that the new NDC is viable, coherent with public policies and capable of accelerating the transition towards a low-carbon and climate-resilient economy.

2.2 Participation

The methodological design combined consultation workshops with sectoral, territorial and thematic approaches to ensure national coverage and multisectoral representativeness, guaranteeing the inclusion of actors at both the national and regional levels, and ensuring the traceability of inputs throughout the entire process.

  • Validation workshops for the Mitigation approach and for the climate change Adaptation approach, held in person, with broad participation of sectoral technical entities and representatives of the territories that had taken part in the initial regional phase. Finally, the process is complemented by sessions presenting progress on the NDC and dialogue among senior officials, strategic representatives of the public, private and financial sectors, multilateral organizations and climate change governance mechanisms, in order to reaffirm commitments and thus consolidate the NDC 2025- 2035 as a shared, implementation-oriented climate action agenda.

3 Mitigation

3.1 Mitigation objectives

Costa Rica contributes approximately 0.03%1 of global emissions, a relatively low percentage in the global context; however, it is important to address it for the well-being of our population and the commitments made on decarbonization, with the reduction of greenhouse gases as a strategic axis of climate policy. The mitigation efforts integrated into development are maintained, and the aim is even to increase the pace, magnitude and breadth of the country's emission reductions.

Mitigation takes place under the approaches of just transition, gender equality and human rights, incorporating social safeguards and effective participation; this commitment guides a vision of sustainable, competitive development, social well-being and environmental resilience, in accordance with the global climate change mitigation and adaptation goals.

The country's emissions are estimated at 16157,62 Gg of CO2e for the year 2021; 50% of these are generated in the energy sector, of which 37,6% of this sector comes specifically from the transport sector, arising from the use of fossil hydrocarbons. Other main emission sources and barriers limiting mitigation or reduction are identified, and the corresponding mitigation measures are generated, which operate in synergy to achieve the proposed objectives.

The inertia of ongoing projects is not enough; regulatory enablers, institutional coordination and disciplined execution by sector are required, with transparent monitoring to avoid setbacks.

The key element for moving from the plateau to a sustained decline is financing, where carbon credits play a decisive role from several perspectives:

  • They act as a price signal that makes high-impact projects bankable.
  • They mobilize private capital and cooperation when there is environmental integrity and traceability.
  • They link verifiable results with performance-based payments, accelerating the priority portfolio.

In terms of management, the operational priority is to concentrate efforts on the set of contributions with the highest marginal return, reduce bottlenecks and ensure that financial flows (including those from carbon markets) arrive on time. Communicating this state of affairs clearly and transparently facilitates political and social support. Meeting the target is possible, but only if the full portfolio is implemented, critical projects are protected and the integrity of every tonne reduced or removed is ensured.

3.3 Mitigation headline target

Costa Rica commits to:

  • Absolute maximum net emissions by 2030 11 380 Gg of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e)
  • Maximum net emissions budget for the period 2025 to 2035 132 700 Gg of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e)

Including all emissions and sectors covered by Costa Rica's National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas Emissions by Sources and Removals by Sinks, 1990-2021.

The emissions target is an exercise in methodological translation; it is aligned with the latest inventory, which adds new emission categories that were previously not accounted for in the country2. Carbon neutrality is a priority in climate change matters, and it is expressed with the same rules and units as the INGEI 1990-2021.

The headline target of the Nationally Determined Contribution is equivalent to a reduction of approximately 53% of carbon dioxide equivalent emissions by 2030 relative to the scenario without additional measures. Even considering only the contributions assumed unconditionally, the reduction would reach about 23% compared with the same scenario. This is a highly ambitious effort that demonstrates Costa Rica's commitment to the environment.

The structure of the contributions proposed and applied in the modelling made it possible to establish the interaction among the sectors analysed, given that, although a contribution belongs to a specific sector, its application does not extend only to that sector; for example, the energy sector interacts directly with industry, with agriculture, with waste; which allows us to establish that this NDC generates co-benefits in various areas, such as the efficiency and renewables scenarios in industry; construction with resilience criteria; water recharge and efficiency in aqueducts; lower energy consumption, resilient agriculture, which also increases soil organic carbon, urban nature-based solutions for heat islands and restoration of mangroves and marine-coastal areas that support blue carbon initiatives, among the most relevant. All these contributions, through their interaction and synergy, strengthen the scenario for complying with the National Carbon Policy, guiding Costa Rica on the path to net zero emissions by 2050.

3.4 Modelling of decarbonization pathways

A multisectoral, economy-wide technical modelling process was applied, aligned with the global 1.5 °C trajectory defined in the 2020 update of the headline target, based on the CLEW analytical framework (Cimate, Land, Energy and Water), with an extension of the traditional CLEW approach to systemically integrate all emitting sectors and sinks covered by the IPCC, including the four major IPCC categories: Energy, Agriculture, FOLU, Waste and Industrial Processes and Product Use. Thus giving rise to the model: CLEW-CR-3.0.

3.5 National contributions for meeting the Mitigation headline target

The classification of climate actions according to the IPCC sectors is presented: Energy, Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU), Agriculture (AG), Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (FOLU), Waste and Oceans. It incorporates principles of just transition and territorial equity, promotes the valuation of natural capital as an axis of sustainable development, and facilitates the integration of multisectoral and inclusive approaches, in line with long-term international commitments on human rights, gender and climate justice. Within the framework of SINAMECC, it adopts the nomenclatures (TJ) to identify Just Transition measures and Oceans (O). These measures do not constitute IPCC source categories; they function as the generation of enabling conditions that support reductions in the sectors.

4 Second National Communication on Adaptation and Contribution to Adaptation

4.1 Introduction

In compliance with Article 7 of the Paris Agreement, and the provisions of Decision 9/CMA.1, the country has proceeded to update its adaptation communication based on the priorities established from the different needs to reduce vulnerability to the effects of climate change. It presents the efforts the country has made in developing policies, plans, actions and measures to adapt to the effects of climate variability and change, which already tangibly affect the country's ecosystems, economy and communities.

Costa Rica submits this NDC as a vehicle for the adaptation communication in accordance with Decision 9/CMA.1. The communication will be updated before each Global Stocktake (every five years; next update in 2028) and will be submitted to the UNFCCC public registry of adaptation communications. The monitoring and evaluation framework is integrated into SINAMECC and includes indicators with a 2024 baseline, targets for 2030 and 2035, annual frequency and defined sectoral responsible parties, covering three dimensions: institutional capacities, vulnerability reduction and resilience. The Ministry of Environment and Energy, through the Climate Change Directorate, will coordinate data collection and validation; sectoral institutions will report annually to SINAMECC and a five-yearly synthesis will be published prior to each Global Stocktake.

Considering national circumstances, the adaptation contributions and targets for this Second Adaptation Communication were defined in a participatory, decentralized manner through consultation processes with all public and private socio-economic and environmental sectors, civil society, organizations, international cooperation and Indigenous communities, taking as a basis the country's status according to the first BTR, which includes the state of adaptation, as well as the follow-up of other public policy instruments in this area.

To achieve the adaptation headline target, the established contributions and targets are linked to national policies, plans, projects and actions, among which the National Climate Change Adaptation Policy (PNACC) and its Adaptation Plan, the National Risk Management Policy and its Plan, adaptation strategies of different sectors, private sector efforts and international commitments can be mentioned.

The contributions and their adaptation targets defined in the AdCOM are aligned, showing coherence between local/national adaptation actions and the indicators of the Global Goal defined in the UNFCCC.

4.2 Institutional arrangements and legal frameworks on Adaptation

Costa Rica's trajectory in climate adaptation began in the 2000s, with the formalization of the National Climate Change Strategy, composed of six strategic components, the main ones being mitigation and adaptation, with cross-cutting axes on metrics, capacity development, technology transfer, public awareness, education and cultural change, and financing. This instrument has enabled the country to evolve with the development of a technical-political framework, developing the National Climate Change Adaptation Policy (PNACC 2018-2030); it establishes lines of action in tourism, water resources, biodiversity and forests, agriculture and fisheries, health, infrastructure and energy, and instructs all public entities to incorporate adaptation within their competencies. Its operationalization materializes through the National Adaptation Plan 2022-2026, which details pathways, targets and indicators for each sector and territorial level, thus reinforcing coherence between the PNACC and the Nationally Determined Contribution submitted in 2020.

The PNACC 2018-2030 is the governing framework that guides the country's actions on adaptation, seeking a resilient development model that avoids human losses and moderates material damage, improves the quality of life of vulnerable populations and takes advantage of opportunities to innovate and transform sectors. The PNACC defines six main axes of action (MINAE-DCC, 2022) :

Instrumental Axes

  • Axis 1. Knowledge management on the effects of climate change, climate services and development of local and institutional capacities.
  • Axis 2. Fostering conditions for the resilience of human and natural systems through territorial, marine and coastal planning.
  • Axis 6. Investment and financial security for climate action.

Substantive Axes

  • Axis 3. Management of biodiversity, ecosystems, watersheds and marine and coastal spaces for adaptation.
  • Axis 4. Protection of services and infrastructure.
  • Axis 5. Adapted and eco-competitive production systems.

The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) 2022-2026 is the first action plan of the PNACC; it details specific actions and indicators to achieve its objectives. Its development process (2020-2022) was participatory, involving various sectors and organizations, and was based on UNFCCC guidelines, complemented by methodologies to manage uncertainty and integrate priorities (MINAE-DCC, 2022) .

The National Risk Management Policy 2016-2030, as the basis for integration between risk management and adaptation, points to the need to articulate both fields in the sectoral and territorial planning of national development through its respective Action Plan, as well as the application of the Global Risk Assessment Framework, to strengthen governance with a systemic approach and improve the country approach to reducing risks.

Other public policy instruments, such as the National Development Plans, the Costa Rica 2050 National Strategic Plan, the National Health Policy, the National Biodiversity Policy 2015-2030, the National Sustainable Production and Consumption Policy 2018-2030 and the National Circular Economy Strategy. The country has shown substantial progress in handling cross-cutting issues within climate change action, including aspects of gender and citizen participation. 4.2.1 Impacts, vulnerabilities and risks

It maintains an adaptation approach grounded in integrated risk management, where, under the National Emergencies and Risk Prevention Law No. 8488, it is perceived as a preventive and sustainable process that seeks to reverse the vulnerabilities of the population, settlements and productive activities, incorporating prevention, response and recovery actions in the face of impacts. Under this approach, national climate policy prioritizes creating the conditions that strengthen the adaptive capacity of social, economic and environmental systems and thereby reduce their exposure and vulnerability to hazards exacerbated by climate change.

4.2.2 Current climate risk

The analyses carried out by the IMN in 2024 show that extreme hydro-meteorological events, both wet and dry, generate the greatest impacts on the country, manifested in floods and droughts that cause significant socio-economic losses, especially in territories with a high concentration of population and critical services.

According to Retana (2012), the three ocean-atmospheric phenomena that cause the greatest rainfall impact in the country are low-pressure systems, the impact of tropical cyclones in the Caribbean and cold fronts, with consequences ranging from floods to soil and structural erosion due to dragging or water friction. The data analysis for the period 1980-2010 indicates that the increase in disasters related to heavy rainfall is evident, correlated with the rise in extreme hydrometeorological events, which have evidently responded to large-magnitude climate variability phenomena, added to an increase in social vulnerabilities that amplify disaster scenarios. The connection of this trend with climate change cannot currently be demonstrated, due to the period of the data record used in the study, so an update is necessary to know the trend of this type of event as a consequence of the effects of climate change.

Dry events that generate droughts have been determined through studies of the temporal distribution of reported impacts; it is shown that many of these are linked to the effect of ENSO in periods when it had a particularly strong intensity. Contrary to the above, there are years in which no drought impacts are detected; this is due to periods with neutral conditions, wetter than normal or under the effect of La Niña. The impacts are varied; the most affected sectors are always agriculture and livestock: crop losses mainly in basic grains (rice, beans and maize), death of livestock associated with lack of pasture or lack of water. To these impacts must be added all those associated with the consumption of water resources for the different activities carried out by the population, especially rationing or shortages of the resource for several days (Quesada, Hidalgo y Alfaro, 2020).

The IPCC Sixth Report indicates that, in the Central American region since the mid-20th century, extremely hot temperatures have increased and extremely cold temperatures have decreased in the region (medium confidence), while the magnitude and frequency of extreme precipitation events have increased, and droughts show mixed signals (low confidence). This report points out that significant increases in the rates of tropical cyclone intensification have been observed in the Atlantic basin. According to Khouakhi et al., 2017 cited by IPCC (2022), they contributed approximately 10 % of annual precipitation; during the cyclone season, the largest number of extreme sea level events exceed a 10-year return period (Asociación Costa Rica por Siempre, 2022).

4.2.3 Future climate risk

The regionalized climate change scenario projections updated to 2021, using the PRECIS regional model, are projected for the RCP 2.6 (low emissions) and RCP 8.5 (high emissions) pathways, evaluated in the short, medium and long term. The scenarios indicate that the country will experience robust warming in all scenarios.

Short Term 2010-2039. The RCP8.5 scenario refers to an increase of 1.1°C to 1.6°C. This warming will be more pronounced in the Caribbean and the Northern Zone, and less on the Pacific slope (Alvarado Gamboa, 2021). and a warming “corridor” running along the Talamanca mountain range, the Central Valley and ending in the lower Tempisque.

Medium Term 2040-2069. Under the same scenario, increases of between 2.4°C and 2.8°C are forecast, where the Talamanca mountain range, the Central Volcanic range and the Northern Zone will be the most affected, while the North and Central Pacific will feel the impact less.

Long Term 2070-2099. With respect to the RCP2.6 scenario, an increase of 1°C to 2°C is expected compared to the current climate; in this scenario it is foreseen that the Caribbean and Northern Zone regions will experience slightly higher thermal increases than the Pacific Slope.

National projections for precipitation under RCP 2.6 show a contrasting pattern where the North Caribbean and the Northern Zone will have greater precipitation, while the South Caribbean, the Central Valley and high areas of the Central Pacific could experience a precipitation deficit. Under RCP 8.5 the first half of the century points to a marked deficit in areas such as Osa-Golfito, going from 6000 - 7000 mm to 4000 - 5000 mm, together with widespread decreases in the dry season; however, towards the end of the century recoveries or even increases are projected over the Central Mountain Range and the Caribbean slope, while the decline persists in areas of the Central and North Pacific.

In terms of wind speed, a slight, almost generalized increase in mean speed is foreseen under RCP 2.6 until 2070, and it remains practically stable in the traditionally windiest regions (North Pacific and mountain ranges) throughout the century. Under RCP 8.5, mean wind speed weakens in the first two periods with decreases < 1 m/s, but towards 2070-2099 speeds increase in the North Caribbean and the Northern Zone, while the rest of the country maintains insignificant decreases; the canícula (July-August) of the North Pacific becomes windier with peaks of up to 4.5 m/s (Alvarado Gamboa, 2021) . 4.3.1 History of hydrometeorological disasters

The country has a registration module called Losses Caused by Natural Phenomena developed by CNE and MIDEPLAN. National records are downloaded into the Disaster Inventory System - Desinventar https://db.desinventar. org/, an active inventory with data from 1970 to the present.

Orozco Montoya, Alice Brenes-Maykall, Rebeca Sura-Fonseca (2022), based on a historical analysis of disasters for the period 1970-2000, out of a total of 19016 Desinventar record sheets, determined that 80% of them were related to disasters from hydrometeorological events; according to the list, the events correspond to flooding, rains, landslides caused by atmospheric conditions, flash floods, storms, gales, drought and storm surges. This study reveals that hydrometeorological events represent the second cause of death, the first cause being rip currents. According to the database records, extreme rainfall events are those that record the highest number of deaths (221 from landslides, 129 from floods and 81 from flash floods).

Maldonado et al. (2018) and Taylor and Alfaro (2005) and Retana (2012) assert that these events are the ones that most affect the country and the Central American region, related to the occurrence of low-pressure systems and the indirect effect of Caribbean hurricanes, where the former can occur from January to December, with April to November being the period of greatest probability of occurrence, and tropical hurricanes are concentrated between June and November, with September to November being when their probability of occurrence is greatest.

4.3.3 Non-economic losses from disasters

The CNE's damage and loss records do not reflect the indirect non -economic costs involved in the loss of human lives, the disturbances and interruptions in the daily life of the population (suspension of classes in public schools, for example) caused by these events. Additionally, they cause damage to ecosystems, with impacts on wildlife and livestock herds, and the associated losses (direct and indirect) are not measured systematically, which suggests an under-recording of the impact of hydrometeorological events in the country (MINAE, 2018). The country needs to advance in the application of methodologies for the development of non-economic assessments for events that generate disasters, including psychosocial damage, as well as physical and mental health, which deepen existing inequalities and generate new social problems.

Due to the country's need to address infrastructure works at imminent risk and in emergency, the country has developed the Emergency Program for the Comprehensive and Resilient Reconstruction of Infrastructure, for the restoration of resilient and sustainable infrastructure projects of 3 General Emergency Plans due to the effects generated by the accumulation of rains caused by tropical waves number 11, 12 and Tropical Storm Bonnie, due to the effects generated in the territory by the accumulation of rains caused by the direct influence of the Intertropical Convergence Zone over Costa Rica, and the national state of emergency due to the effects generated by the accumulation of rains in the territory caused by the indirect influence of Hurricane Julia (CABEI, 2024).

The United Nations Global Assessment Report on Disaster Risk Reduction estimates that the Expected Annual Losses for Costa Rica due to multiple hazards (floods, cyclonic winds, storm surges, tsunamis and earthquakes) will reach 280 million USD$ annually (UNDRR, 2015).

It is estimated that by 2030 losses could exceed 7000 million USD$, and by 2050 they could reach nearly 30000 million USD$. 4.4.2 Investment and financial security for risk management

Costa Rica was the first country to obtain approval from the World Bank of a Disaster Risk Management Development Policy Loan with a Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option in 2023 for an amount of 160 million USD$; of this, the first was approved in 2008 for an amount of 95 million USD$, which was closed 9 years later (2017) after two renewals, and which offered an important financial starting point for addressing disasters such as the Cinchona earthquake (2009), Tropical Storm Nicole (2010) and Hurricane Otto (2016) (World Bank, 2024).

It was the first country in the world to harmonize its National Risk Management Policy and its implementation plan with the agenda endorsed globally to reduce risks, mainly the number of disaster deaths. Despite these efforts to ensure governance processes for risk management and its mitigation, disaster scenarios, economic loss figures, the quantification of human losses and the interruption of the continuity of services -drinking water; education; decent housing; health and sanitation; electricity; mobility, continue to grow.

The National Emergency Fund is created by the National Emergencies and Risk Prevention Law No. 8488, to address and confront emergency situations and for prevention and mitigation. It shall consist of the following resources:

a. The inputs, contributions, donations and transfers from natural or legal persons, national or international, state or non-governmental.

b. The transfer from all institutions of the Central Administration, the Decentralized Public Administration and public enterprises, which shall transfer to the Commission three percent (3%) of profits and of the accumulated, free and total budget surplus.

c. The items allocated in the ordinary and extraordinary budgets of the Republic.

Axis 6 of the National Climate Change Adaptation Policy promotes the mobilization of financial resources, both public and private, to implement adaptation measures and reduce loss and damage in vulnerable populations. Its guideline 6.4 calls for incorporating adaptation and vulnerability reduction criteria into post-disaster reconstruction and recovery processes (MINAE-DCC, 2022) .

In 2024 the World Bank approved a loan of 350 million USD$ and 20 million USD$ in concessional financing from the Global Concessional Financing Facility, a multilateral partnership established to help with the financial needs of middle-income countries that host large numbers of refugees, for the development of the Climate-Resilient Recovery and Territorial Development Project. It provides financing for the reconstruction of grey and green infrastructure damaged in recent disasters, and builds and equips multipurpose emergency shelters and disaster response facilities. It finances investments to improve early warning systems and services at the local level, in several vulnerable river basins, strengthening Costa Rica's capacity to manage disaster risks; it seeks to benefit two million people, almost 40 % of the country's population. Implementation of the project is the responsibility of the National Commission for Risk Prevention and Emergency Response. It contains an inclusion component, as it benefits people living in cantons where infrastructure needs already existed and which receive an enormous number of refugees and migrants (World Bank, 2022).

Costa Rica is part of the Global Shield against Climate Risks programme, whose objective is to strengthen the financial protection and resilience of vulnerable countries and people against disasters and risks of loss or damage related to climate change, through more and better disaster financing options.

4.5 National adaptation priorities, strategies, policies, plans, targets and actions

4.5.1 National adaptation priorities

Maintaining the vision of the 2020 NDC, the climate change adaptation priorities in Costa Rica, aligned with the National Climate Change Adaptation Policy 2018-2030 and the National Adaptation Plan 2022-2026; Costa Rica is committed to managing knowledge about impacts, with the strengthening of the conditions of social, economic and environmental resilience and of natural systems to the effects of climate change, through the development of capacities and information for decision-making, the inclusion of adaptation criteria in financing instruments and territorial and ecosystem planning; adaptation of public services, production systems and infrastructure, as well as the implementation of nature-based solutions, for climate action.

The PNACC is set out as the guiding framework that will direct the country's actions as part of a set of strategic planning instruments. The competent institutions must establish their commitments, in terms of actions and targets to be achieved, which must subsequently form part of the institutional, financial and operational planning of each of them.

Climate change implies the deterioration of water resource quality, a reduction in water availability due to increased evaporation to the atmosphere, and an increase in demand as a result of people's needs both for consumption and for hydroelectric energy production and agricultural systems (MINAE-DCC 2021). The PNACC has placed water as a determining, cross-cutting and priority management axis, so that actions can be linked to the appropriate management of responsibilities regarding the installation of capacities in the public and private sectors, as well as to the building of resilience conditions in communities (MINAE, 2018).

The agricultural sector is the most affected by climate change, since it depends entirely on natural resources; rising temperatures have negative effects on on production yields and can enhance pests and diseases. These damages affect food production and, at the same time, cause a decrease in the availability of basic food products, which affects the country's food security. (MINAE-IMN, 2021).

4.5.2 Adaptation policies, strategies, plans, targets and actions

Costa Rica has demonstrated a continuous and ambitious commitment to climate action. The country began working on climate change mitigation and adaptation in the seventies, with the creation of Protected Wild Areas, forestry incentives and environmental sanitation legislation. In 2000 the issue of climate change began to be discussed within the environmental sector; in 2009 the National Climate Change Strategy was made official, with a mitigation component and an adaptation component. The country's ratification of the Paris Agreement facilitated the articulation of Costa Rica's national adaptation commitments mainly through the National Climate Change Adaptation Policy 2018-2030 and its National Adaptation Plan 2022-2026, as well as the 2020 Nationally Determined Contribution (Government of Costa Rica, 2021a).

In 2025, as an integral part of the Country Programme for Climate Leadership, the Country Programme for Climate Adaptation has been strengthened; it has two certification modalities:

  • Adaptation recognizes organizations that have integrated climate risk management into their operations through tools such as vulnerability assessments, adaptation plans and concrete measures in the face of climate change.
  • Adaptation Plus+ recognizes those organizations that, in addition to implementing actions, demonstrate systematic monitoring, continuous improvement and the generation of positive impacts on their ecosystems and communities.

Both versions of recognition will be formalized by public policy regulating their governance, ensuring alignment with the National Adaptation Plan, the Sustainable Development Goals and the NDC itself. This programme is consolidated as a robust platform to foster climate resilience in all sectors of the country, facilitating multi-stakeholder involvement and promoting national leadership in the face of current and future climate challenges.

4.6 Adaptation headline target

Costa Rica commits to:

  • Strengthen the social, economic and environmental resilience of the country in the face of climate variability and change, through capacity creation and development, the establishment of reliable information models for decision-making, and the incorporation of adaptation and risk management criteria into the planning and financing instruments of public services, production systems and infrastructure.

Costa Rica maintains its climate change adaptation headline target, reaffirming the commitment established in the 2020 NDC and validated in the First Biennial Transparency Report. This update of the contributions to meet this target is based on the progress, challenges and opportunities identified, as well as on the analysis of public policies, international commitments and territorial and sectoral needs, in full coherence with the NAP.

5 Climate finance for adaptation and mitigation in Costa Rica

The Sustainable Finance Taxonomy becomes a strategic instrument; the country has a standardized technical framework that defines economic activities considered environmentally sustainable. This classification system facilitates the identification of investments aligned with national mitigation and adaptation objectives, provides transparency, comparability and credibility to the market, reducing the risk of greenwashing and improving interoperability with other international taxonomies. It covers sectors such as energy, construction, transport, manufacturing, waste management, water, information and communication technologies; as well as land uses, including agriculture, livestock and forestry, with specific criteria to contribute substantially to climate and environmental objectives (MINAE et al., 2024) .

Climate action requires interconnected financing mechanisms that combine the mobilization of public, private and multilateral resources. At the State level, green, sustainable and blue bonds, together with funds from international mechanisms such as the Green Climate Fund (MINAE-DCC, 2025) and the Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (PNUD, 2023) , can channel resources towards large-scale projects. The magnitude of the investment gap makes the participation of the private financial sector indispensable, through sustainable investment funds, green loans and structured financing schemes that favour resilient and low-carbon infrastructure.

Mixed financing or blended finance is a particularly promising avenue; it combines public and private resources to reduce the risk perceived by investors, and is essential for attracting capital to strategic adaptation and mitigation sectors (MINAE et al., 2024) . It explores the opportunities for implementing Art 6 of the Paris Agreement and the different mechanisms for mobilizing and managing funds, to accelerate the achievement of the targets that make up the contributions set out above, in accordance with the provisions determined by the country's governing body on climate change.

5.3 Article 6, Carbon Markets of the Paris Agreement

5.3.1 Cooperation Mechanisms

In the case of carbon markets, in particular the possibility of generating additional financial resources using the cooperation mechanisms provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, we must bear in mind that there is already a market regulated by the Kyoto Protocol, one of whose best-known components is the Clean Development Mechanism, which is currently in the process of transitioning to the mechanisms of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement This article basically creates two market mechanisms; which can generate international transactions of emission reductions to support the implementation of Nationally Determined Contributions:

  • Article 62, which establishes the framework for bilateral cooperation between two or more Parties to the Paris Agreement
  • Article 64 Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism

Both mechanisms can be used as sources of financing to enhance the ambition of NDCs; however, there are methodological rules and special procedures that must be followed, including the application of the principle of Corresponding Adjustments, which basically means that, if a country internationally transfers a certain quantity of emission reductions, it must deduct them from its emissions balance The advantage is the possibility of obtaining additional financial resources that can be invested in priority areas where it is difficult to generate national resources, also enabling the active participation of the private sector

5.3.2 Conditionalities

From the perspective of the availability of resources for their implementation, the actions presented in this Contribution, in general terms, can be classified as Unconditional and Conditional

Unconditional: they have duly identified domestic resources for their full implementation; resources that may come either from the public sector through various modalities – ordinary or extraordinary budgets, national or international loans specifically dedicated to them that have been previously identified as such, or other sources of international technical or financial cooperation such as bilateral or multilateral cooperation, results-based payments and any other form of voluntary cooperation that has been previously identified as such; or from the private sector through specific programmes, projects or initiatives undertaken -voluntarily or as a result of the application of mandatory regulations by the various non-public actors as part of their regular programmes for internalizing national climate targets in their activities, for example: carbon neutrality, blue flag, environmental responsibility, among others

Conditional: they require technical or financial resources that are outside the regular budget provisions of both public and private actors, that is, additional resources to achieve the defined targets; conditional on obtaining the identified additional resources The type of resources required may be of various kinds, including access to new sources of financing or cooperation, both public and private (loans, voluntary or regulated carbon markets, technical cooperation and technology transfer, capacity building)

Developing countries normally express the targets of their NDCs by distinguishing both conditional and unconditional activities, which leaves room to develop actions to seek resources to improve their performance and be more ambitious, if they manage to carry out their conditional actions

  • Gases covered in the headline target: CO2, CH4, N2O, HFCs, PFCs, SF6, NF3

c. How the Party has taken into consideration paragraph 31(c) and (d) of decision 1/CP21.

The 2006 IPCC Guidelines were used, incorporating the improvements of the 2019 Refinement, and the Global Warming Potentials of the Fifth Assessment Report, consistent with current UNFCCC guidance. The target was recalibrated to ensure its methodological consistency with the INGEI 2021, without reducing the level of ambition.

This approach ensures comparability and temporal consistency between the target and the national inventory system, and responds to the principles of transparency, accuracy, completeness, consistency and comparability established in the transparency framework of the Paris Agreement.

d. Mitigation co-benefits resulting from Parties' adaptation actions and/or economic diversification plans, including description of specific projects, measures and initiatives of Parties' adaptation actions and/or economic diversification plans.

In the context of comprehensive climate action, targets aimed at strengthening national resilience through climate information systems not only fulfil an essential function in adaptation, but also generate strategic co-benefits for mitigation. This dual approach allows investments in data infrastructure and climate monitoring to simultaneously contribute to reducing emissions and improving efficiency in decision-making since they strengthen mitigation strategies.

One of the most relevant targets is the implementation, of a national open data policy. This policy democratizes access to quality climate information, enables the development of analytical tools that make it possible to model emissions scenarios, assess the impact of low-carbon technologies and design more effective public policies on mitigation. Interoperability and standardization of formats and metadata among institutions is key to ensuring that data can be used directly in analysis and visualization platforms, such as climate risk dashboards. Complementarily, the launch of a unified national system of meteorological, hydrological and marine data represents a significant advance. This system, by guaranteeing the quality and relevance of the data, enables more precise planning in sectors such as energy, transport and agriculture, where evidence-based decisions can translate into substantial emission reductions.

The operation of a network of automatic weather stations integrated into open data systems strengthens climate surveillance and early warning systems. Beyond its value for adaptation, this infrastructure supports energy planning, especially in harnessing renewable sources such as solar and wind, whose efficiency depends on precise, real-time meteorological data.

The integration of various platforms into SINAMECC will enable better decision-making for mitigation strategies. The information exchange and project mapping module facilitates the identification of replicable initiatives, the channelling of climate finance and the adoption of low-carbon technologies. This component is key to accelerating the implementation of mitigation solutions in sectors such as energy, transport and agriculture.

The formalization and training of people in risk management and climate change in the municipalities and municipal district councils makes it possible to incorporate sustainability criteria into local planning, to promote green infrastructure solutions, energy efficiency in public buildings and sustainable mobility, contributing directly to emission reductions, with an intergenerational vision.

The creation of the Climate Resilience Laboratory for Infrastructure and Transport, together with the technical unit at the National Laboratory of Materials and Structural Models, will enable the integration of mitigation criteria into the design, construction and maintenance of infrastructure, innovation in low-footprint materials and solutions for sustainable mobility proposals.

The National Employment Policy for green and blue jobs and the business support programmes enable conditions for new economic activities to develop with low environmental impact. This includes labour reconversion towards sectors such as renewable energy, regenerative agriculture, ecotourism, waste management and sustainable mobility.

Climate education programmes and the mainstreaming of topics such as circular economy, sustainable construction and responsible consumption in formal and community education will prepare the population to implement solutions that reduce emissions in sectors such as energy, waste, transport and buildings.

At the institutional level, the updating with an adaptation approach of the POIs, the PGAIs and, at the trade-association level, of the strategic sectoral plans (transport, energy, agriculture, waste, construction, industry, oceans) will facilitate the integration of mitigation measures such as technological reconversion, operational efficiency and reduction of institutional and sectoral emissions.

The mandatory incorporation of adaptation criteria into critical infrastructure ensures that new public investments, as well as infrastructure reconstruction projects, are resilient and low-emission, aligned with standards such as the CFIA Adaptation Code.

Among the biodiversity management proposals, the creation and management of urban biological corridors stands out, which improve ecological connectivity and protect climate refuges, contributing to carbon capture, urban thermal regulation and the reduction of emissions associated with intensive energy use in cities. Additionally, the protection of State heritage, through conservation, restoration and sustainable forest management initiatives, strengthens natural carbon sinks, especially in primary and secondary forests, reducing emissions from deforestation, soil degradation and forest fires.

Similarly, the contributions proposed for the restoration of mangroves, corals and wetlands as buffer ecosystems also have an impact on carbon removal since these ecosystems are highly efficient in capturing blue carbon, contributing directly to GHG reduction.

The contributions relating to infrastructure include initiatives for the application of vulnerability and risk assessments to ensure the continuity of essential services. These assessments also make it possible to identify opportunities for incorporating more efficient and low-carbon technologies and systems, mainly in services related to transport, water, energy and waste. Business continuity protocols, not only in the public sector but also in the private sector, enhance a cleaner and more sustainable technological transformation.

For its part, the contribution aimed at promoting sustainable and climate-smart construction also facilitates the transition towards design and construction strategies that apply energy efficiency and lower environmental impact criteria, that favour the use of sustainable materials with low embodied footprint (such as wood) or products that contribute to material circularity, as well as urban regeneration with NbS, favouring mitigation outcomes.

From the contribution on agro-landscapes and sustainable production systems, the mitigation co-benefit stands out by transforming agro-productive models with regenerative and low-carbon practices. These practices reduce the use of synthetic inputs and also favour soil recarbonization. Additionally, by introducing linkage with forestry value chains they enhance removals in the productive sector while generating greater resilience to climate change.

The introduction of the resilience model in industry promotes the adoption of innovation, efficiency and circular economy systems, which is accompanied by clean technologies with lower energy consumption and lower GHG emissions. In turn, encouraging circularity practices reduces emissions from the extraction and transport of raw materials, as well as those associated with the end of life of materials.

The contribution proposed for integrating the budgetary variable into expenditure planning and execution also makes it possible to incorporate measures that contribute to mitigation such as sustainable, energy-efficient infrastructure that promotes ecological restoration. Likewise, the development of financial instruments with expenditure classifiers facilitates the channelling of resources towards investments in clean energy, low-carbon transport and the protection or restoration of natural sinks.

Finally, the contributions proposed to financially strengthen conservation strategies, such as the Payments for Environmental Services Programmes (PSA) and the carbon market, will make it possible to incentivize forest conservation, as well as the restoration of ecosystems which, with the participation of women and indigenous communities, will have an impact on mitigation through the protection and increase of natural sinks.

5. ASSUMPTIONS AND METHODOLOGICAL APPROACHES, INCLUDING THOSE FOR ESTIMATING AND ACCOUNTING FOR ANTHROPOGENIC GREENHOUSE GAS EMISSIONS AND, AS APPROPRIATE, REMOVALS

a. Assumptions and methodological approaches used for accounting for anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and removals corresponding to the Party's nationally determined contribution, consistent with decision 1/CP.21, paragraph 31, and accounting guidance adopted by the CMA.

Costa Rica uses the methodologies of the 2006 IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories, incorporating the 2013 Supplement and the Good Practice Guidance on Uncertainty, as well as the improvements of the 2019 Refinement. Accounting applies the GWPs from the Fifth Assessment Report (AR5).

The methodological details, assumptions and reporting tiers are documented in the INGEI 1990-2021, and in the First Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) 2024, which contains the official update of the inventory. BTR 2024: https://unfccc.int/documents/645177.

b. Assumptions and methodological approaches used for accounting for the implementation of policies, measures or strategies in the nationally determined contribution.

The detail of the methodological approach used in the NDC update for accounting seeks to address the need to improve data collection for monitoring, evaluation, and learning, which are necessary for assessing the effectiveness and impacts of the objectives established in the various strategic framework instruments developed (policies, strategies, plans) that the country already has, as reported in the first BTR. Therefore, the national state of affairs was analyzed in light of the technical attributes of each emission category, the sectors involved, as well as the activities, in order to define scopes, targets and indicators linked to the achievement of the contribution, which are detailed in the NDC Implementation Plan, with management indicators linked to implementation indicators. This detail is presented as an annex to this update. This will make it possible to strengthen traceability between climate action and level of mitigation impact, as well as to identify co-benefits associated with mitigation and adaptation measures, as well as their symbiosis.

c. If applicable, information on how the Party will take into account existing methods and guidance under the Convention to account for anthropogenic emissions and removals, in accordance with Article 4, paragraph 14, of the Paris Agreement, as appropriate.

Costa Rica applies methodologies established under the Convention, specifically the 2006 IPCC Guidelines, the 2013 Supplement, the good practice guidance on uncertainty, and the 2019 Refinement. These methodologies are consistent with the guidance in force under the UNFCCC, and are used in an integrated manner in the INGEI 1990-2021 and the First BTR published in 2024, thus ensuring alignment with Article 4, paragraph 14, of the Paris Agreement.

d. IPCC methodologies and metrics used for estimating anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and removals.

Costa Rica uses the 2006 IPCC Guidelines as the basis for estimating greenhouse gas emissions and removals. According to Table 1.2 of the INGEI 1990-2021, methodological tiers 1 and 2 are mainly applied, depending on the sector and category. Specific improvements have been implemented in line with the 2019 Refinement. This combination allows a balance between the availability of national data and the continuous improvement of the reporting and accounting system.

e. Sector-, category- or activity-specific assumptions, methodologies and approaches consistent with IPCC guidance, as appropriate, including, as applicable:

The methodological detail and key assumptions used to estimate emissions are recorded in the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas Emissions by Sources and Removals by Sinks of Costa Rica, 1990-2021.

Some considerations on the methodological approach as requested:

i. Approach to addressing emissions and subsequent removals from natural disturbances on managed lands.

Costa Rica excludes from the INGEI emissions and removals derived from natural disturbances, such as volcanic activity, river meanders, earthquakes, floods and shadows in satellite images, since they are not considered anthropogenic. Primary forests are not considered managed and, therefore, their carbon flows are only accounted for in the event of deforestation. This exclusion is aligned with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines and was reaffirmed in the most recent FREL/FRL.

ii. Approach used to account for emissions and removals from harvested wood products.

The “production approach” is applied, in accordance with chapter 12 of volume 4 of the 2006 IPCC Guidelines, complemented by the 2013 Good Practice Guidelines. This approach considers that harvested wood products retain stored carbon beyond the year of harvest, avoiding its oxidation. Inclusion is limited to products derived from national forestry activities.

iii. Approach used to address the effects of age-class structure in forests.

The INGEI 2021 distinguishes between primary and secondary forests, applying carbon stocks differentiated by forest type and age class, particularly in secondary forests. Carbon growth in the latter is modeled based on field inventories and growth functions, and is only considered when new forests reach maturity detectable through satellite images (≥4 or 8 years, depending on forest type). Forest plantations are classified as secondary forest and their harvest and replanting cycle is accounted for as deforestation and regeneration, respectively.

f. Other assumptions and methodological approaches used for understanding the nationally determined contribution and, if applicable, estimating corresponding emissions and removals, including:

i. How the reference indicators, baseline(s) and/or reference level(s), including, where applicable, sector-, category- or activity-specific reference levels, are constructed, including, for example, key parameters, assumptions, definitions, methodologies, data sources and models used.

Costa Rica formulates its climate targets on the basis of the best available science, consistent with the global goal of limiting the temperature increase to 1.5 °C. At the national level, this translates into prioritizing measures according to their effectiveness in reducing emissions and maximizing social and environmental co-benefits, supported by a robust and constantly evolving modeling system.

Since 2016, the country has strengthened its technical capacities with open-source tools, advancing from sectoral energy models (TIMES–CR, OSeMOSYS–CR) to the integrated CLEW–CR framework, and more recently CLEW-CR-3.0, which broadens the approach to explicitly integrate five IPCC sectors (Energy, IPPU, Agriculture, FOLU and Waste).

Baselines and reference levels are constructed with:

  • Data from the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas Emissions by Sources and Removals by Sinks of Costa Rica, 1990-2021 (2006 IPCC Guidelines, 2019 Refinement, GWP AR5)
  • Sectoral statistics from national sources (MINAE, ICE, MAG, AyA, FONAFIFO)
  • Projections of GDP, population and sectoral demand (INEC, BCCR)
  • Specific reference levels: FREL/FRL for AFOLU, Kigali Amendment baseline for IPPU, Energy Balances for Energy, Annual Reports for the electricity sector, to give a few examples

The analysis with CLEW ensures technical consistency across sectors and makes it possible to assess the aggregate impact of mitigation measures on national emissions, taking as reference the absolute target for 2030 and the 2021–2030 emissions budget. The modeling results confirm that the country remains on a trajectory compatible with meeting the NDC target.

A natural climate forcer is the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) phenomenon, whose influence has been estimated in the impact on water sources and their relationship with hydroelectric generation, as part of the planning carried out by ICE and the climate modeling related to this phenomenon carried out by IMN. 6. HOW THE PARTY CONSIDERS THAT ITS NATIONALLY DETERMINED CONTRIBUTION IS FAIR AND AMBITIOUS IN THE LIGHT OF ITS NATIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES

a. How the Party considers that its nationally determined contribution is fair and ambitious in the light of its national circumstances.

This contribution reflects a balance between the international responsibility to contribute to the goal of the Paris Agreement and the national circumstances of a developing country with a low share of global emissions, but highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Its formulation integrates criteria of equity and sustainable development, ensuring that emission reduction and adaptation targets are designed consistently with inclusive economic growth and the resilience of communities. Ambition is expressed in the expansion of sectoral scope, the incorporation of quantifiable mitigation and adaptation targets, and the implementation of actions with socioeconomic and environmental co-benefits that go beyond the previous commitment.

b. Fairness considerations, including reflecting on equity.

This NDC incorporates the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, prioritizing measures that simultaneously address emission reduction and adaptation in strategic sectors. It ensures that the burden of climate action is distributed fairly, with special attention to rural populations, Indigenous communities, women and groups in situations of vulnerability. Equity is understood in intergenerational terms, ensuring that decisions preserve natural capital and strengthen resilience.

c. How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 3, of the Paris Agreement.

This NDC represents a progression with respect to the previous one, increasing the level of ambition through more demanding mitigation targets, greater sectoral coverage and the incorporation of more robust monitoring indicators. The definition of these targets has considered the best possible scientific evidence, the progress achieved since 2020 and coherence with long-term national objectives, as well as a strong intersectoral participatory process, ensuring that the contribution reflects the highest possible ambition in accordance with national circumstances.

d. How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 4, of the Paris Agreement.

The NDC strengthens the mitigation and adaptation effort through the inclusion of cross-cutting actions that progressively cover all sectors of the economy, with emphasis on the greatest reduction potential and vulnerability.

e. How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 6, of the Paris Agreement.

The NDC distinguishes between unconditional commitments, financed with national resources, and conditional commitments, subject to the provision of international means of implementation in finance, technology and capacities. The measures are mostly conditional because Costa Rica is, a developing country, and does not have the necessary resources to apply the conditionality. Mechanisms will be sought to address this particularity; however, financial risk is inherent to the proposal due to national circumstances.

7. HOW THE NATIONALLY DETERMINED CONTRIBUTION CONTRIBUTES TOWARDS ACHIEVING THE OBJECTIVE OF THE CONVENTION AS SET OUT IN ITS ARTICLE 2

a. How the nationally determined contribution contributes towards achieving the objective of the Convention as set out in its Article 2.

It proposes a comprehensive set of measures that contribute to stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations at levels that prevent dangerous interference with the climate system, through mitigation actions complemented by adaptation programs, which strengthen the resilience of ecosystems and communities. The prioritized measures allow the natural adaptation of ecosystems, through forest restoration, sustainable land management and protection of marine-coastal ecosystems, safeguard food security through the transition towards resilient agricultural systems and promote sustainable, low-emission economic development.

b. How the nationally determined contribution contributes towards Article 2, paragraph 1(a), and Article 4, paragraph 1, of the Paris Agreement.

The NDC contributes to compliance with Article 2.1(a) by establishing a national emission reduction trajectory consistent with the global goal of limiting the temperature increase to 1,5 °C, through the transformation of energy systems towards renewable sources, the electrification of transport, industrial reconversion and the reduction of emissions from the agricultural and forestry sector. In line with Article 4.1, the proposed actions promote the increase of carbon sinks, such as the restoration of more than 50 000 ha of forest cover and the protection of wetlands and mangroves, contributing to achieving a net balance of emissions and removals in the second half of the century. All of this is developed within the framework of sustainable development and poverty eradication, through the creation of green jobs, the improvement of quality of life and the strengthening of the country's economic and social resilience.
