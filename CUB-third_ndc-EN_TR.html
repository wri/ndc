Nationally Determined Contribution 3.0

National circumstances

1.1 National (general) circumstances that are related to the proposal of the CRC.

Cuba is a country highly committed to the preservation of the environment and natural resources and to actions that contribute to reversing the global climate change currently underway on our planet. The Cuban government and society give high priority to addressing climate change. Cuba is a party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), the Kyoto Protocol, and the Paris Agreement.

The country's commitment to climate change is reflected in the public policies adopted in Cuba, among which the following stand out:

  • The Constitution of the Republic of Cuba, adopted in 2019, promotes the protection and conservation of the environment and the fight against climate change, which threatens the survival of the human species, based on the recognition of common but differentiated responsibilities; the establishment of a just and equitable international economic order; and the eradication of irrational patterns of production and consumption.

  • The State Plan for Addressing Climate Change, also known as Task Life, approved in April 2017, has as its main objective the protection of human life and its quality in the context of a changing climate. It involves all sectors of the economy and society and is applied at the national and local levels to achieve resilient and lower-emission development. Task Life constitutes a comprehensive action plan, comprised of five strategic actions and eleven tasks.

  • The Law on the System of Natural Resources and the Environment (Law 150) came into force in 2023 and in its Article 3 proposes to promote resilient development with low greenhouse gas emissions, through planning for adaptation and mitigation to climate change, in the short, medium and long term.

  • Decree 86 on Addressing Climate Change, published in 2023 as a complement to Law 150, established the objectives, scope, subjects of application, and the institutional framework for addressing climate change.

The coordination of climate action in the country, including the processes for the preparation, monitoring, and reporting of the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), falls to the Ministry of Science, Technology, and Environment. The implementation of the measures contained in the NDC is the responsibility of the Central State Administration Bodies, National Entities, and local governments, as appropriate. The NDC and the reporting of its monitoring through the Biennial Transparency Reports to the UNFCCC are approved by the Council of Ministers.

The economic, commercial, and financial blockade imposed by the United States government against Cuba for more than six decades constitutes the main obstacle to the country's development, negatively impacting all spheres and sectors of Cuban life and society, including policies, programs, and actions to address climate change and its effects.

The inclusion of Cuba on the U.S. State Department's unilateral list of alleged state sponsors of terrorism; the possibility of taking action in U.S. courts to file lawsuits under Title III of the Cuban Democratic Liberty and Solidarity Act (Helms-Burton Act); the sanctions or threats of sanctions against shipping companies, carriers, insurers, or reinsurers involved in supplying fuel to Cuba; the intense and meticulous monitoring of Cuban financial transactions and the resulting obstacles to the supply of essential resources; as well as the continuation of other unilateral listings, have been confirmed as the most severe measures.

The blockade represents the fundamental barrier to Cuba's access to international sources of financing, supplies, and technologies.

In the most recent stage, in addition to the negative influence of the intensified US embargo, the devastating effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and the complex international scenario have deepened the crisis in the Cuban economy, which has been reflected in the behavior of the main economic indicators. Despite the negative factors described above, the Cuban economy made progress in 2021 and 2022, which, although modest, shows favorable results in some productive and service sectors. This has been the result of implementing measures in line with new strategic guidelines adopted for the country's development, such as the decentralization of the State and government to benefit local governments; the increase in foreign investment; and the encouragement of private sector involvement in the established development objectives.

A major challenge to ensuring the country's development lies in the shrinking working-age population facing Cuban society. The Cuban population has shown a declining pattern since 2016, placing the country among the most aged in Latin America and the Caribbean. Its age structure reflects a gradual aging process, which will continue to intensify over time. This situation presents a challenge for Cuban society. On the one hand, the needs of the aging population must be addressed, and on the other, development must be guaranteed.

A determining factor in sustainable development is climate change, which has exacerbated and will continue to exacerbate the environmental problems facing the country.

The insularity of Cuba and the morphology of its coasts, which span 5,746 km in length and are 70% covered by mangroves, 20% by sandy coasts or beaches, and 10% by rocky coasts or cliffs, increase its vulnerability to the potential impacts of climate change. The large extent of the coastline covered by low-lying and flood-prone areas makes it highly vulnerable to the impact of sea-level rise. In this sense, the protection provided by healthy marine and coastal ecosystems, especially mangroves, which, in addition to covering 70% of the coastline of the main island, make up most of the keys and islets that border the edges of the Cuban insular shelf, is essential for sustaining the country's socio-economic activities. At the same time, these mangrove formations and other coastal ecosystems can offer mitigation co-benefits, which are currently under study.

The main climate events with direct impacts observed on water resources, agriculture, human health and other fundamental ecosystems and sectors are1:

  • Changes in the climate regime, with increased temperature and humidity deficit, negatively impacting ecosystems, particularly freshwater and agriculture.

  • Increased frequency of extreme weather events, mainly droughts, consecutive dry days, very hot days, and a reduction in cool nights, with adverse impacts on ecosystems and key socio-economic sectors.

  • Increased impact from intense hurricanes and the occurrence of other dangerous weather phenomena, such as tornadoes, heavy rains, strong winds, and floods.

The results of recent climate projections for Cuba show:

  • Hotter, drier, and more extreme climate expected by the end of the 21st century.

  • Annual air temperature increase for the Cuban archipelago exceeding 1.0°C by 2030 and 3.5°C by 2070, compared to the reference period 1961-1990.

  • Reduction in precipitation of approximately 10% during the rainy season.

  • Sea level rise with values of 29.3 and 95.0 cm for the years 2050 and 2100.

National circumstances have determined the behavior of the results in confronting climate change:

  • The implementation of climate change response plans, including the application of mitigation and adaptation measures, has been limited.

  • Reduced activity in the economic sectors and society compared to pre-pandemic years has influenced the volume of emissions produced during the reporting period.

  • Reductions in Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions have been primarily driven by the decline in economic activity and, to a lesser extent, by the implementation of planned mitigation actions.

  • On the other hand, the absence of deforestation and the efforts made to promote forested areas and conserve existing forest resources have led to an increase in CO2 removal capacity. This has positively influenced the emissions balance by increasing carbon sinks.

1.2. Country Vision on the CNDs.

On November 21, 2015, Cuba submitted to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) the "Proposal for an Intended Nationally Determined Contribution"

Individual Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC), following the invitation to all Parties, to strengthen discussions surrounding the adoption of the Paris Agreement.

With the entry into force of the Agreement and its ratification by Cuba, without any changes being made to its INDC at that time, this automatically became the First Nationally Determined Contribution of our country.

In accordance with Articles 3 and 4 of the Paris Agreement, and paragraphs 22 and 24 of Decision 1/CP.21 of 2015, Cuba carried out an update of its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) during 2020, which it submitted to the UNFCCC as its First Updated NDC in 2020 (hereinafter referred to as NDC-2020). The NDC-2020 maintained its scope with a time horizon up to 2030, but included a significant increase in ambition by incorporating new elements related to adaptation and mitigation, new sectors, indicators with more challenging targets, and improvements in the quality and transparency of the information provided.

This document constitutes an update of Cuba's Nationally Determined Contribution to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and is presented in accordance with Articles 3 and 4 of the Paris Agreement, and paragraphs 24, 25, 27 and 31 of Decision 1/CP.21.

Although technically the CND-2025 would be the country's second CND, in order to align its name with the international process, henceforth the CND-2025 of the Republic of Cuba will be called CND3.0.

By recording its increased ambition in this update, Cuba reiterates its position that the content of the Nationally Determined Contribution is established by each country voluntarily and not prescriptively, and must be viewed within the broader context identified by Article 3 of the Paris Agreement.

Given its national circumstances, Cuba has been forced to condition the implementation of some of its ambitious GHG emissions reduction targets on receiving support from developed countries. To date, this support for the implementation of the 2020 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) has not been received, and there are no explicit indications that it will be forthcoming in the short term.

In accordance with these circumstances, Cuba identifies, as one of the paths to achieving the development of a resilient and lower-carbon economy, the use of the cooperative instruments established in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, mainly related to the implementation of the energy transition program and the country's forestry development.

During the preparation of the NDC, the results of the global stocktake were taken into account, in accordance with Article 4, paragraph 9, of the Paris Agreement.

1.3 Country's view on some specific approaches

1.3.1. On support

Contributions must be examined in the context of the Common but Differentiated Responsibilities of the Parties to the Convention, which implies, among other elements, the ability to realize the commitments expressed by developing countries, the demand for financial resources, technology transfer, and capacity building, which developed countries must contribute incrementally, in accordance with their obligations established in the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement.

1.3.2. Adaptation.

Adaptation is reaffirmed as the top priority, due to the negative impact of climate change on its natural and human ecosystems and given the country's low level of GHG emissions.

The adaptation measures reflected in this Contribution are presented to indicate Cuba's priorities in accordance with its policies and circumstances, and do not in any way indicate an international obligation for the country.

Cuba is making significant efforts to allocate funding, through its economic plans, to the implementation of adaptation measures. These future figures will be limited by the country's financial resources. Much larger amounts will be required to respond to the growing impacts of climate change.

These and other areas associated with the country's economic and social development will require financial resources, technology, and capacity building, derived from international cooperation, compliance with the obligations of industrialized countries under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, and greater effectiveness and agility in the management of Multilateral Funds, particularly the Green Climate Fund.

1.3.3. Mitigation.

Mitigation is valued in national policies as an essential dimension of development, contributing to technological modernization on a sustainability basis, strengthening capacities, improving the effectiveness and efficiency of processes by reducing the consumption of fossil fuels, using more efficient technologies, and improving waste management, among other important elements that are transversal to all economic activity in the country.

GHG removals have shown an increasing trend, primarily due to the sustained increase in the country's forest areas. The potential for further increases in removals from this source is now very limited, as forest cover is practically at its maximum limit. Determining blue carbon potential is an important requirement and a priority for defining new CO2 removal potentials from the atmosphere.

GHG emissions from the Energy sector have constituted the largest volume of the country's emissions, and although a decrease has been observed in the series, due to economic constraints that have forced a reduction in the consumption of fossil fuels for electricity generation and transport, it continues to be the main emitter, with more than 64% of total emissions in 2022.

The Agriculture sector follows in terms of GHG emissions volume, and together with the Energy sector, accounted for approximately 85% of total emissions in 2022.

GHG emissions from the Waste sector have increased, representing approximately 14% of the country's total emissions in 2022. The main cause is attributed to the sustained increase in solid waste generation and its final disposal in uncategorized landfills.

Throughout the series, it is evident that the IPPU sector has been the hardest hit by the economic crises the country has suffered, aggravated by the global economic crisis and the unequal system of trade, representing less than 1% of the country's total emissions in the last year of the series.

Considering the sectoral contribution to the national GHG inventory, the sectors prioritized for emissions reduction at this stage are Energy, Agriculture, and Waste. The country's efforts to identify and implement mitigation measures are concentrated in these sectors. However, the industrial sector must also be taken into account due to its need for future growth and the country's development.

1.3.4. Just Transition

Cuba recognizes that the process of building resilient, low-emission development must occur within the framework of a just transition, with an ethical and social vision that encompasses all of society.

At a global level, this transition must ensure that no one is left behind, while recognizing different national circumstances, in the context of implementing equity and common but differentiated responsibilities, taking into account priorities in sustainable development and poverty eradication, all of which requires appropriate investments and timeframes, which in turn demands the design and construction of international collaboration frameworks.

At the national level, Cuba's vision of a just transition encompasses generating benefits for all of society, with particular attention to gender considerations, children and youth, and including the development of green jobs and the consideration of the "One Health" approach, among other visions adopted in a manner consistent with our socialist development model, which also include:

  • Reduce the territorial and social inequalities experienced in addressing climate change by considering them in the design of public policies and in adaptation and mitigation strategies.

  • To strengthen public knowledge, awareness, and participation regarding the negative impacts of climate change, thereby increasing resilience, citizen participation, equity, and responsibility within Cuban society, by promoting its study at all levels of education.

1.3.5. Role of technology

For this period of implementation of the NDC, Cuba will place greater emphasis on technology and its transfer, including increasing its interaction with the Convention's Technology Mechanism and its Technology Executive Committee, and promoting the national assessment of appropriate technologies, according to national needs and circumstances.

1.3.6. Education and learning.

Strengthen integrated formal climate education and learning at all levels of education—primary, secondary, and vocational—as well as skills training and research for green jobs. Climate education opens opportunities for green jobs, strengthens community resilience, and fosters a deeper understanding and appreciation of nature.

3. Priority adaptation actions that form the basis of national action and for the management of international financing (Adaptation contributions)

Cuba is currently engaged in designing its National Adaptation Plan, starting with identifying the key components, actors, regularities and public policies, the conceptual basis, methodological guidelines, metrics and specific tools that will facilitate climate risk management, incorporate climate variables into development planning processes, improve the planning and implementation of adaptation actions, and strengthen the capacity to react to and prevent the impacts of climate change in Cuba.

This National Plan, which must be completed by 2025, has the following specific purposes:

  • To delve deeper into the effects of climate change that the country must face, including sectors and territories.

  • Identify measures to minimize adverse impacts and effectively manage the risks associated with climate change.

  • To facilitate the coherent integration of climate change adaptation into economic and social development policies, plans, and programs.

  • Establish priorities and coordinate efforts on adaptation measures and actions with clear criteria. Identify adaptation assets, that is, those measures already adopted that currently contribute to adaptation. Create synergies between measures and actions. Foster collaboration between sectors and actors in the field of adaptation.

  • Optimize the allocation of available resources and identify financial sources that can effectively support the execution of the plan.

  • To contribute to creating a strategic culture regarding adaptation to climate change.

As a starting point, the Plan will focus on the priority areas for adaptation, as outlined in the State Plan for Addressing Climate Change (2017), and which are described below:

Do not allow the construction of new housing in threatened coastal settlements that are predicted to disappear due to permanent flooding and are the most vulnerable. Reduce population density in low-lying coastal areas.

Develop construction concepts for infrastructure adapted to coastal flooding in low-lying areas.

Adapt agricultural activities, particularly those with the greatest impact on the country's food security, to changes in land use resulting from sea level rise and drought.

Reduce the areas of crops near the coast or affected by saltwater intrusion. Diversify crops, improve soil conditions, and introduce and develop varieties resistant to the new temperature scenario.

Plan the urban redevelopment processes for threatened settlements and infrastructure within established timeframes, taking into account the country's economic conditions. Begin with lower-cost measures, such as nature-based solutions (beach restoration, reforestation).

To conserve, maintain, and comprehensively restore the sandy beaches of the Cuban archipelago, prioritizing urbanized beaches used for tourism and reducing the structural vulnerability of built heritage.

To ensure the availability and efficient use of water as part of the response to drought, through the application of technologies for water conservation and meeting local demands.

Protect and restore water quality by increasing the proportion of environmentally good quality water and safely treated wastewater, as well as ensuring the protection of coastal aquifers.

Implement actions that contribute to reducing flooding. Improve and maintain hydraulic infrastructure, and introduce measures to measure water efficiency and productivity.

Direct reforestation efforts towards maximizing the protection of soils and water in both quantity and quality, as well as the recovery of the most affected mangroves.

Prioritize reservoirs, canals, and water regulation strips in the tributary basins of the main bays and coasts of the insular shelf.

Stop the deterioration, rehabilitate and conserve coral reefs throughout the archipelago, prioritizing the ridges that border the insular shelf and protect urbanized beaches used for tourism. Prevent overfishing of fish that benefit corals.

Maintain and incorporate into territorial and urban planning the scientific results of the Macroproject on Hazards and Vulnerability of the Coastal Zone (2050-2100), as well as the Hazard, Vulnerability, and Risk Studies in the disaster reduction cycle. Use this information as an early warning system for decision-making.

Implement and monitor adaptation measures derived from sectoral policies in programs, plans, and projects related to food security, land-use and urban planning, fisheries, agriculture, health, tourism, construction, transportation, industry, and integrated forest management. (This is a broad program covering 12 sectors, and the actions to be implemented must be reviewed in accordance with the respective sectoral programs.)

Strengthen monitoring, surveillance, and early warning systems to systematically assess the condition and quality of the coastal zone, water, drought, forests, and human, animal, and plant health.

Prioritize measures and actions to raise risk awareness and increase the level of knowledge and participation of the entire population in addressing climate change; to foster a culture that promotes water conservation; to regulate the use and prevention of fire in agricultural and forestry activities; as well as to regulate and control the use of fire in waste, both domestic and industrial.

4.4. Improvement of the net carbon absorption capacity of Cuban forests.

Brief description of the contribution:

The contribution includes the main variations in which it is possible to improve the net carbon absorption capacity of forests, consisting of:

  1. Continue increasing the country's forest cover: As a result of changes in land use, new areas have been incorporated into the forest land fund, offering new opportunities for afforestation. For this period, it is projected that 50 kha of new forest areas will be added to the forested area through self-generated efforts (unconditional contribution), and it is possible to add another 40 kha if external funding is received (conditional contribution).

  2. Reducing forest degradation levels: Under national conditions, it is possible to manage 75 kha of forests (unconditional contribution) to reduce degradation, and with external funding, an additional 90 kha could be managed (contribution conditional on support).

  3. Establish the National Forest Monitoring and Evaluation System (SNMEF): By periodically conducting the National Forest Inventory and monitoring forest mensuration and degradation variables using permanent sampling plots, it is possible to update the necessary information and improve the quality and transparency of the processes for estimating GHG emissions and removals by forests. All of this also requires external support to improve the technical and institutional capacities that enable the SNMEF to function effectively.

  4. Complete the necessary studies to determine the specific emission factors adjusted for local forest types and species: With the technical and financial support of international organizations, it is possible to transition to the use of methodologies that provide information more closely aligned with the national reality, thereby improving the determination of forest carbon sink capacity.

It is expected that with the implementation of the above measures, the forest cover achieved in the planned period will contribute to CO2 removal levels estimated at 150,000 kt.

The country is making significant efforts to implement the measures committed to in the 2020 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), in a very complex economic situation, largely caused by the US blockade, without receiving the levels of support it requires to implement its climate priorities.

Given the national circumstances described above, the proposed national contribution is highly ambitious and even challenging, particularly in terms of mitigation.

That ambition is implicit from the outset, for countries like Cuba facing challenges in adaptation and the threat of losses and damages that exceed their financial capacity.

Under these conditions, allocating resources to mitigation measures and even expanding their scope compared to the previous National Climate Change Strategy (2020) reflects the country's strong commitment to global efforts to address climate change.

Cuba's efforts are part of a vision for resilient, low-emission development. As we already mentioned when referring to a just transition, our actions are designed to benefit all of society, seeking to combine adaptation and mitigation with the improvement of social and economic conditions for all Cubans.

In the medium term, Cuba supports efforts to gradually build non-destructive national policies (NDCs) that encompass the entire economy and also considers the development of strategies aimed at climate neutrality. However, it cautions that this will only be possible under conditions of a much more ambitious and facilitated environment, contributions in terms of the means of implementation from developed countries, and even deeper reforms of the current international financial system.

For all these reasons, Cuba considers its Nationally Determined Contribution to be fair and ambitious, reflecting the highest possible ambition in light of its national circumstances and taking into account the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and its respective capabilities.

6. The way in which the nationally determined contribution contributes to the achievement of the objective of the Convention, as set out in its article 2

This NDC contributes to achieving the objective of the Convention by making a tangible contribution to stabilizing greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that prevents dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system.

At the same time, it contributes to the achievement of Articles 2 and 4 of the Paris Agreement, through its contribution, commensurate with our national circumstances, to:

    • Keep the increase in global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, recognizing that this would substantially reduce the risks and impacts of climate change;

    • Increase the capacity to adapt to the adverse effects of climate change and promote climate resilience and low greenhouse gas emissions development, in a way that does not compromise food production;

    • Promote an enabling environment for financial flows compatible with a pathway leading to climate-resilient and low-greenhouse-gas-emission development.
