Executive Summary

ii. Executive Summary

The Republic of Ecuador is an Andean country located in northwestern South America, with a land area of 256,370 km² that includes its continental region, composed of the Coast, Highlands, and Amazon rainforest, and its insular region (Galápagos Islands) in the Pacific Ocean. It also has a maritime area of 1,358,440 km², highlighting its natural wealth and geographical diversity. Its economy, characterized by dollarization and dependence on oil, has experienced ups and downs, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, in 2021 and 2022 it showed signs of recovery, with economic growth driven by domestic consumption and key sectors such as oil refining and trade.

Through its Second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), Ecuador reaffirms its commitment to contributing to the achievement of the goals of the Paris Agreement and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Its main pillars are the reduction of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, the increase in adaptive capacity to the adverse effects of climate change, and the mobilization of financial resources to contribute to climate change management.

The overall objective of Ecuador's Second NDC is to implement policies, actions, and efforts that promote the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, increased resilience, and decreased vulnerability to the adverse effects of climate change in the priority sectors of the current National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC), through a joint effort among sectoral entities of the Central Government, Local Governments, the Private Sector, Academia, International Cooperation, and Civil Society. These actions and efforts will be guided by the strategic lines and measures identified in the subsequent sections of this document. Based on Article 4, paragraph 9 of the Paris Agreement and the decisions of the UNFCCC, Ecuador presents its Second NDC with an implementation period from January 1, 2026, to December 31, 2035.

The Second NDC responds to national policies on development, mitigation, adaptation and climate finance, as well as to international instruments ratified by the country, such as the 2030 Agenda and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), reflected in their different components: mitigation, adaptation, loss and damage and means of implementation.

Climate change mitigation encompasses the sectors prioritized in the National Climate Change Strategy 2012-2025: Energy, Industrial Processes, Agriculture, Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) and Waste.

According to the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, in 2022, total GHG emissions in Ecuador reached 88,262.87 kt CO2-eq.

Given this background, the Republic of Ecuador commits, under its unconditional scenario, to achieving a projected reduction in GHG emissions by 2035 of approximately 8,800 kt CO2-eq, 7% compared to the 2010 baseline scenario, across all mitigation sectors. Additionally, a conditional target is set, which could achieve an 8% reduction, approximately 10,600 kt CO2-eq, subject to international cooperation support. This objective has been established using the emissions reduction scenario modeled in the National Mitigation Plan, considered as a long-term strategy.

In line with Article 7 of the Paris Agreement and as a country highly vulnerable to climate change, Ecuador considers adaptation an essential component of its Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). According to the National Climate Change Strategy 2012-2025, the following sectors are prioritized within this component: Natural Heritage, Water Resources, Health, Human Settlements, Productive and Strategic Sectors; and Food Sovereignty, Agriculture, Livestock, Aquaculture and Fisheries (SAG).

Considering the barriers to implementing adaptation at the national level, the need to strengthen adaptive capacity and increase the resilience of communities and ecosystems to the adverse impacts of climate change is emphasized. The Second NDC proposes measures to: conserve natural heritage, guarantee the availability of water resources, strengthen the health response to situations related to the presence of diseases, design and implement infrastructure to address floods and landslides, and strengthen food resilience, ensuring that food production is not affected by climate change.

Regarding the adaptation component, the country commits to contributing, at the national and subnational levels, to global efforts to increase adaptive capacity, resilience, and reduce risk from the adverse effects of climate change, in a context of equity, sustainable development, and poverty eradication, respecting the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, and in accordance with the country's capacities.

Ecuador, despite its voluntary mitigation efforts and the implementation of its adaptation actions, is looking to the future to develop mechanisms that will allow it to address the losses and damages attributed to climate change. The Second NDC has established an approach of initial measures, while the conceptual framework for loss and damages, adjusted to the country's national circumstances, is being developed.

Regarding the means of implementation, Ecuador, considered a developing country, has made an initial estimate of the resources, mechanisms, and financial tools that will allow for the implementation of its actions during the 2026-2035 period. This estimate, based on available information, amounts to approximately USD 6.5 billion, of which 27% is unconditional and 73% is conditional. This amount constitutes an estimated reference budget, the details of which will be specified according to the management needs of the implementing entities. Capacity-building and technology transfer actions have also been identified for the implementation of the initiatives that comprise the NDC. In this context, the need for external resources to contribute to national efforts to address climate change is reinforced.

The efforts reflected in the Second NDC respond to the provisions of Articles 4, 7, 9 and 13 of the Paris Agreement and introduce improvements by providing the necessary information to ensure clarity, transparency and understanding of the progress in its implementation in line with the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF).

In accordance with decision 1/CP.21 of the Paris Agreement, which recognizes climate change as a common problem for humanity, Ecuador has voluntarily included cross-cutting approaches to gender, interculturality, and intergenerationality in its Second NDC in order to reduce social gaps, have an inclusive policy that considers the needs and respects the rights and ancestral knowledge of indigenous peoples and nationalities, as well as the impact that climate change will have on present and future generations.

Finally, the Second NDC represents a significant step towards a low-emission and climate-resilient economy to address the adverse effects of climate change. Through this public policy, Ecuador reaffirms its commitment to contributing to the global goals of the Paris Agreement.

Achieving the proposed goals requires a joint effort with the support of international cooperation. In this regard, the Second NDC will be the basis for strengthening partnerships and mobilizing the necessary resources to implement climate action in the coming years with a view to achieving the proposed objectives.

1. National Circumstances

1.1 Government Structure

The structure of the government5 is determined by five branches or functions of the State established in the 2008 Constitution of Ecuador. The first branch is the Executive Branch, exercised by the President of the Republic, who acts as Head of State and Government, and is responsible for the country's public administration. The Executive Branch is responsible for conceiving and implementing general policies, according to which the laws must be applied, representing the Nation in various spheres. Furthermore, it is supported by 20 Ministries and four Secretariats of State, which are constitutional bodies responsible for specific functions within the government, such as the leadership and management of specific areas of public administration.

The second branch of government is the Legislative Branch, which is represented by the National Assembly of the Republic of Ecuador. This body plays a fundamental role in the drafting and approval of laws, as well as in the oversight and auditing of the executive branch.

The third branch of government is the Judicial Branch, composed of jurisdictional, auxiliary, and autonomous bodies. The jurisdictional bodies are the National Court of Justice, the Provincial Courts of Justice, and the lower courts and tribunals. The auxiliary bodies are notaries, auctioneers, and judicial custodians. The autonomous bodies are the Public Defender's Office and the Attorney General's Office. The Council of the Judiciary is the governing, administrative, oversight, and disciplinary body of the Judicial Branch. The National Court of Justice is the highest jurisdictional body and hears cases involving individuals with national jurisdiction. The Judicial Branch is responsible for the administration, oversight, and discipline of justice in the country.

The Fourth Power is the Electoral Power, represented by the National Electoral Council (CNE), which is the highest body in charge of suffrage in the country. Furthermore, this body is responsible for the appointment of authorities in accordance with the Constitution and the law, thus contributing to the functioning of the democratic system.

Finally, the Fifth Branch of Government is the Transparency and Social Control Function, which focuses on promoting and guaranteeing the exercise of the rights of participation, social control over public affairs, accountability, transparency, and the fight against corruption.

Likewise, the Constitution of the Republic of Ecuador protects climate change governance. This has allowed for the development of regulatory instruments for the management of national environmental policy. Regarding climate change, the Organic Environmental Code (COA) and its Regulations (RCOA) are legal instruments that present the fundamental principles of climate change management, define national competencies and the scope of action of the National Environmental Authority (AAN), and align with international commitments.

In 2009, through Executive Decree No. 1815, climate change adaptation and mitigation were declared a State policy. Subsequently, in the same year, through Ministerial Agreement No. 104, the Undersecretariat of Climate Change (SCC) was created within the National Environmental Authority. This body is responsible for leading climate change mitigation and adaptation actions, while also facilitating the implementation of technology transfer mechanisms, financing, and capacity building for the design, promotion, and evaluation of climate change plans, programs, projects, measures, and actions.

The National Environmental Authority plays a key role in the technical management of inter-institutional instruments for compliance with international climate change conventions and treaties, especially the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD).

In this area, it leads the processes of formulating and monitoring Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) for climate change mitigation and adaptation. This ministry is responsible for coordinating and compiling, along with other public and private institutions, the monitoring and reporting of progress in implementing these commitments to address climate change.

As part of climate change governance, there is the Inter-institutional Committee on Climate Change (CICC), chaired by the National Environmental Authority. The CICC is a collegial body responsible for managing, coordinating, dictating, facilitating, and planning the inclusion of intersectoral climate change public policies at the national level, as cross-cutting themes of public policy at all levels of government and within the private sector. It also seeks to ensure the implementation of public policy that allows it to address climate change issues within the scope of its member institutions, ad-hoc members of the Inter-institutional Committee on Climate Change, and any working groups created for this purpose.

The CICC is made up of representatives from different government ministries, as well as representatives from various local government associations. Among the CICC's responsibilities are the approval and review of progress reports and the evaluation of the NDCs. This Committee plays a vital role in intersectoral and intergovernmental coordination and brings together national authorities from different sectors, along with representatives of local governments, to promote coherent and effective climate change management throughout Ecuador.

Additionally, the fight against climate change in Ecuador is guided by international instruments ratified by the State, including the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification, the Convention on Biological Diversity, the Paris Agreement, the Sendai Framework, the 2030 Agenda, and the Sustainable Development Goals, which form the framework under which Ecuador designs and implements a series of actions in this area, including its NDCs.

In this context, the government structure, the National Environmental Authority, the Inter-institutional Committee on Climate Change, and public and private sectoral institutions ensure that the country fulfills its international obligations and also promotes transparency, accountability, and citizen participation in climate action. This coordinated approach allows Ecuador to submit reports to the UNFCCC, demonstrating its leadership and commitment to proactive climate management.

1.2 Demographic Profile

The National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC) conducted the last national census across Ecuador in 2022. According to this data, Ecuador had 16,938,986 inhabitants in 2022, of which 51.3% were women and 48.7% were men, and the average age of the population was approximately 29 years.

In Ecuador, several distinct peoples and nationalities are identified: mestizos, montubios, Indigenous peoples (18 distinct peoples, 14 distinct nationalities, and 2 groups living in voluntary isolation), Afro-Ecuadorians, whites, and others. According to the 2022 Census, 77.5% of the population identifies as mestizo, 7.7% as montubio, 7.7% as Indigenous, 4.8% as Afro-Ecuadorian, 2.2% as white, and 0.1% as other. Regarding population distribution, 63.1% of the population lives in urban areas and 36.9% in rural areas. Regional analysis shows that approximately 53.3% reside on the Coast, 41% in the Highlands, 5.5% in the Amazon, and 0.2% in the Galápagos Islands.

According to data published by the INEC, the national illiteracy rate fell from 6.8% in 2010 to 3.7% in 2022. The net enrollment rate in early childhood education showed a slight post-pandemic recovery, reaching 56.62% in 2023. Enrollment in basic general education reached 91.71%, while the high school graduation rate stabilized between 89% and 90%.

According to the 2022 Ecuador Census, access to basic services in Ecuador is as follows: electricity (96.2%); drinking water (61.1%); sewage (43.6%) and garbage collection (80.7%).

Finally, in 2023, the national unemployment rate was 3.8%, with an urban rate of 5.0% and a rural rate of 1.6%.

The topography and geographic location of Ecuador allow it to possess countless microclimates within its four natural regions: the Pacific Coast, the Andes Mountains, the Amazon, and the Galápagos Islands. The country's climate is characterized by a complex interplay of biophysical factors related to geographic location, the spatiotemporal interaction, and the persistence of climatic parameters that influence regional and microregional atmospheric systems.

Ecuador boasts a diverse ecosystem and immense wealth of natural heritage. According to the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, as of December 2022, the National System of Protected Areas (SNAP) registered 76 areas totaling 26,208,785.58 hectares. The SNAP encompasses all natural areas that guarantee the coverage and connectivity of important ecosystems at the terrestrial, marine, and coastal-marine levels, as well as their cultural resources and main water sources.

On the other hand, the 2016 National Plan for the Integrated and Comprehensive Management of Water Resources in Ecuador's River Basins and Sub-basins indicates that the country has 361,747 hm³ of surface water resources and 56,556 hm³ of groundwater. The average annual volume for the country's regions—Coast, Highlands, and Amazon—is 70,046 hm³, 59,725 hm³, and 246,246 hm³, respectively. Due to atmospheric factors, but also to its geography, Ecuador has multiple climates and microclimates that vary over very short distances, ranging from warm to glacially cold.

The main river basins in Ecuador are the Pacific and the Amazon. Among the most notable river systems is the Pacific basin, which includes the basins of the Esmeraldas, Mira, Cayapas, Guayas, Jubones, Puyango, and Catamayo-Chira rivers. The Amazon basin includes the basins of the Putumayo, Napo, Tigre, Pastaza, Santiago, Morona, and Chinchipe rivers to the south. The largest river basins are those of the Guayas River (in the Pacific basin) and the Napo River (in the Amazon basin).

1.4 Economic Profile

According to the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, Ecuador has a diverse economy with a variety of productive and strategic sectors, which in the last decade has undergone significant and very evident changes, showing some structural weaknesses such as dependence on oil exports, the lack of macroeconomic buffers, limited access to capital markets, the sluggishness of the private sector, high levels of informality, and large gaps in access to public services.

The Ecuadorian economy is characterized by a strong dependence on oil exports, which, along with dollarization, has undergone significant changes in recent decades. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated existing vulnerabilities, causing an economic contraction in 2020.

The lockdown and mobility restrictions led to a drop in aggregate demand, particularly affecting sectors such as tourism and hospitality. The decline in international oil prices worsened the country's fiscal situation. However, the Ecuadorian economy showed signs of recovery in 2021 and 2022, driven mainly by household consumption and investment. Despite this recovery, structural challenges persist that limit sustainable economic growth. High levels of informal employment, limited productive diversification, and gaps in access to public services are some of the most pressing problems. Furthermore, commodity price volatility and domestic political uncertainty pose risks to macroeconomic stability.

Data provided by the Central Bank of Ecuador (BCE) shows that the Ecuadorian economy has been affected by the health crisis, but has also demonstrated some resilience. The oil refining, accommodation and food services, aquaculture and shrimp fishing, transportation, and trade sectors registered the greatest growth in 2021. However, the recovery has been uneven, and there are sectors that have not yet recovered to pre-pandemic levels.

According to data from the Central Bank of Ecuador, between 2020 and 2022, the Ecuadorian economy grew by 4.9% in 2021, after a contraction of -7.80% in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. This growth was driven by the positive variation in Household Final Consumption Expenditure (10.20%) and Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF) (4.30%).

The Central Bank of Ecuador (BCE) indicates that in 2023, Ecuador's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) grew by 2.4%, lower than the 6.2% growth achieved in 2022. This growth was driven by the dynamism of Government Spending, which increased by 3.7%; Exports by 2.3%; Household Consumption by 1.4%; and Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF) by 0.5%.

At the industry level, the following sectors showed positive performance: Electricity and water supply at 7.1%; fishing and aquaculture at 5.9%; public administration at 5.2%; agriculture, livestock and forestry at 4.9%; and transport and storage at 4.8%.

According to data from the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC), in 2023, the Gini coefficient registered a value of 0.457 compared to 0.505 reported for 2010. This index measures income inequality in a society, and in the case of Ecuador, it refers to per capita household income.

In turn, according to the National Survey of Employment, Unemployment and Underemployment (ENEMDU), the poverty rate in Ecuador in December 2023 was 26%, representing an increase of 0.8 percentage points compared to December 2022. In the same year, urban poverty stood at 18.4% and rural poverty at 42.2%; extreme poverty was 9.8% nationwide, with a higher incidence in rural areas at 23.7%.

1.5. Climate profile

Ecuador's geographical location, its complex topography, and the influence of oceanic phenomena such as El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Humboldt Current have resulted in a great diversity of climates. Unlike temperate regions with their four well-defined seasons, Ecuador experiences alternating dry and rainy periods.

The Andes Mountains, which run through the country from north to south, exert a decisive influence on the distribution of rainfall. The western slopes, exposed to humid winds from the Pacific Ocean, receive abundant rainfall, while the eastern slopes, in the rain shadow, have a drier climate.

Furthermore, the country experiences increased rainfall along the Ecuadorian coast and droughts in other regions due to the El Niño phenomenon, characterized by the warming of the waters of the equatorial Pacific. On the other hand, La Niña, the cold phase of ENSO, typically brings drier conditions to the coast and increased rainfall to the highlands.

This climatic variability has given rise to a wide range of ecosystems, from the tropical rainforests of the Amazon to the Andean highlands. Ecuador's climatic diversity is a fundamental factor in its rich biodiversity and represents a challenge for natural resource management and adaptation to climate change.

Within the framework of the National Adaptation Plan and national communications, future climate projections show that, for the near future (2020-2050), precipitation and temperature patterns will be relatively similar to the present, except for changes in the frequency of occurrence of the scenarios.

Between 2020 and 2050, some years could see temperatures up to 2°C higher than average, which could generate various changes affecting people, living organisms, and natural resources. Changes in precipitation of up to 9 mm per day are also evident in certain areas of the country, potentially leading to a greater number of floods and landslides. Furthermore, data shows that in Ecuador, areas prone to drought and already experiencing low levels of precipitation could see a reduction of 4.5 mm of daily precipitation below the observed average.

Furthermore, it is projected that under climate change scenarios, most days of the year could result in rainy days with average temperatures very similar to those of the present, although slightly wetter on the northern coast and in the eastern part of the country, and slightly cooler in most of continental and insular Ecuador. On average, maximum and minimum temperatures would be more extreme towards the south and southeast of continental Ecuador.

Finally, under climate change scenarios, events like El Niño are projected to occur on only ~3% of the days of the year between 2020 and 2050, although with more extreme magnitudes. The pattern exhibits extreme positive precipitation along the Ecuadorian coast and the Galápagos Islands, normal rainfall along the Andean mountain range, and below-normal precipitation in the Amazon region, as well as extreme positive average and maximum temperatures, and only slightly cool nights in the southern and southeastern half of mainland Ecuador and in the Galápagos Islands. On average, the country's volcanoes have lost about 50% of their glacial surface area over the last half-century.

1.6. Impact of COVID

The COVID-19 pandemic hit the global economy hard, exacerbating development challenges. Ecuador experienced a similar impact, as lockdowns disrupted productive activities and progress, increasing the already high public spending on healthcare. The pandemic has intensified poverty and inequality, posing a significant challenge to the country's sustainability and resilience to climate change.

The main impacts were:

  • Economic Contraction: Economic activity contracted due to lockdown measures and mobility restrictions, affecting sectors such as tourism, hospitality, and commerce. The economy contracted by 7.8%, falling from a GDP of USD 71.897 billion in 2019 to USD 66.308 billion in 2020.

  • Increased Unemployment: Job losses, especially in the informal sector, increased unemployment rates and led to greater job insecurity. Unemployment rose by 1 percentage point, from 4.2% in 2019 to 5.2% in 2021. Underemployment increased by 5 percentage points, from 18.2% in 2019 to 23.2% in 2021. The adequate employment rate fell by 5.8 percentage points, from 38.3% in 2019 to 32.5% in 2021.

  • Drop in exports: The decrease in global demand and the fall in oil prices negatively affected Ecuadorian exports. Revenues in the general state budget fell by 27%.

  • Increase in public debt: The government had to increase public spending to address the health crisis, which increased public debt, showing an increase of 10.9 percentage points of GDP, rising from 53.02% in 2019 to 63.93% in 2020, with 72% being external debt and 28% internal debt.

  • Increased poverty: The pandemic deepened existing inequalities and increased poverty levels. According to the INEC (National Institute of Statistics and Censuses), poverty increased by 8 percentage points, rising from 25% in 2019 to 33% in 2020; extreme poverty registered an increase of 6.5 points, reaching 8.9% in 2019 and 15.4% in 2020; these are the highest rates recorded since 2010.

Despite these challenges, the Ecuadorian economy has shown signs of recovery in recent years, driven mainly by household consumption and investment. However, the recovery has been uneven, and structural challenges remain that need to be addressed.

Regarding the First NDC, the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent mobility restrictions created challenges for climate policy management. Sectors such as Agriculture, LULUCF, Energy, Industrial Processes, and Waste experienced difficulties carrying out their usual activities, from field monitoring and local visits to community training. Furthermore, budget cuts and the reallocation of resources to the health emergency exacerbated this situation, highlighting the need to rethink implementation strategies due to the lack of allocated resources.

In the case of adaptation sectors, they were also affected by the mobility restrictions imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic on project implementation. Sectors such as Water Heritage, Human Settlements, and Agricultural and Livestock Services (SAG) were impacted in the implementation of actions due to the impossibility of carrying out activities in the field, participatory workshops, and in-person coordination with communities. The economic crisis, exacerbated by the pandemic, worsened the situation by generating staff reductions, shortening working hours, and limiting available resources. As a result, there were delays in project implementation and a decrease in management capacity. The Natural Heritage sector, which depends heavily on field studies and monitoring, also suffered the consequences of these restrictions.

In turn, impacts were observed in the implementation of the cross-cutting measures of the First NDC. The organizations responsible for these measures faced initial challenges due to the shift to remote work, which affected deadlines and the coordination of activities. However, over time, they were able to adapt to this new dynamic.

The health sector, especially affected by the health emergency, had to pause all its planned activities within the First NDC to focus on controlling the pandemic.

1.7 Details on climate change sectors

The sectors for climate change management in Ecuador are defined in accordance with the National Climate Change Strategy 2012-2025.

For the climate change mitigation component, the Energy sector considers all processes involving the extraction and exploitation of primary energy; the conversion of primary energy sources into usable forms, such as refineries and power plants; the transmission and distribution of fuels; and the use of fuels in stationary and mobile applications; emissions arising from combustion activities and as fugitive emissions, or emissions from non-combustion exhaust.

The Industrial Processes sector comprises GHG emissions from the transformation of raw materials by chemical or physical means. This sector encompasses a wide range of industries, including cement and lime production, chemical manufacturing, metallurgy, and the use of substitutes for ozone-depleting substances.

On the other hand, GHG emissions from the agricultural sector are related to farming and livestock activities. Specifically, these emissions come from methanogenesis produced by microorganisms in the digestive process of livestock, as well as from the application of organic inputs and nitrogen fertilizers to the soil in crops.

The LULUCF sector is the only one that reports both GHG emissions and removals. Emissions are reported primarily due to a decrease in the national forest area, while removals result from the conservation or increase of forest area through forest recovery or reforestation processes.

Another sector considered in the mitigation component is the Waste sector, which encompasses all activities related to the generation, management, and final disposal of solid and liquid waste, from waste production to its treatment and elimination.

From the point of view of the greenhouse gas emissions generated by the country, it should be noted that the total emissions of the INGEI to the year 2022 amount to 88,262.87 kt of CO2-eq, of which the Energy sector generates the largest contribution with 47.22% (41,674.68 kt CO2-eq) of said emissions, followed by the LUTU-UTS sector16, with 29.26% (25,823.20 kt CO2-eq) and the Agriculture sector, with 13.29% (11,728.67 kt CO2-eq). Similarly, groups and human settlements vulnerable to extreme weather events would see increased risk factors due to increasingly frequent and intense extreme precipitation and temperatures.

In the health sector, increases in diseases and epidemics have been observed, exacerbated by climate change, as an expansion in the distribution of disease vectors is expected, which would adapt to new altitudinal zones (increasingly higher elevations).

In summary, the climate change mitigation component addresses: Energy, Industrial Processes, Agriculture, Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF), and Waste.

The climate change adaptation component includes: Natural Heritage, Water Heritage, Health, Human Settlements, Productive and Strategic Sectors; and Food Sovereignty, Agriculture and Livestock, Aquaculture and Fisheries.

Ecuador's Second NDC considers these 11 sectors and proposes mitigation action lines and adaptation measures, differentiating between two scenarios:

  • Unconditional scenario: This refers to the commitments and actions that a country is willing to implement using its own resources and capabilities.

  • Conditional scenario: Reflects the need for additional support for the country to increase the ambition of its NDCs. It corresponds to the actions a country is willing to take if it receives financial support, capacity building, and/or technology transfer provided by international cooperation.

1.8 Information on how national circumstances affect GHG emissions and removals over time

The sectors prioritized for climate change management have a direct and indirect impact on both emissions and removals. Therefore, the impact of each activity on GHG emissions is detailed below.

Energy: Greenhouse gas emissions from the energy sector are directly related to fossil fuel combustion activities and fugitive emissions from fossil fuel manufacturing. According to Ecuador's National Energy Balance (NEB), which is the main source of information for INGEI in this sector, in 2022 total primary energy production was 203 million Barrels of Oil Equivalent (BOE), of which 86.4% corresponded to oil production (including exports), 9.1% to renewable energy production (hydropower, firewood, sugarcane products, wind power, photovoltaics, and biogas), and 4.5% to natural gas production.

Total secondary energy production reached 80.4 million BOE, broken down into electricity, diesel, gasoline and naphtha, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), fuel oil, and non-energy products. Regarding the electricity generation mix, 75% corresponds to renewable energy (including hydroelectric), 23.6% to thermal sources, and 1.4% to interconnection with Peru and Colombia.

Regarding energy consumption by sector, in 2022, the transportation subsector was identified as the largest energy consumer, accounting for 49.1% of the total energy produced in the country. This was followed by the industrial and residential sectors with 17.9% and 13.1%, respectively.

Lower levels of energy consumption are found in the commercial and public service sectors (6.1%), self-consumption (energy used for processes in power plants, refineries, pipelines, among others) at 4.3%, and agriculture, fishing and mining at 1.2%19.

According to the 2022 National Energy and Gas Index (INGEI), the Energy sector emitted 41,674.68 kt of CO2-eq, representing 47.22% of the national total.

Industrial Processes: The main sources of emissions in this sector come from industrial processes that transform raw materials by chemical or physical means. This sector encompasses a wide range of industries, including cement and lime production, chemical manufacturing, metallurgy, and the use of substitutes for ozone-depleting substances. In 2020, the Manufacturing and Construction industries accounted for 17% of total Gross Value Added (GVA).

In the context of Ecuador, the processing of raw materials into finished products has an impact on GHG emissions. For example, in cement manufacturing, the process of calcining limestone to produce clinker is highly energy-intensive and releases large quantities of CO2 into the atmosphere. Furthermore, some processes in the manufacture of ceramics, iron, and glass can generate greenhouse gases as byproducts, as can the use of products as substitutes for ozone-depleting substances. In this regard, the Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) sector emitted 4,245.78 kt of CO2-eq in 2022, representing 4.81% of the national total.

Agriculture: Emissions from the agricultural sector are related to farming and livestock activities, specifically emissions that come mostly from methanogenesis processes derived from microorganisms in the digestive process of livestock and from the application of organic inputs and nitrogen fertilizers to the soil of agricultural crops.

Agricultural and livestock management activities in Ecuador are crucial to the country's economy. The agricultural sector's share of real GDP reached 7.5% in 2022. The agricultural sector in the country is mainly concentrated in rural areas, where the businesses, associations, and families dedicated to agricultural production are located. Regarding livestock farming, it is related to low levels of productive efficiency (6.03 liters/cow/day and 30-36 months of fattening), occupying large areas of land, with poorly utilized pastures and CO2-eq emissions per unit of milk or meat inversely proportional to the level of productivity.

In this regard, in 2022 the Agriculture sector was responsible for 11,728.67 kt of CO2-eq, representing 13.29% of total emissions.

Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry:

Over the past two decades, the Land Use, Land-Use Change, and Forestry (LULUCF) sector in Ecuador has undergone significant transformations, influenced by a complex interplay of socioeconomic, political, and environmental factors. Deforestation has been a persistent challenge, driven primarily by the expansion of agricultural and livestock frontiers. According to information from the Ministry of Environment, Water, and Ecological Transition, between 1990 and 2022, 2.4 million hectares of native forest were lost in the country, representing an annual deforestation rate of approximately 0.63%.

Despite these challenges, Ecuador has implemented flagship programs such as the Socio Bosque Project and the National Forest Restoration Plan 2019-2030, which contribute to the conservation, reforestation, and restoration of ecosystems. Sustainable forest management has gained ground through initiatives that promote forest certification and the empowerment of indigenous and local communities in the management of their territories. In parallel, Ecuador has actively participated in international initiatives such as the Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation (REDD+) mechanism, seeking to channel climate finance due to the positive results in reducing emissions from the forestry sector and to ensure the sustainability of conservation measures and actions. The country's system of protected areas has expanded with the creation of new national parks and ecological reserves.

This expansion has been accompanied by improvements in forest monitoring systems, leveraging technologies such as satellite imagery, which has allowed for more effective control and management of forest resources. In 2022, the LULUCF sector reported 25,823.20 kt CO2-eq, representing 29.26% of the national total.

Waste: Waste generation is closely linked to urbanization trends and consumption patterns. The growth of cities, along with changes in consumption habits, has increased the amount of waste produced, wastewater discharges, and consequently, GHG emissions from the decomposition of organic matter. However, it is important to note that local conditions and public policies related to waste management also play a fundamental role in this dynamic.

According to statistics from the Environmental and Economic Information in Decentralized Autonomous Municipal Governments (GADM), published in December 2023, the average per capita production of solid waste in urban areas is 0.9 kg/day; of the total Municipal Solid Waste (MSW), 54.9% corresponds to organic waste; 34.5% of the GADMs have implemented or maintain source separation processes; and, on average, the country collects 14,394 tons of MSW per day.

The country's climate zones, population data, per capita solid waste production, solid waste collected, and final waste disposal significantly impact GHG emissions. In this regard, in 2022 the Waste sector was responsible for 4,790.54 kt of CO2-eq, representing 5.43% of total emissions.

2.2 Legal Framework

Ecuador has established a solid legal and political framework to address the challenges of climate change. The 2008 Constitution of the Republic of Ecuador recognizes the importance of protecting the environment and guarantees the right of people to live in a healthy environment. The Constitution also recognizes Ecuador as an intercultural and plurinational state and establishes several collective rights for indigenous peoples and nationalities.

Thus, Article 14 recognizes the right of the population to live in a healthy and ecologically balanced environment that guarantees sustainability and well-being (Sumak Kawsay), and declares the preservation of the environment, the conservation of ecosystems, biodiversity and the integrity of the country's genetic heritage, the prevention of environmental damage and the recovery of degraded natural spaces to be in the public interest.

According to Article 15, the State shall promote, in the public and private sectors, the use of environmentally clean technologies and non-polluting, low-impact alternative energies.

On the other hand, Article 389 of the Constitution establishes that the State shall protect individuals, communities, and nature from the negative effects of disasters of natural or anthropogenic origin through risk prevention, disaster mitigation, and the recovery and improvement of social, economic, and environmental conditions, with the objective of minimizing vulnerability.

Article 413 states that the State shall promote energy efficiency, the development and use of environmentally clean and sound practices and technologies, as well as renewable, diversified, low-impact energies that do not jeopardize food sovereignty, the ecological balance of ecosystems, or the right to water.

Article 414 of the Constitution states that the State shall adopt appropriate and cross-cutting measures for the mitigation of climate change, by limiting greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation and air pollution; it shall take measures for the conservation of forests and vegetation and protect the population at risk.

Ecuador has developed a set of public policies and regulations to address climate change. Through Executive Decrees, climate change adaptation and mitigation have been declared a State policy, and the country has the Inter-institutional Committee on Climate Change (CICC), created by Executive Decree No. 495 in 2010 and reformed in 2017 by Executive Decree No. 064, as a collegiate body responsible for coordinating and implementing the actions necessary for climate change management at the national level.

The Organic Environmental Code, published in Official Gazette Supplement 983 of April 12, 2017, in Book IV, aims to: establish the legal and institutional framework for the planning, articulation, coordination, and monitoring of public policies aimed at designing, managing, and implementing climate change adaptation and mitigation actions at the local, regional, and national levels in a cross-cutting, timely, effective, participatory, coordinated, and articulated manner, in accordance with international instruments ratified by the State and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities. National policies in this area will be designed to prevent and respond to the effects of climate change and will contribute to global efforts to address this anthropogenic phenomenon.

This regulation is extensively developed in the Regulations of the Organic Environmental Code, published by Executive Decree 752 of the Official Registry Supplement 507 of June 12, 2019.

Ecuador also recognizes, through its Organic Code of Planning and Public Finance (COPFP)25, that the design and implementation of public investment programs and projects will promote the incorporation of actions favorable to the ecosystem, mitigation and adaptation to climate change, and the management of vulnerabilities and natural and anthropogenic risks.

Likewise, the National Development Plan (PND) is the highest instrument of national planning, establishing the political and administrative guidelines for designing and implementing public policy in Ecuador. In this sense, the Development Plan for the New Ecuador 2024-2025 is aligned with the 2030 Agenda and its 17 Sustainable Development Goals. It should be noted that the Development Plan establishes the axes, policies, strategies, and goals that guide government management.

The National Development Plan proposes several actions related to climate change. Under its "Infrastructure, Energy, and Environment" pillar, policy 7.4 seeks to "Conserve and restore renewable terrestrial and marine natural resources, promoting sustainable, low-emission development models resilient to the adverse effects of climate change" through strategy b) "Promote climate change management with actions on the ground in the components of adaptation, mitigation, and production; and sustainable development within the prioritized sectors."

Climate change is also considered in policy 7.5 "Promote the integration of environmental management, climate change and disaster risk reduction" through strategy a) "Articulate climate change adaptation measures, considering sustainability criteria, in coordination with competent actors, and contributing from disaster risk reduction."

Likewise, policy 7.7 "Promote the comprehensive and integrated management of water resources and their conservation, fostering the human right to drinking water in sufficient quantity and quality, and its sanitation; as well as irrigation and drainage in an environment adaptive to the effects of climate change" states in its strategy a) "Promote the comprehensive and sustainable management of water resources, in all their uses and exploitations, with the identification and establishment of preventive guarantees and forms of conservation of the public water domain."

Additionally, planning instruments have been developed such as the National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC), the National Adaptation Plan, the National Mitigation Plan, the REDD+ Action Plan (PA REDD+)26, the National Climate Finance Strategy and Ecuador's First Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC).

The National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC) 2012-2025, published through Ministerial Agreement 95 in the Official Gazette, Special Edition 9, on June 17, 2013, is the document that establishes the priority sectors for adaptation (Food Sovereignty, Agriculture, Livestock, Aquaculture and Fisheries; Productive and Strategic Sectors; Health; Water Resources; Natural Resources; Priority Groups; Human Settlements; and Risk Management) and mitigation of climate change (Agriculture; Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry; Energy; Solid and Liquid Waste Management; and Industrial Processes).

Ecuador presented its First Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 2020-2025 in March 2019, and it was declared a mandatory State policy in August of the same year through Executive Decree 840. During the process of strengthening the First NDC, a mechanism was developed to include new contributions that can help achieve the stated goals. This tool, published through Ministerial Agreement MAATE-MAATE-2024-040, will serve as a basis for future NDCs.

The political decision-making body is the Inter-institutional Committee on Climate Change (CICC). This is the political body that directs climate change management at the national level within the framework of current international agreements on the subject. In accordance with current regulations, it is composed of national authorities from the following sectors: environment, foreign affairs, agriculture, electricity and renewable energy, hydrocarbons, industry and productivity, economy and finance, water, risk management, transportation and public works, national planning, research, science, technology, and innovation. Representatives from the Association of Ecuadorian Municipalities, the Consortium of Provincial Governments of Ecuador, and the National Council of Rural Parish Governments of Ecuador participate as observers, as well as other public or private entities related to climate change management, as special guests upon request.

Additionally, the fight against climate change in Ecuador responds to the international instruments ratified by the country, including the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification, the Convention on Biological Diversity, the Paris Agreement, the Sendai Framework, the 2030 Agenda and the Sustainable Development Goals, which form the framework under which Ecuador designs and implements a series of actions in this area, including its NDCs.

By submitting its Second Nationally Determined Contribution, the country contributes to achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement and reaffirms its commitment to reducing its emissions and increasing its resilience to the adverse effects of climate change, taking into account its national circumstances and respecting the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.

Regarding adaptation to climate change and in accordance with Section II, paragraph B of Decision 1/CMA.5—Results of the First Global Stocktake and Decision 2/CMA.5, Ecuador bases the formulation of its Second NDC on the provisions of its First National Adaptation Plan, recognizing the importance of contributing to the country's national policies that guide climate change management. These efforts are reflected in the Second National Communication on Adaptation, which corresponds to the adaptation component of the Second NDC.

The adaptation measures proposed in the prioritized sectors align with the adaptation cycle, considering it as an iterative process that includes risk assessment and analysis, planning, implementation, monitoring, evaluation, and learning. These elements will increase resilience and reduce vulnerability to the adverse effects of climate change. Regarding means of implementation, Ecuador's Second NDC, formulated in accordance with the National Climate Finance Strategy, presents an initial estimate of the required financing, as well as the needs in terms of technology transfer and capacity building. It has also established a set of initial measures while the conceptual framework for loss and damage attributed to climate change is being developed, adjusted to the country's national circumstances.

The formulation of the Second NDC considers international cooperation as a key element to achieve the proposed goals and, to that end, establishes actions under a conditional scenario subject to the provision of resources from international cooperation.

In line with the Global Climate Balance, Ecuador considers its Second NDC more ambitious than its predecessor, including new aspects, methodologies, and components that aim to guide the country's climate management, including the institutional arrangements for preparing and implementing its NDCs.

Ecuador has formulated its second climate commitment in a coordinated and participatory manner with all stakeholders, in accordance with the timelines established by the UNFCCC. This participatory process, developed through several workshops at the territorial level, included representatives from diverse sectors, levels of government, academia, the private sector, and civil society, including Indigenous peoples and nationalities, women, and youth, reflecting gender, intercultural, and intergenerational perspectives.

The Second NDC uses the latest information available in the Fifth National Communication on Climate Change and the First Biennial Transparency Report in light of its national circumstances.

During its formulation process, collaborative work was carried out with sectoral actors, such as information providers and technical validators, who contributed to the development of the different components of the Second NDC.

The elements mentioned above have been considered by Ecuador to demonstrate the increase in climate ambition in its Second NDC, in line with what is established by the UNFCCC in the Global Climate Balance and the Paris Agreement.

3. Description of general objectives of the Second NDC - implementation of the objectives of the Convention and the Paris Agreement

Climate change represents one of the most urgent and complex challenges facing humanity in the 21st century. This global crisis not only affects weather patterns but also has profound repercussions for biodiversity, food security, and economic development. According to Article 2 of the UNFCCC, the primary objective is to stabilize greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that avoids dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system. This level should be achieved in a manner that allows for the natural adaptation of ecosystems, ensures food production, and enables sustainable economic development.

In this context, Ecuador, as a member of the UNFCCC, has ratified the Paris Agreement to support global efforts in the fight against climate change. With this background, Ecuador's Second NDC 2026-2035 reflects a renewed and expanded commitment to addressing climate change through the implementation of concrete policies and actions.

The overall objective of Ecuador's Second NDC is to implement policies, actions, and efforts that promote the reduction of greenhouse gases, increased resilience, and decreased vulnerability to the adverse effects of climate change in the priority sectors of the current National Climate Change Strategy, through a joint effort among sectoral entities of the Central Government, Local Governments, the Private Sector, Academia, and Civil Society. These actions and efforts will be guided by the strategic lines and measures identified in the subsequent sections of this document.

Ecuador's Second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) represents a significant step forward in the fight against climate change, aligning its mitigation and adaptation objectives with the global goals of the Paris Agreement, as well as addressing climate-related loss and damage. Through a combination of emissions reduction, resilience building, addressing the impacts of extreme and slow-onset events associated with climate change, and resource mobilization, Ecuador positions itself as a committed actor in the pursuit of low-emission, climate-resilient sustainable development. This comprehensive approach is fundamental to meeting the challenges of climate change and ensuring a viable future for present and future generations.

Through the implementation of its Second NDC, Ecuador seeks to contribute to the overall objective of the Paris Agreement, as well as the specific objectives established in Article 2 related to: a) limiting the increase in global temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, b) increasing the ability to adapt to the adverse impacts of climate change and enhancing food resilience in a way that does not threaten food production, and c) making financial flows consistent with low-emission and climate-resilient development.

Globally, regarding mitigation, countries have generally agreed to reach peak greenhouse gas emissions "as soon as possible," recognizing that peak emissions will take longer for developing countries. Ecuador continues its efforts toward this goal through its Second NDC.

Regarding the general framework for adaptation, the Paris Agreement establishes an overall objective of improving adaptive capacity, strengthening resilience, and reducing vulnerability to climate change, thereby contributing to sustainable development.

Ecuador prioritizes this objective, as a developing country, and has therefore decided to ratify the adaptation component of the Second NDC as the Second Adaptation Communication, taking into account that adaptation is the country's priority, as well as the need for developed countries to channel financial resources for adaptation in vulnerable countries like Ecuador.

4. Mitigation Component

Description of the approach adopted

Ecuador is committed to combating climate change through the implementation of initiatives and actions that reduce greenhouse gas emissions, enabling sustainable economic development in accordance with national circumstances and in line with the international principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.

The specific mitigation objective for the Second NDC is to contribute to global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by identifying sectors, lines of action, and initiatives that contribute to climate change mitigation in the country. Through these actions, the aim is to support the implementation of Article 2(a) of the Paris Agreement, which stipulates: Hold the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, recognizing that this would substantially reduce the risks and impacts of climate change.

To support the achievement of this global goal, Ecuador, through its current climate policy, seeks to promote favorable conditions for the implementation of actions that reduce emissions and increase carbon sinks in priority sectors. Additionally, civil society has demonstrated its interest and commitment to being active agents of climate action, influencing public policy through citizen participation processes.

Within the process of formulating the Second NDC, the mitigation component addressed the five sectors prioritized in Ecuador's National Climate Change Strategy 2012-2025 (ENCC):

  • Energy

  • Industrial Processes

  • Agriculture

  • Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF)

  • Waste29

In its Second NDC, Ecuador proposes several actions that have the potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions across all mitigation sectors: Energy, Industrial Processes, Agriculture, LULUCF, and Waste, taking into account their specific sectoral characteristics.

These actions will be reflected through sectoral lines of action that will contain the initiatives for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and will contribute directly to monitoring and achieving the mitigation goal.

Lines of action: These are conceived as strategies for guiding and organizing different initiatives, in such a way as to promote the articulation, integration, and continuity of efforts to support climate change mitigation.

Initiatives: These are identified plans, programs, projects, actions, and measures that contribute to mitigating climate change.

The actions of this component respond to the scenarios described in section 1.7 and refer to a country's own efforts (unconditional scenario) and the results that could be obtained with the support of international cooperation (conditional scenario).

The potential for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in the Energy, Industrial Processes, Agriculture, LULUCF, and Waste sectors is established in comparison to the business-as-usual scenario.

The cross-cutting themes, included voluntarily in the Second NDC, are: gender, intergenerationality, and interculturality. These aim to reduce gaps with priority groups and involve women and men from civil society, community and private organizations, and representatives of different age groups. Both the cross-cutting themes and the principles are geared towards the use of citizen participation mechanisms that promote rights and equal opportunities in developing actions to reduce greenhouse gas emissions based on the differentiated needs of these groups.

The Second NDC also contributes to the implementation of the National Mitigation Plan, which not only aims to reduce emissions but also seeks to reduce poverty, decrease inequality, and advance towards sustainable development in harmony with nature. It promotes a competitive, inclusive, resilient, and low-carbon development model, in accordance with the rights of nature enshrined in the Constitution.

To meet these challenges, the country must use all its national capabilities, and international partnerships will play an essential role in reducing the positive relationship between economic growth and increased GHG emissions.

Climate change presents a multifaceted challenge for Ecuador, primarily affecting sectors such as agriculture, natural heritage, infrastructure, and public health, among others. The country has identified adaptation as a priority to reduce the current and future climate risk to social, economic, and environmental systems vulnerable to the adverse effects of climate change.

5.1 National Circumstances, Institutional Arrangements and National Legal Frameworks

5.1.1 National Circumstances

With each assessment cycle by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the impacts of climate change on countries, especially developing ones, become increasingly evident (IPCC, 2014, 2022). In the case of continental Ecuador, high temperatures, intense rainfall, droughts, and frosts stand out as the most frequent threats. Projections indicate an increase in both the magnitude and frequency of these extreme events under current and future scenarios (MAE, 2019).

Furthermore, social, economic, and environmental inequalities, coupled with fragile governance, especially at the local level, have led Ecuador to be considered a country highly vulnerable to climate change. In this context, the national conditions regarding adaptation are presented, highlighting the factors that make the country particularly susceptible to climate challenges.

A summary that provides context on the progress of actions against the problem of climate change in Ecuador, specifically in relation to adaptation, is shown through the following key aspects, detected during the preparation of the Fifth National Communication on Climate Change and First Biennial Transparency Report34:

The complex interplay between natural and social factors has shaped a scenario in Ecuador where land use has become a pressing challenge. The country's geographic diversity, which endows it with unparalleled biological richness, also exposes it to a particular fragility in the face of the impacts of climate change. The main findings are detailed below:

Land use range

To address the challenges posed by climate change to land use, the General Coordination of National Agricultural Information (CGINA) employed various methodologies, such as map analysis and land cover assessment, along with the productive capacity and agricultural suitability of the land, to report the categorization of land use conflict in Ecuador (MAG, 2021).

This methodology establishes a classification of land use areas based on the differences between current land use and agricultural potential, differentiating between two main groups according to their level of exploitation: underutilized lands and overutilized lands, assigning each level of intensity to mild, moderate, or severe. This categorization is derived from a comparative analysis between current land use and its agricultural suitability, facilitating the identification of areas that show adequate management or discrepancies in their use, thus highlighting specific land use conflicts.

Lands without conflict of use

Areas have been identified where the management of the agroecosystem (an ecosystem modified by human activity for the development of agricultural or livestock activities, made up of interacting abiotic and biotic components) aligns with its land use suitability classification or with compatible practices, without inducing environmental degradation, thus allowing the maintenance of activities in harmony with the intrinsic productive capacity of the land.

Lands in conflict of use due to overutilization

In the Ecuadorian context, it is estimated that approximately 67% of the continental territory has not been significantly altered by human activity, preserving its original vegetation cover. This includes extensive areas of native forest in the Amazon and the western and eastern slopes of the Andes, as well as remnants of natural forests in other regions of the country, shrub and herbaceous vegetation, páramo grasslands, glacial cover, and arid zones. On the other hand, 29.9% of the continental surface area consists of land with total or partial human intervention, representing an area of 7,443,075 hectares.

Water Resource Availability

The hydrography and hydrology of Ecuador are intrinsically linked to the land's relief, rock weathering, climatic conditions, and vegetation cover, with relief and precipitation being the crucial elements in the configuration and density of its hydrographic network. The interaction between rivers, mountains, and rainfall creates a close connection, reflecting Ecuador's unique topography, which gives rise to a wide range of hydrological patterns and a remarkable diversity in surface water systems, which vary according to the country's distinct physical and climatic conditions (Espinosa et al., 2022).

This diversity of water resources brings with it both benefits and challenges. Among the benefits, the abundance of water resources in Ecuador stands out, with an estimated average annual runoff volume of 432 billion m³, and a specific runoff of 1,600 mm per year, a figure that significantly exceeds the global average of approximately 300 mm per year (FLACSO et al., 2008). However, the most notable challenge is related to the potential for soil erosion, exacerbated by excess runoff (Espinosa et al., 2022).

Given this scenario, Ecuador has implemented significant measures to preserve water resources, an initiative that indirectly contributes to the protection of the country's biodiversity and natural resources. One of the most notable actions in this area has been the designation of Water Protection Areas (APH), territories identified as harboring

Water sources of crucial importance for public welfare, focusing on guaranteeing access to drinking water and promoting food sovereignty, as established by the Organic Law of Water Resources, Use and Exploitation of Water (LORHUYA).

Furthermore, it is relevant to point out that, according to the regulations promulgated by LORHUYA, water resource management in the country is structured around hydrographic demarcations. These demarcations, defined by the grouping of hydrographic basins in accordance with Article 7 of the aforementioned regulations, constitute the territorial basis for the administration and jurisdiction over water resources, thus establishing a framework for efficient and sustainable management. In this sense, the Vice-Ministry of Water of the Ministry of Environment, Water and Ecological Transition bases its scope of action on nine Hydrographic Demarcations, thereby outlining the path to follow for the conservation of water resources, including surface and groundwater in Ecuador.

During the period covered by the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, 15 new Protected Areas of Historical Conservation (APH) were added, increasing the protected area by 151,085 ha. This brings the total number of APH to 21, covering an area of 167,615.52 ha reported up to December 2022.

This achievement represents Ecuador's commitment to safeguarding a vital resource like water, covering approximately 0.6% of its continental territory. This effort underscores the country's determination to strengthen climate change adaptation strategies and protect its water and natural heritage for future generations.

Biodiversity

Given the country's rich biodiversity, the National System of Protected Areas (SNAP) registered 76 protected areas as of December 2022, totaling 26,208,785.58 hectares, representing 19.42% of the continental territory designated for conservation under this category (MAATE, 2024). Of this total, a significant 79.68% corresponds to marine protected areas, demonstrating the priority given to marine conservation. Protected areas in the Amazon follow in importance, representing 12.55% of the total. The Insular or Galapagos, Andean, and Coastal regions contribute 3.04%, 2.66%, and 2.08% of the total protected area, respectively, showing a conservation effort distributed across diverse ecosystems.

According to the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, 19 new areas were added to the SNAP, reflecting Ecuador's firm commitment to formulating public policies aimed at addressing the challenges of climate change.

Ecosystem Distribution

Ecuador stands out as a globally important biodiversity hotspot due to its unique density of species relative to its land area and the confluence of ocean currents that enrich its marine and coastal ecosystems. The country's biological richness is manifested in an exuberant variety of terrestrial and marine ecosystems, a product of the interaction between biophysical and biogeographical factors. This uniqueness has led to Ecuador being recognized as one of the 17 most megadiverse countries on the planet, notable for the abundance of species in all taxonomic groups (Iturralde-Pólit et al., 2017; Mittermeier et al., 1997). In 2013, the National Environmental Authority reaffirmed its commitment to the conservation of this natural heritage by presenting the Map of Ecosystems of Continental Ecuador, identifying the existence of 91 distinct ecosystems through an analysis that integrates biophysical and biogeographical variables.

On the other hand, in the Coastal region, 24 types of ecosystems have been identified, distributed across two biogeographical zones with marked differences in floristic composition and bioclimatic characteristics: the humid Chocó region and the predominantly dry Equatorial Pacific region. The Andes Mountains are home to 45 ecosystems distributed across six distinct biogeographical sectors, while in the Amazon, 22 ecosystems have been cataloged, distributed across five biogeographical sectors, highlighting the natural richness of these regions (MAE, 2013).

Furthermore, Ecuador stands out for the rich variety of its marine and coastal ecosystems, boasting 24 of the 27 types recognized worldwide (MAE, 2013). This encompasses 12 coastal ecosystems, including wetlands, cliffs, dunes, mangroves, and more, as well as 12 marine ecosystems ranging from bays to ocean trenches. This impressive ecological diversity, which covers both terrestrial and marine areas, not only highlights Ecuador's natural wealth but also emphasizes its fundamental role in preserving biodiversity globally (Hurtado & Rodriguez, 2012).

In the Galápagos Islands, the distribution and variety of vegetation are directly influenced by precipitation patterns, which has led to the identification of four main bioclimatic zones: coastal, dry, transitional, and humid (MAE, 2013). The smaller, lower-altitude islands exclusively have coastal and dry zones, lacking humid and transitional areas.

Each of these bioclimatic zones harbors unique dominant plant species, which play a crucial role in environmental conservation and the regulation of the water cycle. Although the Galápagos Islands are not characterized by a high diversity of species compared to other places, the high degree of endemism and their well-preserved natural state underscore their ecological and conservation value (Iturralde-Pólit et al., 2017).

5.1.2 Institutional Arrangements

Institutional arrangements determine the roles and responsibilities of the actors involved in adaptation management. These processes are supported by national and international regulatory frameworks that ensure compliance with the commitments made by the country under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC).

Ecuador, through the National Environmental Authority, has sovereignly defined the following institutional arrangements based on what is described in section 2.3.1 of this document:

  • The CICC has the role of intersectoral and intergovernmental coordination and brings together national authorities from different sectors, along with representatives of local governments, for the implementation of adaptation actions.

  • The Ministry of Environment, Water and Ecological Transition, through the Undersecretariat of Climate Change, leads climate change adaptation management in Ecuador, which includes coordinating climate transparency processes.

  • The Climate Change Adaptation Directorate is responsible for promoting the management and implementation of policies, plans, programs, projects, measures, and actions to achieve climate-resilient communities and ecosystems. To this end, it has a technical team that provides advice to different actors and sectors to achieve this goal.

  • Adaptation is the result of joint efforts from different actors representing the National Government, Decentralized Autonomous Governments (GADs), Research Institutes, Academia, International Cooperation, and the Private Sector, who, through the implementation of their actions, contribute to achieving the country's climate goals.

  • In the area of adaptation, local governments play a fundamental role in the implementation and territorialization of the national climate public policy, considering that the impacts of climate risk are most evident in the most vulnerable areas and populations, as defined in the National Adaptation Plan.

5.1.3 National legal frameworks

The Constitution of the Republic of Ecuador is the main resource that has allowed the design and implementation of regulatory frameworks and policies to address climate change in the country, in accordance with the national and international agreements signed within the framework of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC).

With this, the country has strengthened its regulatory framework for adaptation through the Organic Environmental Code (COA) published in 2017 and its Regulations issued in 2019. These instruments, together with the National Climate Change Strategy 2012-2025, establish the importance of formulating and implementing the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan (PNA) as a key instrument to reduce climate risk and increase resilience and adaptive capacity.

Through Ministerial Agreement MAATE-2023-029 of April 12, 2023, the National Climate Change Adaptation Plan 2023-2027 is formalized, with the general objective of contributing to the integration of climate change adaptation into national, local, and sectoral development planning; as well as to the identification and reduction of current and future climate risk to social, economic, and environmental systems vulnerable to the effects of climate change.

The National Climate Change Plan (PNA), in accordance with the National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC), addresses six sectors for climate change management: Natural Heritage, Water Heritage, Health, Human Settlements, Productive and Strategic Sectors, and Food Sovereignty, Agriculture, Livestock, Aquaculture and Fisheries (SAG).

Ecuador's First and Second NDCs are aligned with the national legal framework established for climate change adaptation management.

In the case of the Second NDC 2026-2035, the proposed measures respond to the provisions of the National Adaptation Plan 2023-2027 regarding addressing climate risk and designing measures to cope with the adverse effects of climate change.

5.2 Effects, risks and vulnerability

Ecuador is a country vulnerable to the effects of climate change, as explained in section 1 of this document. Furthermore, the presence of extreme weather events and the intensification of natural climate variability phenomena, such as El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and La Niña, combined with the prevailing geographic, morphological, and demographic characteristics of South America—its geographic location, rugged terrain, and atmospheric conditions—impact the country's development.

According to the Fifth National Communication and First Biennial Transparency Report, it can be inferred that future trends imply, in the medium term, that continental Ecuador will experience many more episodes of high temperatures and intense rainfall, which could lead to an increase of 15 to 20 days above the average for high temperatures and intense rainfall in certain parts of Ecuador by the year 2030.

In line with the above, climate projections play a fundamental role in prioritizing adaptation to the impacts of climate change, presenting a mosaic of typical years based on historically observed climate patterns. These simulations become valuable tools that clarify the influence of changes in atmospheric circulation on local temperature and precipitation patterns.

In addition, the inclusion of ocean projections in Ecuador's National Climate Change Adaptation Plan is fundamental, even though they are not explicitly prioritized in the current adaptation sectors of the PNA. The marine-coastal zones of the Ecuadorian continental shelf and the Galapagos Islands are identified as crucial for the formulation of comprehensive and sustainable strategies.

The oceans, fundamental in regulating the global climate, are reservoirs of much of the additional heat generated by the greenhouse effect and play a vital role in the carbon cycle. Variability in sea surface temperature, acidification, and dissolved oxygen levels have direct and indirect effects on marine biodiversity, food security, and local economies that depend on fishing and tourism. Furthermore, rising sea levels and changes in wave patterns are key factors in land-use planning and coastal infrastructure. Disregarding the ocean could result in a significant underestimation of risks and the implementation of insufficient or inadequate adaptation policies.

Within the framework of the adaptation cycle, climate risk constitutes the first step in defining climate rationality. Ecuador, in its National Adaptation Plan 2023-2027, establishes the methodological levels of climate risk analysis to determine the impact of prioritized sectoral elements, which have served as the basis for the development of the adaptation measures included in the Second NDC.

According to the Plan, the impacts, risks and vulnerabilities for the prioritized sectors are presented:

Impacts, risks and vulnerability

Natural Heritage
Climate change poses serious threats to biodiversity and ecosystems in Ecuador, a country known for its biological richness. According to the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2022), this phenomenon results from the interaction between natural and human factors, underscoring the need to address it from a comprehensive perspective. The accelerated loss of biodiversity, exacerbated by climate change, can destabilize ecosystems and alter species distribution, as demonstrated by Pearce-Higgins et al. (2015) and Freeman et al. (2018). Ecuador's National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC) prioritizes the conservation and sustainable management of natural heritage, recognizing the urgency of protecting species and their ecosystems.

A crucial approach in assessing climate change risks in Ecuador is the analysis of vascular plants. These constitute the dominant structure in most terrestrial ecosystems and are key indicators for understanding the impacts of climate change on biodiversity (Pearson et al., 2019). Many of these species are endemic to Ecuador, highlighting their importance in assessing extinction risks under different climate scenarios.

To model the impacts of climate change on species distribution, methodologies such as ecological niche models were used with the MaxEnt software, which allowed for projecting the environmental suitability of 606 species under current and future climatic conditions (2020-2050). This analysis included key climatic variables, such as the minimum temperature in the coldest season and precipitation in the driest season, which are fundamental for modeling the impacts of climate change on the geographic distribution of species. These models help identify critical areas for conservation, taking into account current biodiversity and climate change projections.

The results of the Climate Risk Analysis (CRA) reveal that, under assumptions of limited dispersal, many endemic species could face a significant reduction in their distribution areas, increasing their risk of extinction. Furthermore, the assessment shows that 57.3% of species are distributed in areas that constitute less than 20% of Ecuadorian territory, underscoring their vulnerability. Therefore, areas of high species concentration in the Northern Andes and the highlands of the northwestern Amazon are identified as crucial.

Conservation strategies should prioritize the expansion of protected areas and the creation of ecological corridors that facilitate species mobility in the face of climate change. Planning should be based on scientific evidence and climate projections to ensure that species can adapt to new habitats. This methodology allows Ecuador to strengthen the resilience of its biodiversity and develop effective conservation strategies to address future climate challenges.

In conclusion, the climate risk analysis for the Natural Heritage Sector highlights the need for immediate action to adapt to the adverse effects of climate change. Biodiversity conservation and strategic planning are fundamental to protecting the country's ecosystems and ensuring their long-term sustainability.

Water Heritage

The management and conservation of water resources in Ecuador has become a priority in the face of the adverse effects of climate change. According to the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, greater water variability is projected, which will generate both water scarcity and excess in different regions. This variability represents a challenge to food security, human health, and biodiversity. In response, Ecuador has adopted a comprehensive approach that prioritizes water use for human consumption, agriculture for food sovereignty, ecological flow, and productive activities, as established by the Organic Law of Water Resources, Uses, and Exploitation.

To assess climate risks to water resources, Ecuador uses advanced models such as the Soil & Water Assessment Tool (SWAT), which allows for the simulation of future climate change scenarios and the projection of impacts on the hydrological cycle. The methodology applied in the analyses includes the selection and prioritization of river basins, and the calibration of the SWAT model with current hydrological data to project future variations in water availability.

This rigorous approach has identified four priority areas: the Esmeraldas, Jubones, Guayas, and Pastaza rivers, although the latter was excluded due to a lack of data. The results of the analysis show that, under certain scenarios, these basins face risks such as increased flow rates, greater sediment production, and prolonged periods of water scarcity. This poses challenges for water management, especially in productive areas and areas vulnerable to erosion.

For example, in the Jubones River Basin, sediment production is estimated to increase by 12.7%, both under future climate change conditions and with changes in land use, with Type Year 4 showing the highest production with increases of 206%.

Ecuador has the necessary tools and data to implement adaptation strategies that ensure the sustainability of its water resources and reduce vulnerability to extreme weather events, promoting proactive and evidence-based management.

Health
Climate change has a direct and indirect impact on human health, exacerbating pre-existing problems such as access to water, sanitation, and nutrition, while favoring the spread of vector-borne diseases like dengue. In Ecuador, vulnerability to this disease is influenced by factors such as geography, socioeconomic status, and urban poverty, where the lack of adequate sanitation contributes to the proliferation of the Aedes aegypti mosquito, the vector of dengue, Zika, and other diseases. Climate change is increasing environmental suitability for dengue transmission in the country, favoring its spread in areas where it was previously uncommon. All four dengue virus serotypes circulate in Ecuador, exacerbating the risks to the population. Significant outbreaks, such as the 2015 outbreak with 40,000 cases, demonstrate the magnitude of the problem. Surveillance and vector control are essential to mitigate these risks.

Predictive models, such as Sibila®, were used to assess the environmental suitability for dengue in Ecuador, based on climatic, environmental, entomological, and epidemiological factors. This approach allowed for the projection of future scenarios, showing that areas such as Guayaquil, Portoviejo, and Manta will experience changes in the seasonality and magnitude of dengue cases. Spatiotemporal risk patterns were identified that will help in implementing more effective preventive measures.

In conclusion, the Health Sector analysis highlights the need for a comprehensive and adaptable approach to dengue prevention and control, based on a combination of surveillance strategies, health promotion, service provision, community education, and inter-institutional and inter-sectoral coordination. Furthermore, the models help in making timely decisions about when and where to implement control strategies, based on seasonality and climatic conditions, with the aim of reducing the impacts of dengue in the context of a changing climate.

Human Settlements

The climate risk analysis for the Human Settlements sector focused on the analysis of exposure and vulnerability to floods and landslides in seven selected cities in Ecuador: Daule, Ventanas, Chone and Vinces in the Coastal region; Guaranda and Sangolquí in the Sierra region; and El Coca in the Amazon.

The methodology employed includes the selection of cities, the use of hydrological and hydraulic models to simulate floods and determine affected areas, as well as the use of machine learning to identify areas vulnerable to landslides. In addition, different future climate scenarios were considered to assess the evolution of risk over time.

The results of the climate risk study of the prioritized Human Settlements sector show the clear need for the development and updating of technical tools at the national level to facilitate the incorporation of climate change criteria into local planning instruments. This will provide municipalities with the necessary tools to formulate their development and land-use plans, land use and management plans, and complementary instruments that lead to the implementation of adaptation measures to reduce the impact of climate threats and the effects of climate change in their territories, especially for the most vulnerable populations.

The findings reveal high vulnerability in coastal and Amazonian cities, which are more susceptible to flooding. In contrast, cities in the Andes face a greater risk of landslides. Furthermore, the report underscores the urgent need to implement adaptation measures in the assessed cities. The creation of early warning systems, improved land-use planning, and the construction of resilient infrastructure are proposed as key strategies to mitigate the risks associated with climate change.

In conclusion, the study highlights that climate change exacerbates the risks of floods and landslides in human settlements in Ecuador. It is essential to integrate climate risk into urban planning and strengthen the capacity of communities to respond to extreme events.

Productive and Strategic Sectors

The Productive and Strategic Sectors (PPEE) are strategic subsectors in which planned inter-institutional efforts are foreseen to increase and strengthen the adaptive capacity and resilience of the subsectors (Hydroelectric Power Plants and Projects, Road Infrastructure, and Hydrocarbons).

In terms of climate change adaptation, when the National Adaptation Plan (PNA) (2023-2027) refers to productive and strategic infrastructure, it includes hydroelectric generation infrastructure (hydroelectric plants and projects), as well as major state highways and the Trans-Ecuadorian Pipeline System (SOTE). These are high-priority systems for the country, as they have a direct impact on its productivity, connectivity, and economy. Therefore, understanding the effects of climate change, such as landslides and floods, on these systems is crucial for adaptation processes.

The models selected and used to evaluate these were differentiated according to the impact. For floods, the HEC-RAS hydrological model and the HEC-HMS hydraulic model were chosen, and for landslides, the heuristic model was established as the most suitable. Each of the subsectors is described below:

Road infrastructure enables the transport of people, goods, and services, facilitating connections between different regions and productive sectors. However, this infrastructure faces a series of extreme weather events such as intense rainfall, frost, and high temperatures, which can severely affect road infrastructure, increasing the frequency and magnitude of impacts such as floods and landslides. These events disrupt connectivity and jeopardize road safety. For this subsector of road infrastructure, 27% of the prioritized road system has been modeled.

For the modeling of landslides and floods in the main roads and hydrocarbons subsectors, trends and extreme values from the historical series (1985-2015) and climatic conditions were analyzed using weather tables (ATs), which will serve as input for flood and landslide modeling. The analysis was performed using maps of the number of days with precipitation exceeding the 95th percentile at each prioritized site. Subsequently, hydrological modeling was applied using the HEC-HMS model to calculate peak flows and runoff at different return periods of 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 years.

The modeling results show increased precipitation along most sections of the road infrastructure, with some areas experiencing reduced precipitation (some sectors of the Los Ríos and Santa Elena provinces). Under climate change scenarios, the magnitude and frequency of extreme landslide events will progressively increase.

The hydrocarbon sector is of vital strategic importance, being the main contributor to Ecuador's Gross Domestic Product (GDP). In recent years, this strategic infrastructure has been affected by climate change, from extraction to downstream operations such as storage, transportation, distribution, and refining. Given these challenges, it is crucial to implement adaptation and resilience measures in the hydrocarbon infrastructure to mitigate the negative impacts of climate change and ensure its long-term sustainability. The SOTE pipeline represents the main contributor to Ecuador's GDP, through a 497.7 km long pipeline that crosses the country with a capacity to transport 360,000 barrels of crude oil per day and carries approximately 70% of the oil extracted in an area at high risk of biophysical impacts.

As a result of modeling the potential effects, a 7% to 38% increase in precipitation over a 10- to 100-year return period is expected to result in a 7% to 42% increase in river flows in the Amazon basins. For basins in the coastal zone, even greater increases in precipitation, ranging from 66% to 119%, are expected, which will increase peak flows by 19% to 64%.

Regarding landslides in the hydrocarbon subsector, the El Reventador area is highly susceptible to landslides, and with the current erosion conditions, it is necessary to consider relocating the SOTE infrastructure due to the imminent risk of a landslide.

Hydroelectric power plants are the most important component of Ecuador's electrical infrastructure. On average, they contribute around 80% of the country's electricity production. However, these plants face increasing vulnerability due to the effects of climate change. Variability in the quantity and quality of water available in river basins, caused by extreme weather events such as droughts and heavy rainfall, can affect the power generation capacity of this strategic infrastructure. Ten hydroelectric power plants and projects with a total capacity of 4,621.40 MW were analyzed for the ARC.

For this subsector, the current and future climate was initially characterized using information from general circulation models and regional climate change models for the periods 2011–2040 and 2041–2070. The generated models correspond to the IPCC-AR5 scenarios: RCP2.6 (optimistic), RCP4.5 (intermediate), RCP6.0 (intermediate), and RCP8.5 (pessimistic). Subsequently, the SWAT hydrological model was defined, whose information requirements involved the use of precipitation and temperature data, as well as potential anthropogenic pressures that influence vegetation cover and land use, which define the generation of streamflow and sediment under future climatic conditions.

Finally, in the hydroelectric subsector, it is inferred that under possible future climatic conditions, the hydroelectric plants located west of the Andes Mountains will experience a considerable reduction in energy generation.

Food Sovereignty, Agriculture, Livestock, Aquaculture and Fisheries

This paper addresses the importance of plant growth models for analyzing crop development and productivity in Ecuador within the context of climate change. These models are essential for assessing climate impacts on food production, bridging information gaps on crop yields, and designing adaptation strategies. Calibrating these models is fundamental to ensuring that their simulations reflect real-world conditions, which is achieved by adjusting specific parameters for each region.

To assess the impacts of climate change on six crops of national interest, the Environmental Policy Integrated Climate (EPIC) model was used, which simulates crop phenology and its interaction with climatic variables. The Agroecological Zoning (ZAE) methodology was also applied to determine the best crop options based on land suitability, combining climatic information and soil characteristics.

The results showed that the impact of climate change on agricultural yields varies by crop and region. For example, yellow dent corn experiences yield reductions due to heat stress, while sugarcane shows improved yields in certain areas. Land suitability analysis reveals that, by 2050, rice and sugarcane crops could suffer widespread losses in suitability, increasing production costs.

The study concludes that, although there is potential for positive changes in agricultural performance, it is crucial to implement agricultural policies that promote productive efficiency and the conservation of natural resources, avoiding the expansion of the agricultural frontier.

Adaptation to climate change is essential to ensuring the sustainability of agriculture in Ecuador, along with actions that promote family farming.

    6. Loss and Damage Component

    Loss and Damage (L&D) attributed to climate change is becoming increasingly important, considering that countries will be affected despite multiple mitigation or adaptation efforts.

    The definition of the terms loss and damage as such is still under discussion in the international arena. In general, P&D can be understood as the negative consequences caused by the threats and impacts of climate change on environmental, social, and economic systems, despite the mitigation and adaptation efforts that countries may undertake. In this sense, the report of Working Group II of the IPCC on Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability (part of the Sixth Assessment Report) includes the following key messages:

    1) Loss and damage have already been suffered; 2) Future loss and damage will occur with increased global warming; 3) Loss and damage are inevitable and unevenly distributed; and, 4) Loss and damage are not comprehensively addressed through financial, governmental, and institutional arrangements.

    Despite its voluntary mitigation efforts and the implementation of its adaptation measures, Ecuador is working on developing mechanisms and actions to address the losses and damages attributed to climate change. The first step in this process is to establish a conceptual framework tailored to the country's national circumstances. Ecuador is currently working on this.

    Under this context, in a first effort, Ecuador has made an approach to addressing loss and damage by focusing its planning on the following actions established by the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)41:

    • Avert (Averting): to warn of the potential losses and damages caused by climate change.

    • Minimizing: actions to reduce losses and damages associated with climate change.

    • Addressing loss and damage: involves recognizing, addressing, and taking action against loss and damage caused by climate change.

    The scheme for addressing loss and damage is based on the following diagram:

    To address losses and damages, the timing of actions in response to events, response times, and who assumes the risk must be considered. This allows for appropriate actions to be taken to address the problem on a timescale, focusing on actions before (t-1), after the specific event has occurred at time (t), and after the event (t+1 and t+2).

    Time scale versus events

    1. Pre-event (t-1): Up to this point, the potential risk has been assessed and strategies implemented to reduce it. The aim is to avoid (averting) and minimize losses and damages from the negative impacts of extreme events (heavy rainfall, drought, frost, etc.) and slow-onset events (glacial retreat, sea level rise, biodiversity loss). The challenge at this stage is attributing these events to climate change. Up to this point, the potential risk has been assessed and strategies implemented to reduce it.

    2. Event (t): During the occurrence of an event where losses and damages exceed the adaptation limit, that is, are unavoidable, systems require a short-term response: within hours and days. To attribute losses and damages, scenarios experiencing climate risk were determined, such as strategic sectors for climate change adaptation.

    3. Post-event response (t+1): The approach to Loss and Damage is to address, through action measures, the medium- and long-term response to the event, leading to the development of initiatives leveraged by some financial instrument and that respond to climate rationality42.

    4. Post-event recovery (t+2): on the timescale for climate change events are the remediation actions implemented to recover systems affected by climate change in the long term.

    The different measures to address the issue are implemented simultaneously, which requires coordination and collaboration between different actors and sectors, to give way to effective proposals. Despite implementing mitigation actions to avoid losses and adaptation measures to reduce them, losses and damages still occur. Therefore, it remains to respond to or address these losses, and this is the point on which the country will focus its efforts in the period 2026-2035.

    Avoiding, minimizing, and addressing loss and damage involves protecting and strengthening the resilience of communities, livelihoods, and ecosystems to climate change, ensuring they are safeguarded for future generations.

    6.1 Measures to address loss and damage attributed to climate change

    In this context, the country plans the following measures based on UNFCCC reports and publications43 44 :

    Rebuild

    Reconstruction refers to the process of restoring or recovering something that has been damaged or destroyed, with the aim of returning it to its original state or improving it. In the context of climate change and natural disasters, reconstruction focuses on the recovery of infrastructure, housing, basic service systems, and communities that have suffered damage due to extreme events such as floods, landslides, or other adverse weather phenomena.

    Reconstruction is a comprehensive process that seeks to restore daily life and infrastructure to affected communities after a disaster, with the aim of improving resilience and reducing vulnerability to future events.

    Rehabilitate

    This refers to the restoration of natural or built systems that have suffered damage as a result of extreme weather events or slow-onset events. This measure seeks to restore these systems to their functionality and capacity to provide vital services to the affected communities.

    In the context of Loss and Damage, rehabilitation can include a variety of actions, such as the restoration of degraded ecosystems, the reconstruction of damaged infrastructure, the rehabilitation of affected agricultural land, and the recovery of homes and livelihoods for people affected by extreme weather events.

    Rehabilitation not only addresses the immediate impacts of climate disasters, but also seeks to strengthen the resilience of communities to face similar future events. This may involve implementing adaptation measures and integrating climate risk considerations into long-term planning and development.

    In summary, rehabilitation is a key measure in the response to Loss and Damage associated with climate change, as it contributes to the recovery of affected communities and ecosystems, as well as to building more resilient societies in the face of future climate impacts.

    Recover

    This refers to the process of restoring the functionality of a sector after it has been affected by an adverse climate event. It refers to restoring and rebuilding the natural, economic, and social systems affected by adverse climate impacts, with the aim of improving resilience and adaptive capacity to future extreme climate events.

    Relocate

    Relocation in the context of Loss and Damage refers to the strategy of moving communities, infrastructure, or economic activities from high-risk areas to safer locations to minimize the impact of future natural disasters or the effects of climate change. This measure is taken when remaining in the original location is not viable due to the constant and significant threat of hazards such as floods, landslides, and others.

    This brief analysis of the action measures—Rebuild, Rehabilitate, Recover, and Relocate—links the sectors with the most appropriate measures to adopt after an event occurs.

    Once the conceptual framework for Loss and Damage is defined, initiatives that can contribute to these measures proposed during the implementation period of this NDC, in both the unconditional and conditional scenarios, may be included.

    This approach is an overview of the perceived timescale for responding to Loss and Damage; the attribution or transfer of risk will be defined by the competent public entities involved in climate risk.

    7.1 Climate finance for the implementation of the Second NDC

    Ecuador, considered a developing country and in response to its national circumstances, has made a significant effort to estimate the resources, mechanisms, and financial tools that will allow the implementation of its actions for the period 2026-2035.

    In accordance with its national regulations, the country defines climate finance as the set of financial resources and technical assistance from any source intended for climate change management.

    In this context, estimating the resources needed to meet the goals set out in the NDC 2026-2035 will allow:

    • Identify funding opportunities for the conditional scenario initiatives, including actors from national and international entities.

    • Estimate the resources invested by the country in climate change management through its NDC actions.

    • To provide the necessary information to strengthen decision-making regarding the allocation of resources for climate change management in the country, as well as its periodic reporting.

    • Establish innovative mechanisms and financial strategies that enable compliance with the NDC, including, among others, partnerships between different actors.

    All these efforts are aligned with the National Climate Finance Strategy 2030. The EFIC, as a climate governance instrument developed through participatory processes, identifies financing gaps for achieving climate goals, seeks to improve the management of available resources, and thus promote the mobilization of additional flows. This document helps identify the enabling conditions and key actions that the various actors in climate finance must undertake. (MAATE, MEF, 2021).

    The objective of the National Climate Finance Strategy is to guide the access, management, allocation, and effective and efficient mobilization of international, national, public, and private climate finance to enhance the achievement of national and international climate change objectives, promoting low-carbon and climate-resilient development in the country, in line with national planning instruments and international climate commitments.

    The Strategy focuses on the following actions:

    • Establish institutional arrangements and a governance framework that facilitates multi-level and multi-sectoral coordination among key actors for climate finance.

    • To promote the consolidation of a financial system that mainstreams the risks and opportunities arising from climate change management.

    • Identify and design mechanisms and instruments that allow for the transparent, effective, and efficient mobilization of climate finance resources available to Ecuador.

    • To promote enabling conditions that facilitate and democratize access to, allocation of, management of, and mobilization of climate finance.

    • Promote an applicable legal and regulatory framework that provides certainty and incentives to leverage climate finance across all sectors.

    • To facilitate an adequate flow of information and knowledge on climate finance that, on the one hand, connects available supply and demand, and on the other hand, helps to ensure accountability and transparency in the monitoring, registration, and verification of national and international resources.

      Ecuador has developed a Climate Change Activities Catalog (CACC) that allows for the identification of amounts invested in climate change, the proper mobilization of resources, and the quantification of existing financing gaps for meeting climate goals. It also allows for the identification of sectors that concentrate the largest amount of resources and the continuous monitoring of financing flows for mitigation and adaptation projects.

      In this regard, the actions outlined in the 2026-2035 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) are aligned with those outlined in the National Climate Finance Strategy and have estimated an investment of approximately USD 6.5 billion for the implementation of their unconditional and conditional actions. This amount constitutes an estimated reference budget, the details of which will be specified according to the management needs of the implementing entities. This estimate has been developed based on available information and in accordance with national circumstances. This amount is distributed as follows: 27% in the unconditional scenario and 73% in the conditional scenario. Furthermore, achieving the goals set forth in the Second NDC requires technology transfer and capacity building to enable effective implementation in the proposed scenarios.

      8.1 Addressing gender, intergenerationality and intercultural approaches in the Second NDC

      In accordance with the principles of social justice and with a view to achieving a robust and transparent climate ambition in line with the First NDC, Ecuador, in its Second NDC, has voluntarily included the mainstreaming of gender, intergenerational, and intercultural approaches, ensuring that this climate policy fosters fair opportunities and contributes inclusively to the well-being of the entire Ecuadorian population.

      These approaches recognize and promote the participation of Indigenous, Montubio, and Afro-Ecuadorian peoples and nationalities; the participation of children, adolescents, and youth; and the empowerment of women, highlighting their knowledge and capacities as essential to addressing climate change. The cross-cutting approaches have been implemented through participatory processes, the identification of benefits, and the use of relevant and inclusive language for all the aforementioned sectors.

      The Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) in Ecuador and their formulation processes have highlighted actions to mainstream the gender approach as a commitment demonstrated by the State in the First NDC and its implementation. This process has yielded relevant lessons for the country's progress in this area, and based on this previous experience, the Second NDC has strengthened the scope of this approach in terms of the effective participation of women in territorial planning, also seeking gender parity, access to the benefits of adaptation and mitigation initiatives, and ensuring that the language in this instrument is inclusive and respectful of gender identities.

      The NDCs aim not only to contribute to reducing greenhouse gases and building resilience in the population, but also to ensure that women benefit equally—as men—from climate change actions and initiatives, that they acquire greater decision-making power and control over natural, economic, and cultural resources, and that they develop greater resilience and adaptation capacities to the impacts and risks of climate change.

      Furthermore, recognizing that climate change will have long-term impacts affecting both present and future generations, the intergenerational approach developed in the Second NDC has sought the participation of different age groups in structuring this public policy, ensuring sustainable and equitable actions that protect the rights of present and future generations.

      Child-sensitive NDCs strengthen essential social services, making them more resilient to climate change and disasters, and guaranteeing social protection, access to safe food, and a healthy environment. They also promote the rights of children and youth and ensure their role as drivers of solutions. Children and youth are among the groups most vulnerable to the effects of climate change, which puts their rights and well-being at risk. Their inclusion in the Second NDC has strengthened climate policies with an intergenerational vision that creates spaces for them to act and be included as empowered, resilient, and consciously committed agents of change in climate action.

      The Second NDC places particular emphasis on strengthening Indigenous, Montubio, and Afro-Ecuadorian peoples and nationalities, recognizing the fundamental value of their ancestral knowledge and practices in the design and implementation of this public policy. Cross-cutting participation is guaranteed in identifying the benefits derived from the measures and lines of action proposed for climate change adaptation and mitigation in the Second NDC, ensuring they are consistent with the principles of protecting their rights and territories. Implementing the actions of the Second NDC will allow for the continued development of planning instruments for these peoples and nationalities, tailored to their needs and characteristics (Life Plans), and will facilitate their access to financing mechanisms.

      Likewise, strengthening capacities and providing technical and institutional support to Indigenous peoples' and nationalities' organizations on climate change will be key to the implementation of the Second NDC, including the development of educational and communication content that ensures culturally appropriate language.

      Additionally, the country is working on its public policy to promote Climate Empowerment Action (CEA). This will allow for the identification of lines of work and agents of change in climate change management, encompassing education, training, public awareness, public access to information, public participation, and international cooperation. This strategy will be based on the key principles that guide climate action at the global and national levels, with a comprehensive approach that prioritizes the active participation of all sectors of society, including children and adolescents, as well as sustainability, climate justice, and resilience.

      Finally, the process of formulating the Second NDC included the participation and contributions of the National Councils for Equality of: Gender (CNIG), Peoples and Nationalities (CNIPN) and Intergenerational Equality (CNII).

      9. Information on how the Party considers its Nationally Determined Contribution to be fair and ambitious in light of national circumstances

      9.1 How does the Party consider its Nationally Determined Contribution to be fair and ambitious in light of its national circumstances;

      Ecuador considers the Second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) to be fair and ambitious in light of its national circumstances. While the country recognizes its shared responsibility in the fight against climate change under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, it is also aware of its vulnerability to its impacts.

      Ecuador is a country with significant needs for sustainable development and pressing social problems that limit its standard of living compared to developed countries. As of December 2023, the national poverty rate was 26% and the extreme poverty rate was 9.8%, with a higher incidence in rural areas (23.7%). In that year, the national Gini coefficient was 0.457, compared to 0.505 reported in 2010.

      Regarding illiteracy rates, a value of 3.4% was recorded up to 2022, while the early childhood education attendance rate showed a post-pandemic recovery, reaching 56.62% in 2023.

      Access to basic services is distributed as follows: electricity 96.2%, drinking water 61.1%, and sanitation 43.6%. It is important to note that the adverse effects of climate change may negatively impact access to these services.

      As Ecuador is a developing country with economic and social conditions that make it even more vulnerable to adverse climate effects, the country reaffirms its commitment to complying with international agreements and the provisions of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change.

      In this context, it has developed its Second NDC considering the guidelines established in the Common Timelines for NDCs, the Enhanced Transparency Framework and the guidelines for developing mitigation actions and adaptation measures to climate change in line with the results of the Global Stocktake.

      Ecuador understands climate ambition as extending far beyond simply increasing its commitment to reducing greenhouse gas emissions in the coming years. In this sense, it also considers empowerment and inclusivity, a just transition, sustainable development, clarity, and transparency as fundamental elements for accelerating climate action.

      On the path towards the goal of keeping warming to 2°C and embarking on the route to 1.5°C, the construction of the mitigation target, based on the calculation model of the National Mitigation Plan, allows for direct alignment with the efforts that the country is undertaking in the long term.

      Regarding adaptation, the Second NDC responds to the guidelines established in the National Adaptation Plan, which seeks to reduce climate risk and cover sectors and territories, giving a relevant role to Decentralized Autonomous Governments for the inclusion of actions in local planning that lead to the development of projects to make territories more resilient. Additionally, it presents an initial approach to measures to address losses and damages attributed to climate change.

      Given that NDCs are a cyclical process, this contribution has taken into account possible tools and methodological improvements in order to guide decision-making towards effective actions in the fight against climate change.

      This contribution is characterized by its territorial focus and its ability to address the needs of all people, including the voices of women, children, adolescents, youth, and indigenous peoples and nationalities who have been part of the processes of building this new climate commitment.

      The Second NDC was developed through a participatory process involving diverse actors in society, ensuring that it reflects the priorities and needs of all Ecuadorians. Furthermore, it incorporates principles of climate justice, acknowledging the historical responsibilities of developed countries and the need to support developing countries like ours.

      Ecuador's NDC represents a significant step towards low-emission, climate-resilient sustainable development. Through this contribution, the country seeks to strengthen resilience, protect ecosystems, and contribute to the global goals of the Paris Agreement, the National Development Plan, and other national climate change management instruments. However, the country recognizes that achieving these goals will require a joint effort and international cooperation. This NDC will serve as a basis for strengthening partnerships and mobilizing the necessary resources to meet its targets.

      9.2 Equity considerations, including a reflection on fairness

      Ecuador recognizes that equity is a fundamental principle in the fight against climate change. Therefore, it seeks to ensure that the benefits of climate action are distributed fairly and equitably, prioritizing the most vulnerable groups.

      We have worked closely with Indigenous peoples, Afro-Ecuadorian communities, and rural women as groups particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Therefore, the country has developed lines of action and measures to strengthen their resilience and promote their participation in decision-making.

      Furthermore, the importance of intergenerational justice is recognized, which is why the Second NDC has been designed with a long-term vision, ensuring a sustainable future for future generations.

      9.4 How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 4 of the Paris Agreement

      Ecuador considers that the Second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) responds to the provisions of Article 4 of the Paris Agreement, based on the recognition of its shared responsibility in the fight against climate change under the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, and this is reflected in the mitigation component of this document (Section 4).

      The way in which the Nationally Determined Contribution contributes to the achievement of the objective of the Convention, as stated in its article 2:

      10. How the Nationally Determined Contribution contributes to the achievement of the objective of the Convention, as stated in its article 2:

      10.1 The manner in which the Nationally Determined Contribution contributes to the achievement of the objective of the convention, as set out in its article 2

      Ecuador, in its Second NDC 2026-2035, contributes to the provisions of Article 2 of the UNFCCC through a commitment to reduce GHG emissions that aligns with the National Mitigation Plan, adopting a long-term vision to contribute to stabilizing global emissions concentrations. These efforts will be reflected in the lines of action and proposed target described in section 4.4.1.

      The adaptation component, for its part, responds to the National Adaptation Plan, which seeks to prioritize the implementation of measures to guarantee food sovereignty through specific actions stipulated in section 5.3 of this document.

      Finally, all these efforts will allow the country to move towards low-emission, climate-resilient sustainable development in accordance with national circumstances and in line with the international principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.

      10.2 How the Nationally Determined Contribution contributes to the implementation of Article 2, paragraph 1(a), and Article 4, paragraph 1, of the Paris Agreement.

      The Second NDC represents a significant step towards a low-emission and climate-resilient economy to address the adverse effects of climate change. Through this public policy, Ecuador reaffirms its commitment to contributing to the global goals of the Paris Agreement, through the various components of mitigation, adaptation, means of implementation, and loss and damage established for the period 2026-2035.

      The specific mitigation objective for the Second NDC is to contribute to global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by identifying sectors, lines of action, and initiatives that contribute to climate change mitigation in the country. Through these actions, the aim is to support the implementation of Article 2(a) of the Paris Agreement, which stipulates: Hold the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, recognizing that this would substantially reduce the risks and impacts of climate change.

      Globally, regarding mitigation, countries have generally agreed to reach peak greenhouse gas emissions "as soon as possible," recognizing that peak emissions will take longer for developing countries. Ecuador, through its Second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), continues its efforts toward this goal and, to that end, has set a greenhouse gas emissions reduction target in accordance with its national circumstances, contributing to the implementation of the National Mitigation Plan, which seeks a progressive reduction of emissions nationwide.
