Nationally Determined Contribution, NDC 3.0

Prologue

The Republic of Honduras, through the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (SERNA), reaffirms its commitment to global climate action by submitting this Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), in compliance with the Paris Agreement and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). This document reflects the Honduran people's willingness to actively contribute to climate change mitigation and adaptation to its impacts, despite being one of the countries that contributes the least to global greenhouse gas emissions.

We recognize that combating climate change is a shared but differentiated responsibility. Therefore, we urge developed countries to fulfill their financial and technology transfer commitments, as established by the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement. International support is essential for countries like Honduras to effectively implement their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and move towards sustainable and climate-resilient development.

This update incorporates all sources and sinks of greenhouse gases in accordance with the methodological guidelines of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. This inclusion strengthens the environmental integrity of our NDC and allows for more accurate accounting of our emissions and removals. Furthermore, it integrates a new loss and damage component and strengthens the mitigation and adaptation components, as well as the cross-cutting components of climate finance and transparency.

Honduras has demonstrated leadership in implementing Articles 5 and 6 of the Paris Agreement. We have complied with the requirements of Article 5 by voluntarily reporting our Forest Reference Emission Level (FREL) and by including the REDD+ results for 2021, 2022, and 2023 in our First Biennial Transparency Report.

These results are now eligible to be traded as Internationally Transferred Mitigation Results (ITMOs) under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. Furthermore, we are exploring cooperation with other countries to promote the implementation of these articles at the national level, including the development of innovative carbon trading mechanisms that are consistent with United Nations (UN) standards and the implementation of corresponding adjustments in accordance with guidance from the Conference of the Parties (CMA), which serves as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement.

We deeply appreciate the technical and financial assistance provided by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and its Climate Promise initiative, Deutsche Society for International Cooperation (GIZ) through the NDC Partnership Readiness Project (NPR), The Pew Charitable Trusts, the Alliance of Bioversity International and the International Center for Tropical Agriculture (CIAT) and the NDC Partnership, whose support has been fundamental in the preparation of this update.

We especially recognize and appreciate the unconditional, sustained, and strategic support of the Coalition of Tropical Forest Countries (CfRN), which has accompanied Honduras since 2022. Their valuable review of the document, ongoing technical assistance, and close support have been crucial in strengthening our institutional and technical capacities and have enabled us to consolidate this Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), more robustly integrating strategies for forest conservation, sustainable management, and restoration. This contribution has been essential in reinforcing the country's mitigation ambition, biodiversity preservation, and climate resilience. Furthermore, the CfRN's support has been crucial and highly effective in strengthening the implementation of Articles 5 and 6 of the Paris Agreement, including the necessary technical and institutional advancements for Honduras to achieve internationally transferred mitigation outcomes (ITMOs).

We also thank the 212 individuals from 55 entities, including public institutions, civil society organizations, academia, and the business sector, whose participation was fundamental to the development of NDC 3.0. This instrument is the result of a technical, participatory, multi-stakeholder, and multi-level process. We reiterate our commitment to continue working collaboratively, both nationally and internationally, to address the challenge of climate change and build a more sustainable future for present and future generations.

National Circumstances

Territorial Extension

Honduras is a Central American country, with coastlines on the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean. Its geography is characterized by high mountains, high plains, and valleys crossed by various rivers (Government of the Republic of Honduras, 2024).

Territorial extension: 112,777 km² — comprising 18 departments and 298 municipalities (ICF & SERNA, 2024).

Its government structure is democratic, republican, and representative, comprised of three complementary and independent branches: the Legislative, the Executive, and the Judicial. The Executive branch is headed by the President of the Republic, who is supported by a cabinet of secretaries of state. Legislative power is exercised by a Congress of Deputies, elected by popular vote. Judicial power, for its part, is composed of the Supreme Court of Justice, the Court of Appeals, and the courts established by law (Government of the Republic of Honduras, 2024).

Population

In 2024, the population was approximately 9,898,279 inhabitants, of which 42% live in rural areas and 58% live in urban areas.

  • The population density is 86.6 inhabitants per km² (INE, 2025), concentrating the main urban centers in the interior of the country (Tegucigalpa and San Pedro Sula).
  • Life expectancy is estimated at 77.3 years (INE, 2025).
  • The fertility rate is estimated at 2.38 (number of children) (INE, 2025).
  • Mortality rate is estimated at 4.46 (per thousand inhabitants) (INE, 2025).
  • Honduras is a multi-ethnic country; according to the official 2013 census, there are nine distinct ethnic groups of native and Afro-Honduran people who totaled approximately 718,000 people (SERNA, 2024). It is estimated that they currently number over 900,000. Among them are the Maya-Chortí, Lenca, Garífuna, Miskito, Pech, Tolupán, Nahua, Tawahka peoples, and English-speaking Afro-Honduran Creoles.

Economy and Development

  • Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita (2021): 3,533.60 USD (INE, 2025)
  • Annual real GDP growth (2022): 4% (World Bank Group, 2023a)
  • Inflation rate (2022): 9.1%, the highest since 2008 (World Bank Group, 2023b)
  • Total poverty (2023): 64.1% (El Mundo, 2024; SETRASS, 2023)
  • Gini Index (2024): 0.49 (INE, 2025)
  • Human Development Index: 0.624 (INE, 2025)
  • In 2021, the national poverty rate was 73.6%; in 2023, it fell to 64.1% and, by 2024, it had decreased to 62.9%.
  • Extreme poverty showed a significant decrease, going from 53.7% in 2021 to 41.5% in 2023, and to 40.1% in 2024 (National Institute of Statistics (INE), Honduras, 2024).
  • The unemployment rate has decreased gradually. In 2020, unemployment peaked at 10.9%. However, thanks to the economic recovery, this rate fell to 5.2% in June 2024.

Environment and Climate Change

Honduras accounts for 0.06% of global greenhouse gas emissions (Government of the Republic of Honduras, 2024).

According to the First Biennial Transparency Report of the Republic of Honduras (BTR1) 1990–2022, national emissions, excluding Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (UTCUTS), added 21,795 Gg of CO₂e in 2022.

In 2022, the Energy sector was the largest emitter, with 10,880 Gg CO₂e (50%), followed by Agriculture, with 6,262 Gg CO₂e (29%), Waste, with 2,566 Gg CO₂e (12%) and PIUP, with 2,087 Gg CO₂e (9%). Including the UTCUTS balance, which acted as a net sink of −3,145 Gg of CO₂e, the country's total emissions were 18,650 Gg of CO₂e (Central Bank of Honduras, 2022; Government of the Republic of Honduras, 2024).

Climate Vulnerability

In recent decades, Honduras has experienced losses and damages from devastating extreme climate events, which confirms its position as one of the countries most affected by climate change.

Recent history shows a succession of disasters with significant effects and impacts on the population, the economy and the environment (SERNA, 2024).

Events such as tropical cyclones Mitch, Eta, and Iota, as well as prolonged droughts, tropical storms, fires, and floods have had a significant impact on the country.

By 2023, it was estimated that 3.2 million inhabitants resided in highly vulnerable areas, urgently needing humanitarian aid by the government (United Nations Honduras; Humanitarian Network of Honduras; Secretariat for National Risk and Contingency Management (COPE-CO), 2023).

Honduras recognizes that climate change impacts affect the population in a disproportionate manner, especially affecting women, indigenous peoples and Afro-Hondurans, who face lower levels of resilience due to multidimensional historical inequalities.

Linking of Instruments

Within the framework of coherence and continuity of national climate policy, NDC 3.0 adopts as its methodological and numerical anchor for the 2021–2030 period the baseline and reference scenario established in the second NDC update (April 2025), constructed from the 2011–2020 historical series, with data reported in its first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) (December 2024), ensuring temporal comparability and consistency in monitoring. In accordance with this framework, all proposed sectoral targets are considered conditional, as their achievement depends on the mobilization of and access to international climate finance that allows for scaling up measures and levels of mitigation, which is subject to definition and availability, meaning that the ambition and scope of the goals may be adjusted in the future depending on the materialization of said support.

To extend the horizon of NDC 3.0 to 2035, the 2031–2035 period is incorporated through a methodological extension beyond 2030, constructed as a transparent continuation of the official trajectory, without altering the values for the 2021–2030 period. In parallel, and recognizing differentiated progress by sector, such as the LULUCF sector, this sector is already operational through the implementation of actions within the framework of Article 5.2 (REDD+ Approach at the National Level), which has generated and reported results.

Recent progress (2021–2023) has been made, and a first cooperative approach has been formalized to facilitate access to results-based financing, which is being developed within the framework of the implementation of Article 6.2 of the Paris Agreement. This progress may contribute to financially supporting the strengthening and eventual updating of future goals, once the agreed-upon financing is received.

Consequently, in terms of interpretation, coherence, and internal consistency, all references contained in NDC 3.0 to the Long-Term Strategy (LTS) must be understood as a programmatic guideline subject to ongoing review and consultation within the national context, given that the LTS is still being consolidated. In this sense, from the period after 2035 onward, the LTS is used exclusively as a framework for scaling up measures and planning toward 2050, without replacing, modifying, or rewriting the official values, baselines, scenarios, or targets contained within the NDC 3.0 horizon. However, the proposed quantitative targets expressed in numbers are the commitment objectives of this NDC.

With NDC 3.0, Honduras reaffirms its commitment, at the highest level, to climate action, in accordance with the Paris Agreement. This instrument aligns planning, implementation, and accountability with the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, and establishes an operational architecture for monitoring: the National Climate Change Monitoring System (SNMCC) is created, providing a comprehensive MRV of emissions and removals, adaptation progress, and support flows (financial, technological, and capacity-building). This system is integrated with the National Greenhouse Gas Inventory (INGEI), the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTRs), the NDCs, the National Adaptation Plan (PNA), the National Climate Empowerment Strategy (ENACE), the implementation strategy for Article 5.2, and as a guiding vision in Honduras's Territorial Planning Law (LTS), which is currently being consolidated.

NDC 3.0 integrates components of mitigation, adaptation, loss and damage, cross-cutting issues, financing and climate transparency, under a logic of sustainable development, recognizing that climate action can be aligned with economic growth, competitiveness, technological innovation, investment attraction and the strengthening of institutional capacities.

This NDC 3.0 takes into account the findings of the First Global Stocktake established in Decision 1/CMA.5: although almost all countries have initiated actions, global efforts remain insufficient to achieve long-term goals (UNFCCC, 2023). Limiting warming to 1.5 °C requires reducing global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 43% by 2030 and by 60% by 2035 compared to 2019, and achieving net zero CO₂ by 2050 (UNFCCC, 2024); current progress is not enough.

The alignment with the first global balance sheet is highlighted by the Acceleration of mitigation (before 2030). NDC 3.0 establishes five mitigation targets and sixteen measures distributed across five sectors: Energy, Production, Agriculture, Land Use and Land Use Planning (LULUCF), and Waste, encompassing all emissions and sinks reported in the National Energy and Gas Index (INGEI). Among the priorities is strengthening institutional capacity to ensure effective implementation and monitoring of actions over time. The proposed quantitative targets, expressed numerically, constitute the country's commitment objectives regarding mitigation.

To ensure a common and robust methodological and numerical basis during the period 2021–2030, this update adopts the baseline and reference scenario established in the second NDC update (April 2025), constructed from the 2011–2020 historical series and based on data reported in the first Biennial Transparency Report (December 2024), which ensures temporal comparability and consistency in monitoring. Likewise, the Long-Term Strategy (LTS) was consulted as a guiding input in the process, without this constituting its official submission to the UNFCCC. This approach is consistent with the recommendations of the Katowice Climate Package (COP 24 (UNFCCC, 2019)), which promotes coherence and integration between NDCs and long-term strategies to strengthen countries' climate action.

Reduction of adaptation gaps. Interventions are strengthened, moving from 9 goals and 14 measures to 6 sectoral goals and 26 measures in the following strategic areas: agri-food and food security; biodiversity and ecosystem services; infrastructure and socio-economic development; water resources; and health and sanitary assistance.

Enhanced financial support. The financing chapter is being strengthened and, in the coming stages, the NDC 3.0 Implementation Plan will be established, in which the financial needs will be quantified.

With these definitions, Honduras' NDC 3.0 not only promotes emissions reduction, but also reduces vulnerability through better adaptation, early response and an inclusive approach, while seeking to leverage international financing to accelerate climate action and move towards sustainable and climate-resilient development.

Honduras's fulfillment of its NDC 3.0 is entirely contingent upon effective, sufficient, and timely access to climate finance, as well as support for capacity building and technology transfer under the Paris Agreement. We recognize that combating climate change is a common but differentiated responsibility; therefore, we urge developed countries to fully comply with their financial and technology transfer commitments under the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement. International support is essential for countries like Honduras to effectively implement their NDCs and move towards sustainable and climate-resilient development.

Differential Value

NDC 3.0 represents a significant step forward in consolidating a more institutional and technical framework, aligned with national climate instruments. This progress builds upon the original NDC 2.0 and, in particular, the updated NDC 2.0 on mitigation, which has been key as a methodological and numerical starting point for the formulation, monitoring, and comparability of NDC 3.0's targets. Based on this foundation, NDC 3.0 introduces a more structured and measurable approach, with greater inter-institutional ownership. This update process strengthens national climate governance and lays the groundwork for more effective implementation and access to financial resources.

Main Differences

Institutionalized goals and measures: NDC 3.0 validates and institutionalizes climate goals and measures, incorporating a plan of institutional arrangements that formalize the commitments of the various actors.

Indicators:1 Indicators are developed that allow for systematic and transparent monitoring and evaluation of the achievement of objectives.

Alignment with national instruments: NDC 3.0 is aligned with the country's main climate instruments, such as the National Action Plan (NAP) and sectoral strategies, ensuring coherence in climate action. Furthermore, Honduras's NDC is strongly aligned with the implementation of Articles 5 and 6 of the Paris Agreement, primarily within the LULUCF sector, incorporating these approaches into the projections already established in the updated NDC of April 2025. It complements other relevant national instruments and takes into account, in an indicative manner, some of the recommendations of the Land Use Plan (LUP).

Technical and methodological support: The new version strengthens the technical and methodological support for the goals and measures, ensuring that each commitment is based on evidence, analysis, and appropriate tools for its monitoring and validation.

Losses and damages: New loss and damage component, which outlines actions to avoid, minimize and address the adverse effects of climate change.

Thoroughness and consistency: Honduras includes emissions and removals from all sectors in its National Greenhouse Gas Inventory, following the guidelines of the IPCC guidelines.

Highly Participatory and Multi-sectoral Process

NDC 3.0 is the result of a technical, participatory, multi-stakeholder and multi-level process. Between July 2025 and October 2025, seven in-person workshops, three virtual workshops, four information sessions, and 32 bilateral sessions were held, with the participation of 212 people from 55 entities representing public institutions, civil society, academia, and the business sector. The in-person workshops included three on adaptation, two on mitigation, cross-cutting issues, and social inclusion, one on refining adaptation and mitigation strategies, and one on disseminating all relevant information.

The sectoral workshops addressed financing (with prominent participation from SEFIN), cross-cutting issues (with prominent participation from SEDESOL), and loss and damage; the information sessions were divided into 3 on loss and damage and 1 on mitigation, social inclusion, and cross-cutting issues; and the bilateral sessions comprised 14 on adaptation, 12 on mitigation, and 6 on loss and damage. The input and opinions gathered helped strengthen the development of this document.

01. Mitigation Component

To support mitigation commitments with solid evidence, Honduras has strengthened its capabilities to estimate GHG emissions and removals by preparing and publishing its BTR1. This report constitutes a milestone in the consolidation of national information on climate change, which ensures Honduras' commitment to transparency, accountability and effective climate action.

The most up-to-date version of the INGEI, included in the BTR1, covers the period 1990–2022 and all sectors and key gases: carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆), and nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃). The estimates were developed in accordance with the 2006 Guidelines of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and using the updated Global Warming Potentials from the Fifth Assessment Report (AR5), consistent with the requirements of the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement.

The information presented in BTR1 allows us to overcome previous gaps, especially in the UTCUTS sector, which now has more solid data for analysis. This means that, unlike previous exercises, Honduras now integrates the LULUCF sector into its commitments to mitigation and in its emissions reduction trajectory.

With this methodological improvement and the incorporation of updated information, and considering the vision of the LTS of Honduras as a guiding input, the NDC 3.0 of Honduras reflects a real progression in terms of transparency, clarity in the goals set and technical robustness.

In this context, the Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) tool, also known as NDC Tracking, developed by CfRN, becomes especially relevant. It is adopted as the main tool for monitoring compliance with the NDC from the perspective of mitigation results, strengthening the traceability of progress, the consistency of monitoring and accountability.

All of this reinforces the country's commitment to the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in light of its national circumstances.

The sectors included reflect the country's emissions structure and align with IPCC guidelines. NDC 3.0 organizes its measures into five main sectors, consistent with the classification established by the IPCC:

  1. Energy — covers all emissions resulting from production, processing, transport and use of energy.
  2. Industrial Processes and Product Use — includes greenhouse gas emissions from industrial processes other than combustion, and emissions related to the use of gases in products and non-energy uses of fossil fuels when used as raw materials.
  3. Agriculture — covers emissions derived from agricultural and livestock production, such as enteric fermentation, the management of manure, rice crops, and the use of fertilizers.
  4. UTCUTS (LULUCF) — includes carbon and greenhouse gas flows associated with deforestation, reforestation, forest management, and other changes in land cover and land use.
  5. Waste — includes emissions associated with the generation, handling and disposal of solid and liquid waste, including biological decomposition, incineration, landfills, open burning of waste, and the treatment and discharge of wastewater.

In each of these sectors, measures, actions, and indicators are defined that constitute the framework for organizing and monitoring Honduras' progress in fulfilling its NDC 3.0. A total of 16 mitigation measures are established, distributed among the sectors mentioned above.

Climate mitigation is conceived as a sustainable development opportunity for Honduras. Beyond emissions reduction, the measures will boost innovation, competitiveness, the creation of green jobs, poverty reduction and social cohesion. With the support of international cooperation and the strengthening of national capacities, Honduras will work to decouple economic growth from increased emissions, building a resilient and low-emission future.

Mitigation Component Goals

For this NDC 3.0, Honduras maintains the goals established in the second update of the NDC that seek to reduce projected emissions by 17% during the period 2021–2030 (April 2025), maintaining consistency with the information presented in its first Initial Report (December 2025) under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

The distribution of mitigation measures is based on updates to the national GHG inventory and revisions and/or updates to mitigation measures presented in the first biennial transparency report (BTR), as well as the Forest Reference Level of Honduras. Therefore, in the second update, Honduras included a new Business As Usual (BAU) scenario of 50,381.3 Gg CO₂e projected to 2030.

This NDC 3.0, while maintaining the same established goals, updates the mitigation values for all sectors based on the data presented in its BTR. These goals reaffirm and extend the commitments, contributions, plans, and principles presented in the first NDC update.

Furthermore, in this NDC 3.0, Honduras expands this 17% target for 2035, applying it to the business-as-usual (BAU) scenario for the same sectors, maintaining the same relative level of reduction compared to the trend scenario. That is, by 2035 a cumulative national emissions reduction of 19.6% is expected during the period 2021–2035, equivalent to 8,564.8 Gg CO₂e, including all sectors.

Measures of Mitigation

The following are the mitigation measures to achieve the proposed goals. Further details on the measures can be found in the Annex Sheets of the NDC 3.0 Technical Document.

Energy Sector

Measure 1 — By 2030, increase the share of renewable energy to reach 70% of the electricity generation mix and maintain it above this level by 2035. Expand the participation of renewable sources in electricity generation in order to diversify the energy mix, reduce dependence on fossil fuels and decrease greenhouse gas emissions, in accordance with the Indicative Plan for Generation Expansion (PIEG) 2026–2035. It also aims to promote the creation of enabling conditions, including the development of energy storage systems and the strengthening of the electricity transmission network.

Measure 2 — By 2035, boost energy efficiency in the residential, commercial, and industrial sectors by optimizing energy use and reducing the economy's energy intensity. This will be achieved through the electrification of end uses, the replacement of inefficient equipment and lighting, the application of energy performance standards, and the promotion of sustainable and bioclimatic buildings. The goal is to reduce electricity intensity, in absolute terms, by 3.4 kWh/USD in 2030 and by an additional 4 kWh/USD in 2035, compared to 2022. The plan also aims to increase the share of electricity in final energy consumption by at least 3 percentage points (pp) by 2030 and by at least 5 pp by 2035.

Measure 3 — By 2035, promote sustainable and modern bioenergy, prioritizing the use of biogas, biofuels and biomass from waste or sustainable energy plantations. Promote the development of sustainable bioenergy through biodigesters on livestock farms and in wastewater treatment plants, as well as the design and implementation of national strategies aimed at promoting sustainable energy crops and biofuels. The goal is to achieve a 5% share of biofuels in final consumption by 2030 and maintain that percentage by 2035.

Measure 4 — By 2030, reduce firewood consumption per household by 39% compared to the consumption level recorded in 2012. Reduce firewood consumption by expanding improved stove programs, prioritizing vulnerable households and sustainable biomass management.

Measure 5 — By 2030, promote electric mobility in the country, aligned with the National Electric Mobility Strategy of Honduras (ENME), and extend the ambition towards 2035. The goal is to electrify 3% of the national vehicle fleet by 2030 and expand this target to 7.5% by 2035.

Measure 6 — By 2035, promote the progressive hybridization of private transport fleets as a transition to zero-emission technologies, driving sustainable public procurement and accompanied by technical training. By 2035, it is expected that 20% of light vehicles will be gasoline hybrids and 15% of heavy and light freight vehicles will be diesel hybrids.

Measure 7 — By 2030, promote sustainable urban mobility plans. Promote the proper planning and operation of public transport systems, pedestrian and cycling infrastructure, and urban mobility management measures, with the aim of reducing emissions, improving air quality in cities and the health of their inhabitants.

Industrial Processes and Product Use Sector

Measure 8 — By 2035, reduce emissions generated in cement production processes. Reduce CO₂ emissions associated with cement production by decreasing the clinker factor. By 2030, a reduction of 7.4 percentage points is expected compared to the values reported in the BTR, and by 2035, a reduction of 8.5 percentage points, supported by promoting the use of alternative materials and updating environmental standards.

Measure 9 — By 2035, reduce the use of fluorinated gases in refrigeration and air conditioning, aligning actions with the Kigali Amendment and promoting efficient technological equipment endorsed by the KIP. Gradually reduce HFC consumption to 90% by 2029 and 70% by 2035 relative to the country's baseline, as per the Kigali Amendment.

Agriculture Sector

Measure 10 — By 2035, transform the livestock sector towards a low-carbon economy, aligned with the Honduras Livestock NAMA. Transform the livestock sector through the implementation of a sustainable livestock model that reduces enteric emissions, improves productive efficiency and increases carbon capture in soils and pastures. These actions will be implemented on 20% of the country's farms. A reduction of 2,100 Gg CO₂e is projected by 2035.

Measure 11 — By 2035, boost low-emission coffee production in Honduras. Promote agroforestry practices, the efficient use of inputs, and lower-carbon processing methods in coffee farming through the adoption of sustainable practices. The plan is to establish and manage diversified agroforestry systems in coffee plantations, reaching a total of 3,300 farms by 2035. More efficient management of nitrogen fertilizers will be promoted on 3,300 farms. Biodigesters, composting systems, and/or oxidation ponds are expected to be implemented in 160 facilities by 2035. The plan is to progressively replace electric or mechanical dryers with solar technologies, reaching 80 modernized facilities by 2035.

Measure 12 — By 2035, promote low-emission agriculture with increased carbon sinks. Promote the adoption of efficient agricultural technologies, especially in priority crops, that contribute to reducing GHG emissions, increasing carbon sinks and improving the productivity and sustainability of the agricultural sector. Actions include reducing the use of nitrogen fertilizers, projected to reach 12.7% of the area dedicated to agriculture by 2035, and the implementation of efficient irrigation systems such as drip irrigation, expected to be established in 20,000 hectares by 2035.

UTCUTS (LULUCF) Sector

Measure 13 — By 2030, reduce net emissions from the LULUCF sector by 1% compared to BAU. Promote the reduction of net emissions through reforestation, restoration, protection, and sustainable forest management. This measure will foster coordinated forest restoration and recovery processes, mobilizing resources and strengthening environmental governance through community participation. It will also promote ecological restoration techniques to recover or improve water regimes in degraded areas and strengthen the phytosanitary status of forests.

Waste Sector

Measure 14 — By 2030, have an operational sanitary landfill in Tegucigalpa with a capture system and by 2035 have biogas utilization for energy generation and emission reduction. Strengthen, through the Municipal Government of the Central District, the comprehensive management of solid waste in Tegucigalpa by the construction and operation of a sanitary landfill incorporating biogas capture and energy utilization systems. The project will have a total capacity of up to 8 million tons of solid waste, distributed between a first cell with a capacity of 3 million tons (10-year lifespan) and a second cell with an additional 5 million tons capacity (15-year projected operational life).

Measure 15 — By 2030, promote Integrated Solid Waste Management, prioritizing the reduction of waste generation, the increase in the use of valuable materials and the implementation of sustainable technological solutions at final disposal sites. The goal is to reduce annual per capita generation of non-hazardous solid waste by 5% by 2030, achieving a 10% reduction by 2035 compared to the baseline. The recovery rate of recyclable waste is expected to increase to 22% by 2030 and to 30% by 2035. By 2030 there will be three solid waste disposal sites with methane recovery capabilities, and four by 2035. By 2035, the number of managed solid waste disposal sites will be expanded to six additional municipalities.

Measure 16 — By 2030, strengthen Integrated Wastewater Management. Strengthen compliance with current regulations regarding the treatment and discharge of wastewater into receiving bodies and sanitary sewer systems, in order to reduce the organic load and methane and nitrous oxide emissions associated with the sector. It is projected that by 2030, 80% of all Wastewater Treatment Plants will have a diagnostic assessment conducted by the Discharge Control Unit, reaching 90% by 2035.

02. Adaptation Component

Since the NDC update in 2021, Honduras has made significant progress in building enabling frameworks and strengthening climate action. These include the First Adaptation Communication; the findings of the First Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1); and the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) Review and Progress Report for the 2018–2023 period. The NAP is currently being updated for the 2025–2030 cycle. These instruments identify gaps and key recommendations to guide the formulation of NDC 3.0.

Despite these advances, the country continues to face increasing pressures on its water resources, production systems, ecosystems, infrastructure, and human health. Water governance remains limited by the lack of an operational authority, fragmented monitoring systems, and the absence of fundamental regulatory instruments, while the degradation of recharge zones and the pollution of surface and groundwater sources compromise water security. Agri-food production is also highly vulnerable to climate variability, droughts, soil degradation, and pests, with persistent gaps in technical assistance, financing, and productive infrastructure, especially in value chains such as those for staple grains, coffee, and livestock. In parallel, deforestation, the degradation of marine and coastal habitats, and pressure on protected areas weaken essential ecosystem services. These vulnerabilities are amplified by the critical exposure of road, energy, and water and sanitation infrastructure to hurricanes, floods, landslides, and heat waves, in a context lacking national regulations for resilient infrastructure and integrated information systems to support preventive planning. The health sector also faces increasing impacts, evidenced by the increase in climate-sensitive diseases and by structural limitations in epidemiological surveillance, health infrastructure and operational capacity, especially in rural and coastal areas.

This combination of factors underscores the urgent need to strengthen enabling frameworks, institutional capacities, and robust information systems to support climate action and move towards more resilient development.

The NDC 3.0 update is based on the five strategic pillars of the NAP:

  1. Agri-food and food safety — climate-smart agriculture, improved varieties, sustainable coffee management and resilient livestock farming, in order to guarantee food security and economic sustainability.
  2. Biodiversity and ecosystem services — conservation and restoration of terrestrial and marine-coastal ecosystems, to increase ecological resilience and protect ecosystem services for the benefit of the population.
  3. Infrastructure and socioeconomic development — integration of the adaptation approach into territorial and sectoral planning to strengthen the resilience of infrastructure, the energy system and tourism.
  4. Water resources — strengthening governance and sustainable water management through effective planning, adaptive actions and evidence-based decisions to ensure the country's water resilience.
  5. Health and healthcare — integration of adaptation and resilience climate considerations in health sector planning and strengthening surveillance and prevention systems to reduce the risks of climate-sensitive diseases.

Within this framework, NDC 3.0 substantially raises the ambition for adaptation by including actions aimed at addressing structural challenges in water management, the resilience of agri-food systems, ecosystem conservation, the protection of critical infrastructure, energy system adaptation, and the strengthening of the health sector. It also consolidates essential enabling frameworks, such as sectoral policies, climate information systems, and multi-level governance mechanisms, to ensure effective and transparent implementation aligned with the country's international commitments under the Paris Agreement.

03. Loss and Damage Component

Honduras is among the countries most vulnerable to climate change globally, with hydrometeorological events causing recurring losses and damage in the productive, social, and environmental sectors. According to the BTR, floods account for approximately 48.5% of historical losses, and droughts 34.1%, concentrating more than 80% of total economic losses (SERNA, 2024). Tropical cyclones Mitch (1998), Eta, and Iota (2020) caused significant damage to infrastructure, housing, agriculture, and basic services, affecting 40% of the population (SERNA, 2024).

The country has made progress in institutionalizing risk management through the State Policy for Comprehensive Risk Management in Honduras (PEGIRH, 2013), the Law of the National Risk Management System (SINAGER, 2009) and the draft Climate Change Law (2025). Among Honduras' priorities is the creation of the National Loss and Damage Mechanism, in alignment with the international Warsaw Mechanism (WIM), in accordance with the provisions of Article 8 of the Paris Agreement, which allows access to technical assistance and international financing, promoting the integration of the approach to loss and damage within the national climate agenda.

Goal: By the year 2035, Honduras will have established the institutional foundations, technical and financial resources for an operational National Loss and Damage Mechanism, strengthening its capacities to understand, act, support and access financing in order to avoid, minimize and address the economic and non-economic losses and damages associated with the adverse effects of climate change, both from slow-onset events and extreme weather events.

Strategic Actions of the Loss and Damage Component

The seven strategic actions are organized according to the three approaches of the State Policy for Comprehensive Risk Management of Honduras (PEGIRH): prospective management, corrective management and compensatory management (SINAGER & COPECO, 2013) and in alignment with the Long-Term Low Emission and Climate Resilience Development Strategy (LTS) and the First Biennial Transparency Report.

Strategic Action 1 — Generation of baselines of natural and physical assets in prioritized sectors. Establish national baselines on the exposure and vulnerability of natural and physical assets in priority sectors, such as infrastructure (water and sanitation, energy, transportation), social services (education, health, housing), productive sectors (industry, commerce, agriculture, livestock, and tourism), and the environment, considering vulnerable groups. These baselines will constitute the initial inventory and diagnosis of assets and services exposed to the impacts of climate change.

Strategic Action 2 — Sectoral climate risk assessment in prioritized sites. Conduct comprehensive climate risk assessments for prioritized sectors and sites, using climate and socioeconomic scenarios. These will incorporate climate change projections, the updating and development of hazard maps (including floods, landslides, and sea-level rise) in coordination with COPECO, and the construction of specific hazard, exposure, and vulnerability scenarios at pilot sites.

Strategic Action 3 — Definition of the National Climate Risk Index and sectoral sub-indices. Develop a National Climate Risk Index for Honduras that objectively integrates threat, vulnerability, and exposure factors at the national level and becomes a standardized tool for prioritizing actions. This action includes the compilation and processing of quantitative indicators that allow for comparison of relative risk among regions and populations within the country, as well as the development of more detailed sectoral climate risk indices.

Strategic Action 4 — Integration of the loss and damage approach into national planning. Incorporate actions to address losses and damages (economic and non-economic) into the main instruments of national planning, including priority measures in sectoral policies, strategies, plans, and programs. The development of monitoring and evaluation mechanisms aligned with the SNMCC is planned, beginning with methodological design and the collection of key information.

Strategic Action 5 — Operationalization of the national registry of losses and damages (SNMCC). Operationalize the SNMCC's loss and damage module for the quantification and systematic recording of information at the sector level, advancing in homogeneous quantification methodologies and standardized reporting protocols. An interoperable platform for the national registry of losses and damages will be developed, integrated with the SNMCC and capable of collecting economic and non-economic data disaggregated by event type, sector, region, and affected population.

Strategic Action 6 — Institutional and regulatory strengthening for the establishment of the National Mechanism of Losses and Damages. Consolidate the legal and institutional framework that supports the creation and operation of the National Loss and Damage Mechanism, including updating key legislation (the Climate Change Law, the SINAGER Law, and other related regulations) and advancing the formalization of the National Loss and Damage Committee as the governing body.

Strategic Action 7 — Strengthening of national and local capacities in loss and damage. Develop the necessary human and technical capacities in institutions, communities, and among other actors to effectively implement the National Loss and Damage Mechanism through awareness-raising and training processes on how to prevent, minimize, and address losses and damages. This includes strengthening the capacities of actors within SINAGER (including CODEL, CODEM, and CODECE) in loss and damage assessment, use of the SNMCC registry, climate risk analysis, resilient reconstruction, post-disaster response, and climate finance.

In the long term, Honduras will focus its efforts on strengthening the structural and operational capacity of the National Loss and Damage Mechanism, promoting the adoption of national technical standards for resilient reconstruction and expanding the scope of Early Warning Systems (EWS) in synergy with the monitoring and registration processes of the SNMCC. The functional integration of multi-hazard early warning systems will be promoted through common protocols, data interoperability, and public dissemination of alerts, enabling early intervention to be linked with actual damage records.

04. Cross-cutting (Transversal) Component

Honduras has demonstrated a sustained commitment to social inclusion in its climate action. Based on the technical analysis conducted for the preparation of NDC 3.0, Honduras incorporates for the first time a cross-cutting component of social inclusion and climate governance. This component consolidates the approaches of participation, gender equity, human rights, and just transition as structural principles for the effective implementation of goals related to mitigation, adaptation, financing, loss and damage, and climate transparency.

This cross-cutting component is conceived as the enabling framework that guarantees the legitimacy, sustainability, and coherence of national climate action, strengthening institutions, citizen participation, and inter-institutional coordination. It is based on the recognition that climate change does not affect people uniformly. Gender, age, ethnic identity, economic, and territorial inequalities directly influence vulnerability and the capacity to respond to climate impacts. Therefore, NDC 3.0 adopts an inclusive and rights-based approach, aimed at making the transition to resilient and low-emission development equitable and fair for all people and communities.

The component is structured around three interdependent approaches to action, whose synergy allows progress towards inclusive climate governance, equitable access to resources and the generation of sustainable livelihoods:

  • Inclusive governance and citizen participation — establishes the political and institutional framework that enables representation and shared responsibility in decision-making across all NDC components.
  • Equitable access to resources, technologies and financing — ensures that the benefits and opportunities derived from climate action reach all groups in society on fair terms.
  • Green livelihoods and jobs — embodies the principles of equity and sustainability in the real economy, strengthening social and economic resilience of the territories.

These three approaches are mutually reinforcing: without inclusive governance, equitable access cannot be guaranteed; without equitable access, sustainable livelihoods cannot be consolidated; and without sustainable livelihoods, they weaken the legitimacy and effectiveness of governance. All of these elements are essential for a just transition.

01. Inclusive Governance and Citizen Participation

Honduras is moving towards participatory, representative, and transparent climate governance, integrating vulnerable groups.3 To this end, NDC 3.0 proposes strengthening the Inter-institutional Technical Committee on Climate Change (CTICC) through a body responsible for ensuring the integration of these approaches into the NDC planning, implementation and monitoring processes.

The institutionalization of systematic participatory processes and territorial dialogues is promoted, aimed at building public policies sensitive to the social and cultural context of the territories. This approach not only strengthens the legitimacy of decisions, but also contributes to raising climate ambition by incorporating the citizen voice as a foundation of climate governance.

Strategic Action 1 — Institutionalization of inclusive climate participation mechanisms. Consolidate permanent spaces that ensure the effective participation of vulnerable groups in decision-making. The creation and strengthening of a body within the CTICC constitutes a strategic step to mainstream these approaches into climate governance, providing technical advice, regulatory reviews, guidelines, and participatory monitoring of the NDC. For its sustainability, this group must have balanced representation, transparent democratic appointment processes, dedicated resources, and coordination with government sectors.

Strategic Action 2 — Development and generation of information that guides sensitive adaptation to conflict. Implement a strategic tool to ensure that climate interventions explicitly consider the social, territorial, cultural, and political dynamics that can generate tensions or exacerbate existing conflicts. This tool should offer practical criteria, methodologies, and protocols for identifying socio-environmental risks, recognizing key actors, and understanding the power relations present in each territory, incorporating gender, intercultural, and human rights perspectives.

Strategic Action 3 — Implementation of a comprehensive strategy for social and gender inclusion. Develop and implement a comprehensive social, gender, and climate change inclusion strategy for the implementation of the NDC, aimed at ensuring the effective and representative participation of vulnerable groups in decision-making. The strategy should include a diagnosis of gaps and differentiated capacities; clear criteria for territorial, cultural, and age-based representation; mechanisms for ongoing participation; spaces for multi-stakeholder dialogue; guidelines for the gender-responsive implementation of the NDC; and a participatory monitoring system.

02. Equitable Access to Resources, Technologies, and Financing

NDC 3.0 promotes access to climate resources as a principle of justice and equity; this is a key element of a just transition. It will promote the design of investment and financial security protocols that ensure vulnerable groups actively participate, accessing financing, technologies, and market opportunities on equitable terms.

Strategic Action 4 — Design and implementation of an investment, financial security and incentive protocol for inclusive climate action of the NDC. Develop a protocol to guide the allocation and management of climate resources based on the principles of equity, transparency, and efficiency, ensuring that financial benefits reach vulnerable groups proportionally. These protocols should include guidelines for the ex-ante assessment of differentiated social impacts, criteria for territorial prioritization based on climate and socioeconomic vulnerabilities, safeguards to prevent exclusion and discrimination, and financial monitoring mechanisms sensitive to gender and interculturality. They must also define traceability standards for the use of funds, early warnings for financial risks, and procedures to guarantee the economic security of projects in contexts of high climate vulnerability.

Strategic Action 5 — Creating an instance of Climate Change and Human Mobility. Establish a permanent body that coordinates government entities, subnational governments, international organizations, civil society, and indigenous and Afro-Honduran organizations to comprehensively address the relationship between climate change and human mobility. This body must develop guidelines for prevention, protection and response to displacement and migration induced by climate events, promote harmonization of regulations, promote intercultural care protocols, and strengthen coordination between sectors. Furthermore, it must generate evidence on climate-related migration patterns and incorporate gender and human rights approaches.

Strategic Action 6 — Integration of disaggregated social information into the National Climate Change Monitoring System (SNMCC). Incorporate social data disaggregated by sex, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and territory into the SNMCC systems, within the National Safeguards Framework for Climate Change Initiatives and in the Social and Environmental Safeguards Submodule for REDD+, to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the differentiated impacts and benefits of climate action. The system must guarantee active transparency with a gender focus, ensuring that the information generated is accessible, understandable and timely for women, indigenous peoples, youth and other historically excluded groups.

Strategic Action 7 — Promoting inclusive financial instruments for climate resilience. Promote the creation, adaptation and dissemination of inclusive financial instruments, such as green microloans, revolving funds, guarantees, parametric insurance, community climate initiatives led by women and youth, and resilience bonds, which allow populations in situations of vulnerability to cope with and recover from climate impacts. This strategic action includes coordination with the banking sector, public and private, innovative financing mechanisms, incentives for sustainable investments and integration of social safeguards.

03. Livelihoods and Green Jobs

NDC 3.0 proposes prioritizing the creation of green jobs and inclusive economic opportunities in strategic sectors such as agroforestry, ecosystem restoration, the circular economy and a just energy transition. These actions will focus on diversifying livelihoods and reducing migration pressures in vulnerable areas, strengthening local resilience and social cohesion.

Strategic Action 8 — Technical training programs in green jobs for vulnerable groups. Implement specialized technical training and skills certification programs aimed at strengthening the abilities of vulnerable groups to integrate into sectors linked to the green economy and climate adaptation. These programs should include modules on renewable energy, agroecology and resilient agriculture, sustainable forest management, bioeconomy, responsible tourism, nature-based solutions, clean technologies, and digitalization. The training should combine theory and practice through workshops, field schools, innovation labs, and internships, ensuring cultural relevance, intercultural methods, and linguistic accessibility.

Strategic Action 9 — Promotion of sustainable and safe labor standards with a focus on equity. Develop and implement labor standards that guarantee safe, decent working conditions aligned with the green transition, incorporating principles of gender equality, interculturalism, and non-discrimination. This includes establishing criteria for preventing occupational hazards in sustainable activities, ensuring equal pay, promoting shared responsibility for caregiving, preventing workplace harassment and sexual harassment, and ensuring accessibility for people with disabilities.

Strategic Action 10 — Incentives for green ventures led by vulnerable groups. Design and implement financial, fiscal, and technical incentive mechanisms for green businesses led by vulnerable groups, recognizing their potential to revitalize local economies and contribute innovative solutions to the climate crisis. Incentives may include competitive funds, seed capital, green microloans, financial guarantees, specialized technical assistance, and incubation and acceleration programs.

Honduras's NDC 3.0 marks a qualitative shift by adopting a structural mainstreaming of the principles of inclusion and equity. This means that every action related to mitigation, adaptation, financing, loss and damage, and climate transparency will be designed, implemented, and monitored according to criteria of participation, gender equity, transparency, and human rights. Mainstreaming provides institutions with tools to integrate gender equality, youth participation, and cultural diversity into their policies, budgets, and mechanisms, strengthening national climate governance by making these approaches a shared responsibility between the NDC's implementing and reporting entities.

05. Financing Component

Climate finance is an essential component in the fight against climate change because it allows financial resources to be channelled toward mitigation and adaptation actions that would otherwise be unfeasible, especially in developing countries. The transition to low-carbon economies requires massive investments in renewable energy, energy efficiency, sustainable transport, and nature-based solutions, among others. Likewise, adapting to the impacts of climate change, such as droughts, floods, and biodiversity loss, demands resources to strengthen infrastructure, early warning systems, and institutional capacities. Without a steady and sufficient flow of climate finance, the global commitments established in the Paris Agreement would be unattainable.

Furthermore, climate finance acts as a catalyst by mobilizing private sector investment and aligning financial systems with global climate goals. Through instruments such as green funds, climate bonds, guarantees, and risk-sharing mechanisms, public resources can leverage private capital and promote a structural transformation of economies. This financing not only reduces emissions and vulnerabilities but also drives sustainable development, generates green jobs, and strengthens the resilience of the most affected communities.

Climate finance is of vital importance to Honduras, as the country is among the most vulnerable in the world to the impacts of climate change, despite being a low emitter of greenhouse gases. Phenomena such as tropical cyclones, hurricanes, prolonged droughts, and extreme rainfall directly affect its economy, food security and the lives of millions of people. To strengthen its resilience, Honduras needs financial resources that will allow it to invest in climate-resilient infrastructure, sustainable water management, and agriculture adapted to new conditions, and the protection of ecosystems that act as natural barriers against disasters. Without an adequate flow of climate finance, the country faces a high risk of setbacks in its social and developmental achievements.

This financing also represents a strategic opportunity for Honduras to promote a more sustainable and inclusive development model. With well-managed resources, the country can diversify its energy mix, foster resource efficiency, promote green investments, and strengthen institutional capacities to access and manage international funds. In this way, climate finance not only helps address the impacts of climate change but also drives job creation, technological innovation, and poverty reduction, contributing to the fulfilment of its commitments under the Paris Agreement and the Sustainable Development Goals.

Goal: By 2035, Honduras will have strengthened its climate finance architecture by implementing a National Climate Finance Strategy and Investment Plan, integrating a climate approach into the country's planning and budget execution, and implementing innovative mechanisms to mobilize resources, such as carbon markets and results-based payment schemes. Furthermore, financial MRV systems aligned with international standards will have been established to expand access to climate finance and support the implementation of mitigation and adaptation goals.

Financing Measures

Measure 1 — By 2035, Honduras commits to developing a Climate Change Financing Strategy and adopting an Investment Plan with a prioritized roadmap to mobilize public and private resources. The measure seeks to strengthen the country's financial architecture in the face of climate change by adopting, by 2035, an Investment Plan and a Climate Finance Strategy, aiming at strategically mobilizing international public and private resources necessary to implement mitigation and adaptation actions, loss and damage management and cross-cutting climate change management. Specific actions include generating detailed information on climate finance needs and creating a strengthened regulatory framework for transparency and efficiency in resource management.

Measure 2 — By 2035, Honduras commits to promoting actions that leverage private sector funds to mobilize for mitigation investment, adaptation, losses and damages and the cross-cutting component. The measure's main objective is to mobilize private investment towards climate change mitigation and adaptation actions by creating enabling conditions and financial mechanisms that encourage the participation of the business sector. Specific actions include prioritizing climate projects with investment potential, developing innovative economic and financial instruments such as green bonds, guarantee funds, or blended finance mechanisms, and strengthening national capacities to design, manage, and monitor climate finance initiatives.

Measure 3 — By 2035, Honduras commits to establishing payment schemes for results, including carbon markets, and to regulate its architecture, in particular for Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. The measure aims to establish results-based payment schemes and carbon markets that will allow the country to access performance-based financing and contribute effectively to the objectives of the Paris Agreement, particularly Article 6. This measure seeks to create a national carbon market architecture that incentivizes emissions reductions and the conservation of forest ecosystems.

Measure 4 — By 2035, Honduras is committed to establishing an Integrated System of Transparency and MRV for Climate Finance. The integrated measure proposes the creation of a National Integrated System for Transparency and MRV of Climate Finance, which would unify the traceability of public climate spending with the monitoring of international support received (financial, technological, and capacity-building). Led by the Ministry of Finance (SEFIN), this system seeks to consolidate a robust architecture for reporting, verifying, and analyzing climate finance flows, both domestic and international. Specific actions include strengthening climate budget tracking, improving the quality and frequency of implementation reports, standardizing registration methodologies, the modernization and expansion of SIAFI, and promoting interoperability among different national information systems.

Measure 5 — By 2035, Honduras commits to strengthening a financial protection system and early response to climate events to increase the fiscal and social resilience of the country. The measure is geared towards financial protection and early response to climate events, seeking to strengthen the country's fiscal and social resilience through an integrated national system that combines public funds, transfer instruments, risk and rapid disbursement mechanisms linked to early warnings and the National Loss and Damage Mechanism. This promotes the operationalization of the National Fund for Prevention, Mitigation, and Emergency Response (FONAPRE), the development of parametric insurance, agricultural and housing microinsurance, and advance transfers triggered by alerts. The measure also promotes public-private partnerships with insurers, cooperatives, and financial institutions to expand parametric and microinsurance coverage among vulnerable households and MSMEs.

06. Climate Transparency Component

The Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) is a key element for the implementation of Article 13 of the Paris Agreement, by generating the necessary inputs to analyze the progress of climate action and the contribution of each country to global climate change goals.

In this regard, Honduras has made significant progress in the development of the National System of Climate Change Management (SNMCC), led by the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (SERNA), through the National Directorate of Climate Change (DNCC). The system aims to make the monitoring of climate actions implemented in the country more transparent and to facilitate the development of reporting instruments that support national priorities and international commitments to the UNFCCC.

The National Climate Change Monitoring System (SNMCC) is the official tool for collecting, processing, analyzing, and reporting climate information in an integrated, transparent, and verifiable manner. It was developed within the framework of the CBIT-Honduras Project, executed by SERNA and implemented with the support of the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and funding from the Global Environment Facility (GEF).

Structure of the SNMCC

The System is hosted by the Climate Change Management and Monitoring Unit (UGMCC) of the DNCC. The SNMCC is a comprehensive system comprising a set of modules and submodules designed to address various aspects related to transparency and climate action in Honduras. Its structure is organized into seven (7) main modules for collecting and processing information and two (2) complementary modules for reporting and disseminating climate information for the country.

The system modules represent the country's large climate information sets (e.g., mitigation, adaptation, or financing), while the submodules form the logical framework for the development of each of the information elements that the country needs (e.g. INGEI, climate scenarios, projections, or support received).

Both the modules and submodules are interconnected, allowing for the development of the necessary elements within a specific topic or area. The system defines the function and interaction of each module and submodule, as well as their importance in the context of climate transparency and how they contribute to the collection, management, and presentation of climate data.

Strategic Actions to Improve Climate Transparency

Strategic Action 1 — Operationalization of the platform modules of the National System of Climate Change Monitoring (SNMCC). Operationalize the remaining modules of the system — Mitigation (MRV), Adaptation, Loss and Damage, Climate Action Instruments, Markets, Cross-Cutting Elements, and Knowledge Management — along with the deployment and piloting of version 2.0. The integration of sectoral data flows will be promoted through formal information exchange agreements, strengthening interoperability among institutions. Simultaneously, progress will be made in aligning the system with national and international climate reporting instruments, ensuring the coherence and traceability of the information generated.

Strategic Action 2 — Institutional Arrangements as an official tool for climate transparency. Review, disseminate, and issue the SNMCC Regulations, currently in draft form, ensuring a clear regulatory framework for its operation. Furthermore, the creation of the National Climate Change Observatory (ONCC) will be promoted in coordination with academia to support the system's information management. The governance framework among the participating institutions will be strengthened.

Strategic Action 3 — Design the NDC tracking module and develop the Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) and Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) systems. Design MRV and MEL systems, aligned with international requirements and national priorities for climate transparency, defining their objective, scope, roles and responsibilities, activities, data flows, results, and resources necessary for their operationalization. This action will also establish the fundamental elements of a national transparency system that allows compliance with the Modalities, Procedures, and Guidelines (MPG) of the Paris Agreement, while reviewing current regulations to identify the relevant institutional roles and mitigation and adaptation policies for the effective implementation of NDC 3.0.
