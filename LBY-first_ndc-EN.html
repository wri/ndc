As Minister of Environment, it is my honor to present our country’s first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). This document represents more than a climate commitment—it is a development strategy for resilience, equity, and long-term prosperity.

Climate change is not a distant risk for Libya. Its impacts are already being felt across our regions, from coastal flooding and salinization in the north to drought, water scarcity, and desertification in the south. With solidarity and support, Libya can transform climate challenges into opportunities—building a future that is more secure, sustainable, and just for all Libyans, particularly those most affected by climate risks.

First Nationally Determined Contribution of Libya

I. Executive Summary

Libya's first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) marks a crucial starting point in the country's journey toward climate action under the Paris Agreement. This initial NDC reflects Libya's intention to gradually integrate climate priorities into national development plans, promote environmental sustainability, and build the institutional and technical foundations necessary to respond to climate change. These efforts are made despite ongoing challenges which previously prevented Libya from submitting an NDC.

Building on a 2017 starting year and a 2023 reference year, the NDC translates sector plans into quantified targets for 2026–2035 using a Business-as-Usual (BAU) trajectory and a bottom-up mitigation scenario.

The mitigation target of Libya's national NDC is to reduce its carbon intensity by 28% by 2035 compared to 2023 levels using domestic resources or by 49% provided international support is provided (Figure 1). This target is appropriate for Libya as its economy is still growing. Using carbon intensity—the amount of emissions per unit of GDP—allows the economy to grow while making that growth cleaner and more efficient. It reflects Libya's current stage of economic transition and aligns with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities under the Paris Agreement.

Figure 1: Carbon intensity trajectory of Libya according to conditional and unconditional GHG Mitigation scenarios over the period 2023-2035

The objective to reduce carbon intensity will be pursued through a package of sectoral mitigation measures. The largest contributions will come from gas flaring recovery (35.9%), power generation (26%), and energy efficiency measures including electricity (15.4%). Together these account for over three quarters of projected emission reductions.

Gas flaring recovery focuses on capturing and utilizing natural gas currently wasted in oil production, providing both emissions reduction and additional energy supply. In power generation, mitigation measures involve shifting to cleaner fuels and modernizing plants. Energy efficiency programmes target reduced electricity demand through improved building performance, efficient appliances, and industrial upgrades. Complementary actions include renewable energy deployment (10.5%), tree planting and land restoration (4.5%), and low-emission transport improvements (5.2%), all of whom collectively enhance Libya's transition toward a diversified, low-carbon energy system.

Delivering this trajectory requires sustained investment. Total mitigation needs for 2026–2035 are estimated at ~USD 29.6 billion, with ~USD 9.2 billion derived from domestic resources and ~USD 20.4 billion from external support. Sector-specific "unconditional contribution rates" determine what can realistically be financed domestically, with the residual forming conditional needs to be supported by international partners.

In parallel, the NDC advances adaptation through eleven priority measures that strengthen resilience across key system — water, agriculture, health, coastal zones, and ecosystems—guided by the findings of the 2025 Climate Risk Assessment and the National Adaptation Plan. By 2035, the NDC aims to:

  • Reduce by 30% the population exposed to recurrent droughts and water scarcity through modernized irrigation, groundwater protection, and expansion of desalination and reuse systems;
  • Protect 70% of priority coastal and urban zones against flooding and sea-level rise through integrated coastal management and risk-sensitive spatial planning;
  • Increase by 40% the adoption of drought-resilient crops and climate-smart agricultural practices;
  • Restore 150,000 ha. of degraded drylands and biodiversity corridors; and ensure that at least 50% of health facilities and 30% of critical infrastructure meet climate-resilience standards by 2030.

Implementation of the mitigation and adaptation measures is coordinated through the National Climate Change Committee and its Executive Office (NCCC-EO). The NCCC-EO supported by Sectoral Mitigation and Climate Change Committees (MACCCs) and Sectoral Adaptation and Climate Change Committees (SACCCs) to convert policy into deliverable outcomes and to monitor their implementation. The MACCCs include the energy sector, industry, transportation, agriculture, solid waste and wastewater while the SACCCs include water, agriculture, biodiversity, health, energy, and infrastructure.

Implementing these measures requires ~2.9 USD billion in financing of which USD 350 million (12%) is to be provided using domestic resources and USD 2.55 billion (88%) with external support including that from GIZ–EU STEP and SECCAR initiatives. Conditional measures include large-scale water and ecosystem restoration, coastal defenses, and digital monitoring systems, while unconditional measures focus on institutional strengthening, local adaptation planning, and community-based resilience.

Governance and transparency arrangements ensure credible delivery. The NDC will operationalize an MRV system for mitigation and a complementary MEL system for adaptation, converging under the Enhanced Transparency Framework. Each sector will track not only emission reductions and resilience outcomes but also support needed and received—finance, technology transfer and capacity-building—informing Biennial Transparency Reports and guiding iterative improvements.

The programme is designed to further a just energy transition by lowering energy costs, improving air quality and public health, diversifying the economy including creates green jobs, especially for youth and underserved regions. Nature-based solutions such as afforestation, rangeland/oasis restoration and coastal ecosystem protection reinforce resilience and carbon sequestration. Libya’s unconditional track anchors near-term progress.

Libya signed the Paris Agreement in 2016 and intends to ratify the Paris Agreement as soon as practicable. The current NDC aligns with the core principles of the Agreement, particularly the Enhanced Transparency Framework (Article 13). Libya plans to establish a robust MRV system, begin submitting Biennial Transparency Reports (BTRs), and adopt long-term, low-emission strategies in future NDC updates. Libya calls on development partners and the international community to support its ambitious climate agenda. Targeted international finance, technology transfer, and capacity-building partnerships are essential to scale up implementation and ensure long-term impact.

II. National Circumstances

Libya, the 17th largest country globally, covers an expansive area of approximately 1.7 million squares kilometers and is home to around 7.3 million people, with nearly 80% of its population residing in urban areas. The nation is predominantly desert, with about 95% of its land area classified as such. Forests, which are mainly concentrated in the Green Mountain area, cover a mere 0.12% of the total land area and have been significantly reduced over the past three decades. Libya’s extensive coastline along the Mediterranean Sea spans nearly 2,000 kilometers and supports an exclusive economic zone of approximately 364,696 square kilometers. 1

Libya possesses substantial hydrocarbon resources, with proven crude oil reserves accounting for 3% of the global total, making it the largest reserve in Africa and the ninth largest worldwide. Exploitation of oil and gas creates substantial opportunity for investment and development. To insulate itself from global price fluctuations, contribute to the energy transition and reduce its exposure to environmental vulnerabilities, Libya is working to diversify its economy.

Libya has demonstrated a commitment to addressing environmental issues and climate change through its participation in various international frameworks and agreements over the years. As a member of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), Libya aligned itself with international climate governance structures. Libya is a party to the Basel Convention and the Montreal Protocol reflecting its efforts to regulate hazardous waste and phase out ozone-depleting substances, though it has yet to adopt the Kigali Amendment, which targets hydrofluorocarbon emissions. Its participation in initiatives like the UN Framework for Cooperation in Sustainable Development underscores its dedication to resilience-building and sustainable growth. These frameworks provide opportunities to integrate climate adaptation, biodiversity conservation, and emissions reduction into a cohesive national strategy.

The development of Libya’s first NDC marks a crucial step in formalizing its climate action goals. It represents a strategic effort to define national targets for mitigation, adaptation, and sustainable development, ensuring that climate considerations are embedded in Libya’s broader policy landscape and that future development aligns with environmental sustainability and global climate goals. 2

Climate Trends and Impacts

The southern Mediterranean, including Libya, is warming faster than the global average. Over the past 50 years, temperatures have risen 1.85°C across Libya. 3 The warming trend is particularly intense along the coast. 4 Over the next forty years, under moderate scenarios (SSP3-7.0), average Libya surface temperatures are projected to increase an additional 1.7°C from 22.20°C between 1995-2014 to 23.95°C between 2040-2059. 5 By mid-century (2040-2059), summers will become dangerously hot with average warming of 2.38°C during the hottest month in August. 6 As a result, the number of extreme heat days during the summer (>40°C) will double from 5.66 to 11.46 with the average maximum temperature reaching 46.62°C. There will similarly be a fivefold increase in tropical nights (>26°C) from 2.88 days to 14.39 days making sleep difficult without cooling.

Increasing humidity will increase the danger created by heat to human health. The Heat Index (HI) threshold, which combines temperature and humidity, indicates that 32°C is the limit for safety. This increase in humidity is a change for Libya as its climate has typically been arid. There is a high degree of variability by locality, with the greatest risk of heat is in the northwestern regions. Currently, Zuwara has the highest number of days, 13.68, with a HI >= 35°C while the northeast has the lowest, with places like Derna having 0.54 days with HI >= 35°C. Increased heat leads to higher energy demand for cooling while increasing technical losses in electricity during transmission. Libya will experience increasing variability in rainfall with more intense precipitation events expected more frequently, increasing the likelihood of floods. Northern Libya will continue with a trend of decreasing precipitation, while the south will continue to experience a slight increase in precipitation. Nationwide, precipitation has decreased by 1.6 mm per decade from 1971 to 2020. As temperatures rise, the already small area in Libya that is characterised by the Mediterranean climate, where rainfall remains significant, will reduce in size, with sand and dust storms increasing in frequency and intensity. These shifts are expected to intensify pressure on water resources critical to agriculture and pastoralism, particularly in the southern regions, potentially fueling competition over water sources and grazing areas.

Increasing sea levels will increase the threat to coastal areas and the infiltration of seawater into groundwater systems. Sea level is expected to rise by 0.23 meters by 2050 and 0.68 by 2100. Due to its lower elevation and high population density, the areas most vulnerable to sea level rise will be the oil crescent and low-lying cities such as Benghazi. 7 A much larger area will be affected by stronger storm surges, which could cause infrastructural damage, including to Libya’s vast network of oil infrastructure. Much of the sea level rise is driven by the warming of the Mediterranean, whose sea surface temperature averages 19.2°C and is expected to increase by 1.3°C by mid-century (2041-2060) and 2.8°C by the end of the century. The higher temperatures contribute to the intensity and frequency of storms, which generally occur in summer and early fall. The 2023 flood that impacted north-west Libya as well as floods in other urban areas demonstrates the high costs of these vulnerabilities. These climate changes pose significant risks to Libya’s environment and socio-economic stability, with desertification threatening agricultural land and food security, while land degradation impacts coastal regions and oases. 8

Increased temperatures and more sporadic rainfall contribute to existing constraints to Libya’s water supply. As the country has no perennial rivers, it depends almost entirely on non-renewable fossil aquifers, notably the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System and the Northwestern Sahara Aquifer System (NWSAS). These transboundary water reserves are being depleted faster than they can be recharged, with limited governance frameworks in place for sustainable use. Additionally, the Great Man-Made River (GMMR)-one which conveys the water from these aquifers providing over 80% of Liyba’s fresh water is facing increasing technical vulnerabilities making Libya’s water security increasingly fragile.

Libya’s population of 7.3 million is highly urbanized, with 86% living in cities— mainly concentrated along the Mediterranean coast where Tripoli, Benghazi, and Misrata together host over 40% of the population. While the coastal belt holds most economic activity and infrastructure, the Fezzan region remains sparsely populated and underdeveloped. Since 2011, rapid and largely unplanned urban expansion has intensified pressures on peri-urban areas and exposed populations to hazards such as flash floods, heat stress, water insecurity, and heightened social vulnerability, particularly among displaced and low-income groups.

Libya’s biodiversity—ranging from the woodlands of Jabal al Akhdar and Jebel Nafusa, to fragile desert oases, and its extensive Mediterranean coastline—is increasingly threatened by climate change and human pressures. Rising temperatures, drought, and desertification are degrading highland and oasis ecosystems, while sea-level rise, coastal erosion, and overfishing endanger marine habitats and species such as monk seals and loggerhead turtles. These impacts undermine ecological stability, food security, and local livelihoods. Despite being party to global biodiversity agreements, Libya’s conservation system remains under-resourced, making climate-informed, community-led, and nature-based adaptation critical to safeguarding its ecological heritage.

Linkages to national development plans or strategies

Libya’s first NDC is designed to complement and reinforce national development priorities as set out in the Government of National Unity’s Development Plan for 2025–2026. The NDC serves not only as a climate commitment but also as a framework that strengthens economic recovery by significantly boosting GDP growth, diversification, resilience, and more generally sustainable growth.

Specifically, it promotes renewable energy, energy efficiency, and sustainable land management, supporting national goals to reduce dependence on oil and expand green economic opportunities.

Its adaptation measures—such as efficient irrigation, drought-resilient crops, and integrated water resource management—directly support national strategies addressing the high share of water consumed by agriculture and the need to secure domestic food production. In doing so, the NDC complements and furthers the objectives of the 2025 National Drought Management Strategy and Action Plan as well as the 2023 National Biodiversity Strategy, by advancing measures that strengthen water security, enhance ecosystem resilience, and promote biodiversity protection.

The NDC also supports Libya’s obligations under the SDGs, the African Union Agenda 2063, and the Arab Strategy for Sustainable Development, ensuring coherence between climate action and broader development frameworks. It is a central contribution to the UN Sustainable Development Cooperation Framework (UNSDCF) for Libya. Its mitigation targets will build support for green investment—including renewables, energy efficiency, gas flaring reduction, and waste recovery, —thereby diversifying the economy and dependence on fossil fuels. It is also an important contribution to adaptation planning, strengthening resilience and risk reduction, both of which are vital vehicles for implementing the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.

Governance and Institutional Framework for Climate Action

The State of Libya recognizes that effective climate action requires a coherent, inclusive, and territorially responsive institutional framework. To this end, Libya has progressively established a multi-level climate governance system that integrates national, sectoral, and subnational actors to ensure strategic coherence, technical depth, and contextual relevance.

Climate governance in Libya is anchored in the National Climate Change Committee (NCCC), established under Cabinet Decree No. 336/2021. The NCCC serves as the primary coordinating body for climate policy, providing high-level direction, inter-ministerial coordination, and reporting to the Office of the Prime Minister, which ensures political oversight and policy and financial support.

To carry out its tasks, the Executive Office of the National Committee for Climate Change (EO-NCCC) was established by the Minister of Environment, in his role as Chairman of the National Committee for Climate Change, pursuant to Decree No. 247 of 2023 as a multi-sectoral coordination mechanism. The NCCC-EO serves as the technical and executive arm of the NCCC, and includes representatives from key ministries including the Ministries of Water Resources, Agriculture, Industry, Transport, Local Government, Health, the General Electricity Company and the National Oil Corporation to develop and implement strategic climate documents such as the National Determined Contribution and the National Adaptation Plan. The EO-NCCC will also lead a progress review, ensuring policy coherence across sectors, and identifying the complementarity of mitigation and adaptation actions.

To strengthen sectoral ownership and technical implementation, the EO-NCCC has initiated the creation of Sectoral Mitigation Climate Change Committees (SMCCC) and Sectoral Adaptation Climate Change Committees (SACCC). These committees, hosted within lead sectoral ministries, include representatives from affiliated departments, subnational authorities, academia, civil society, and the private sector. SMCCCs have been established in key sectors including energy (NOC and GECOL), transport, industry, agriculture, solid waste, and wastewater. SACCCs have been formed in health, agriculture, water resources, and biodiversity. They conduct sectoral risk assessments, identify and prioritize measures, and monitor implementation progress.

At the subnational level, municipalities play a critical role as frontline actors for localized adaptation, water management, resilience-building, but also as a critical actor to implement mitigation programs. Efforts are underway to strengthen their capacities and establish Municipal Climate Action Plans (MCAPs) through targeted training and financing mechanisms, ensuring that climate action delivers tangible community-level benefits.

Civil society organizations, academia, and the private sector serve as key partners in implementation, innovation, data generation, and monitoring. Their participation enhances transparency, promotes public awareness, and supports the piloting of context-specific solutions.

The Prime Minister serves as the national point of contact with the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), ensuring that national climate action is aligned with international commitments under the Paris Agreement. Technical institutions and national experts are also contributing to the preparation of the GHG emission inventory, the design of a system (monitoring, reporting and verification) and the modelling of climate scenarios.

Efforts are ongoing to strengthen the legal and regulatory frameworks for climate governance, including the integration of climate considerations into national development planning and the formulation of dedicated legislation to support long-term climate action. The Ministry of Planning will support the process of integrating sectoral components into implementation plans and mobilizing the required domestic resources.

Successful implementation of Libya’s first NDC (2026–2035) will depend on mobilizing adequate financial resources, ensuring access to appropriate technologies, and enhancing institutional and human capacities. The EO-NCCC will oversee the preparation of a comprehensive NDC Implementation Plan that links project pipelines, financing sources, and results reporting in line with the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF). This governance framework provides the backbone for effective implementation, monitoring, and accountability of Libya’s transition toward a low-carbon and climate-resilient future.

IV. Mitigation Contribution

Libya's vision for climate action aims to establish a robust framework that paves the way for sustainable long-term mitigation efforts. For this purpose, the Government of Libya is applying a set of policies and measures, combining policy- based and sectoral bottom-up methods to identify and quantify mitigation contributions. This approach ensures the participation of the main emitting sectors and integrates the mitigating measures from each of their sectoral decarbonization plans.

The NDC was developed based on extensive consultations with key stakeholders involved in climate change issues in Libya, including public institutions, businesses, civil society, and experts working in various fields and sectors related to GHG mitigation. These consultations were conducted through several national and sectoral workshops, as well as sectoral and individual meetings with key stakeholders, both on presential and online modes. The main sectors involved in this process include, among several others, the oil and gas, electricity, transportation, industry, agriculture, solid waste and wastewater.

These measures were then modelled to show how they contribute to lowering carbon emissions against a business-as-usual scenario. Sectoral contributions were then combined to show how they contribute to aggregated national targets. This process will be repeated in the future to progressively measure and enhance the integrity and transparency in future cycles.

In successive NDC cycles, Libya will strengthen its modelling through improved plant-level data, country-specific emission factors, and integration with macro- economic analysis. A national Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV)

protocol will be developed to ensure consistency and facilitate the upcoming preparation of Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR). This methodology ensures that Libya’s NDC is anchored in current national realities while providing quantified mitigation trajectories where feasible, and establishes a transparent pathway for enhanced precision, credibility, and ambition in future submissions. To enhance the alignment between the observed mitigation potential and national climate efforts, Libya is adopting a targeted approach by linking specific emission categories in the GHG inventory to corresponding mitigation actions in the NDC.

Libya intends to implement numerous mitigation measures, including a combination of policy regulations and infrastructure investments. These will result in cumulative emission reductions exceeding 250 million tCO2e over the entire period from 2026 to 2035. Mitigation Energy sector: Measures

The mitigation plan envisages the implementation of a comprehensive programme comprising 36 measures. These will be structured around six main components:

  1. The first component will target the oil and gas sector. The National Oil Corporation (NOC) is committed to continually develop its technical and institutional capacities and to strengthen its measurement, monitoring and reporting systems to ensure alignment with relevant international commitments. This reflects an institutional approach based on transparency, responsibility, and sustainability. The NOC’s engagement in specialized international initiatives and partnerships, most notably the Oil and Gas Methane Partnership (OGMP 2.0), along with related tools such as the Methane Alert and Response System (MARS) which provides monitoring and remote sensing capacities, represents a practical approach to enhancing the ability to detect methane emission sources early, analyze them, and address them in accordance with available technical and logistical capabilities. This engagement also reflects the Corporation’s continuous interest in actively participating in relevant local and international initiatives, as well as its clear support for national efforts to meet climate commitments. It further reinforces a culture of continuous improvement in environmental performance, contributing to environmental sustainability and enhancing the credibility of the oil and gas sector at both regional and international levels .
  2. The second component will cover the electricity sector through a comprehensive programme comprising a package of energy efficiency measures targeting electricity consumption in each sector, with an overall objective of improving energy performance by 40% in street lighting and by 15% in other sectors (households, public services, commerce, agriculture and industry). These objectives would be achievable through “technical” measures such as the widespread use of smart metering systems, the rehabilitation and retrofitting of street lighting networks and full retrofitting to LED, the promotion of LED lighting in all sectors (residential, services, industry, etc.), the implementation of energy labelling and MEPS for electrical appliances in all sectors (air conditioning, refrigeration, electrical water heaters, washing machines, motors, pumps, generalization of ISO50001 where relevant, etc.), or smart mechanisms such as transferring part of energy subsidies to support energy efficiency and renewable energy in all sectors, or credit systems via energy bills to implement technical measures. All of this will be implemented with a win-win approach for consumers, energy producers and the Libyan state.
  3. The third component will also target the electricity sector, from the perspective of electricity supply, and will mainly include three types of actions: (i) Grid modernization, including upgrades to transmission infrastructure and distribution networks, to reduce technical losses, (ii) Switch to gas (natural gas currently 67%, will reach 85% in 2035) and, (iii) Promote energy efficiency in electricity production through the use of combined cycle power plants (currently 45%, will reach 90% in 2035).
  4. The fourth component will also target the electricity sector, through the launch of a major programme to develop electricity production from renewable energies, with the aim of achieving a 22% share of the electricity mix through the main renewable technologies by 2035. This component will also include the launch of a strategic study and the commencement of the operational phase of the Green Hydrogen Technology Development programme.
  5. The fifth component will cover Energy Efficiency, mainly the thermal energy uses by the economic sectors. This includes: (i) Introducing compulsory Energy Auditing programmes in the major consumer sectors and entities, (ii) Implementing an ambitious program to developing Cogeneration and Trigeneration where relevant, (iii) Promoting the optimization of process-heat consumption in the industries, and (iv) Promoting the optimization of Insulation of heating and cooling circuits.
  6. The sixth component under the energy umbrella will target the transport sector through the launch of a major programme focusing on four areas: (i) Enhance the National Mobility System by building capacity for public/mass transport, including improvements to the bus system (introduction of dedicated BRT lanes, modern high- capacity buses, real-time ITS signals, discounted tickets for students and those with limited incomes, and electronic ticketing in Tripoli), etc. (ii) Launch a national campaign to encourage mass transport involving the private sector, (iii) Promote Hybrid and Electrical vehicles (target : 100.000 vehicles in use in 2035), both covering both private and public sectors, in particularly for the latter through a “Green cars in government institutions” achieving a shift towards low-emission transport by gradually replacing the government vehicle fleet with electric or hybrid vehicles, (iv) Promote EE + ”MEPS" + Maximum age of imported vehicles+ promoting driving practices + maintenance practices, etc.

Industrial processes : The mitigation plan for the Industrial processes will target the three main sources of emissions, comprising three measures:

(i) Ratify the Kigali Amendment to reduce HFC emissions, (ii) Reduce emissions from reheating furnaces (calcination, billet rolling, profile rolling and hot rolling) in the Iron&steel industry, (iii) Reduce the clinker/cement ratio from 0.93 to 0.90% by 2035, in particular by replacing clinker with blast furnace slag and fly ash from steelworks.

Agriculture, Forestry and Land Use Change: The mitigation plan primarily envisages an action aimed at strengthening the carbon absorption capacities of ecosystems in Libya, through the Prime minister initiative to green Libya: Planting 100 million trees, covering 150,000 hectares in various relevant regions and areas in Libya, to enhance carbon sequestration and contribute to ecosystem preservation. 10 This will include drought-resistant tree species around cities to act as windbreaks and microclimate stabilizers.

Business- as-Usual Scenario (BaU)

The baseline or BaU scenario was developed based on modeling work carried out separately for the various sectors, sub-sectors, and where relevant for sources such as flaring. Activity data projections are based on historical analyses, with particular emphasis on the period 2017–2023, which was covered by the GHG inventory. In many cases, such as for GDP, it was also necessary to go back to earlier periods, including those before 2011, when they were deemed more representative of future trends, or simply because more reliable data was available for the period prior to the Libyan revolution. Sectoral specificities are explained below:

§ Energy Sector: The assessment of emissions of the baseline scenario for the energy sector was based on a simple modelling approach of the final energy demand by sector (including transport) and by energy type (e.g. electricity). In the simulations, particular attention was paid to the electricity sector, which accounts for around one-third of Libya’s emissions (in 2023), but above all offers the greatest room for emissions reductions and therefore represents by far the most significant source of GHG mitigation in the Libyan context; i.e. almost half of the GHG mitigation potential for the 2026–2035 NDC period. Baseline emissions are assessed on a year-by-year basis from 2024 to 2035, applying the sectoral approach of the 2006 IPCC guidelines. This NDC covers almost all emission sources included in the GHG inventory and representing a significant source of GHG mitigation. In addition, the baseline comprehensively covers the main gases: CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, as well as HFCs used in Libya, which are then expressed in tons of CO₂ equivalent.

§ Industrial processes: The assessment of emissions in the baseline scenario was based on a trend extension of the evolution of activity data in the cement sector observed over the 2017–2023 inventory period. For iron and steel, production increased by 160% between 2017 and 2023, and as it is unlikely to increase further, production was considered stable at the same level as in 2023 for the entire period 2024–2035. With regard to HFCs, the 2017–2023 inventory considered their use to be stable over the 2017–2023 period, probably reflecting the lack of actual data on the use of these gases. The baseline therefore applied the same stability rule to their use and, consequently, to the related emissions. For the remainder of emissions attributable to industrial processes, the baseline scenario was simulated in a similar manner to the cement sector, based on a trend extension of emissions observed over the 2017–2023 inventory period.

§ Agriculture: The baseline considered the continuation of the trend in emissions from livestock farming, as reflected in the 2017–2023 trajectory. For aggregate sources and non-CO₂ emissions sources on land, interannual emissions do not show any truly noticeable trajectories, fluctuating within a very narrow range. The baseline therefore used the 2017–2023 annual average emissions as a stable basis for emissions for the period 2024–2035.

§ Forest and Other Land Use (FOLU): The GHG inventory did not cover these sources, reflecting the lack of activity data enabling the estimation of related emissions/removals. Consequently, this source was ignored in BaU projections.

§ Solid Waste and Wastewater: For solid waste the baseline considered the continuation of the trend in emissions from existing uncontrolled dumps, as reflected in the 2017–2023 trajectory. For wastewater treatment the interannual evolution of emissions over the period 2017–2023 does not show any truly noticeable trajectories, fluctuating from year to year downward or upward within a very narrow range. The baseline therefore used the 2017–2023 annual average emissions as a stable basis for emissions for the period 2024–2035.

Co-Benefits of Mitigation Measures

The mitigation initiatives proposed in Libya’s NDC are designed to deliver multiple co-benefits that extend beyond reducing greenhouse gas emissions. They will stimulate economic diversification, generate employment—particularly for youth—and strengthen national capacity to deliver energy, water, and services more efficiently.

Energy Sector: As part of its contribution to reducing emissions, the implementation of gas flaring reduction projects in the oil and gas sector will avoid an estimated 90 million tons of carbon dioxide equivalent emissions during the period 2026-2035. This measure will also contribute to saving the equivalent of 40 million tons of oil equivalent of natural gas for domestic use or export, representing total additional economic revenues estimated at approximately USD 14 billion. 13

Similarly, energy efficiency measures and the switch to natural gas in the power sector are expected to save an additional 40 million toe over the same period, generating cumulative savings of about USD 29 billion. The transition will also foster the emergence of new economic sectors, particularly in renewable energy, generating high added value and creating jobs. Overall, NDC implementation in the energy sector is projected to increase GDP by about 1 to 2 percentage point annually between 2026 and 2035 compared to the baseline, while creating thousands of new jobs and yielding significant budgetary gains through energy subsidy savings and additional export revenues. These measures will also provide technological and strategic advantages for Libya in the energy and industrial sectors.

Industrial Processes: In the cement and iron and steel industries, adopting cleaner technologies will lower production costs, benefit consumers and related sectors, while reducing pollution and improving air quality.

Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU): Tree planting and sustainable land management will restore degraded land and enrich tree cover across Libya, contributing to the creation of green urban spaces that improve environmental quality and quality of life. These initiatives will also support Libya meeting its objectives in relation with other international conventions, such as biodiversity and desertification, reinforcing cross-sectoral sustainability.

Waste Sector: Investments in waste management and recycling will promote the emergence of new sectors focused on waste recovery and the circular economy, creating significant employment opportunities. Improved management of solid and liquid waste will help preserve soil, water resources, and public health, while the reuse of treated wastewater will provide vital inputs for large-scale afforestation and agricultural programmes under the NDC.

Beyond these sectoral benefits, Libya’s climate actions are designed to contribute to poverty reduction, inclusion, and stability. By linking climate action with national development priorities, the NDC seeks to ensure that the benefits of low- carbon development reach all regions and social groups, with special attention to women, youth, and vulnerable populations in underserved regions such as the Fezzan. Investment in renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, waste management, and green infrastructure will help create decent jobs, strengthen local value chains, and support communities most affected by both climate impacts and economic transition. Skill development programmes will enable workers to adapt to new technologies and participate in emerging green sectors, while social protection measures will cushion potential short-term economic disruptions.

The NDC also embeds the principles of a just and equitable transition. As a non- Annex I Party to the UNFCCC, Libya’s commitment to significantly reduce its carbon intensity below 2023 levels—despite contributing less than 0.2% of global emissions—demonstrates fairness and ambition. Through these enabling policies and socially responsive measures, Libya’s NDC ensures that its transition to a low- carbon, climate-resilient economy is also a transition toward greater equity, regional balance, and national cohesion.

By linking climate action with economic diversification, employment, and social inclusion, Libya reaffirms its commitment to a just, prosperous, and sustainable future for all its citizens.

V. Adaptation Contribution *

Libya faces acute climate vulnerabilities shaped by its arid–hyper-arid geography, fragile water systems, and the concentration of more than 90% of its population and infrastructure along the coastal belt. Intensifying hazards—heat extremes, drought, flash floods, salinization, sea-level rise, and sand and dust storms—are compounded by institutional fragility and conflict dynamics. Dependence on non-renewable water resources and food imports (over 80%) further heightens national exposure and limits resilience.

In response, Libya has defined an adaptation vision, in line with Article 7 of the Paris Agreement, to build a climate-resilient society that progressively scales up measures across food, water, health and ecosystems to safeguard lives and livelihoods while advancing inclusive and equitable development. These measures will contribute to the Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA) to inform successive NAP and NDC reporting cycles. The strategy emphasizes two enabling platforms: the National Adaptation Intelligence and Coordination Platform (NAICP) and the Climate Risk Intelligence and Decision Support System (CIDSS). These provide the governance and data backbone for evidence-based decision- making, early warning, and ETF-compatible reporting.

Building on this foundation, Libya has prioritized eleven adaptation measures that address its dominant hazards. These include coastal defenses, oasis water resilience, climate-smart agriculture and fisheries, climate-resilient health systems, municipal infrastructure upgrades, nature-based solutions, renewable-energy-powered water and sanitation, ecosystem protection in Jabal al Akhdar and coastal wetlands, and sustainable urban flood management. Together, these measures maximize co-benefits for employment, health, biodiversity, and system efficiency. Indicative costs range from USD 1.3 billion for enabling platforms to USD 1.7 billion for large-scale coastal infrastructure, underscoring the urgency of international finance mobilization.

Libya’s adaptation strategy is country-driven and participatory, informed by consultations with ministries, municipalities, academia, and women and youth groups. It is integrated across sectors and scales, linking water, food, health, energy, and ecosystems; progressively ambitious, designed for iterative enhancement through NAP/NDC cycles; and grounded in transparency and learning, with NAICP and CIDSS enabling reporting under the ETF and credible contributions to the Global Stocktake. This dual focus on national ownership and international accountability positions Libya as a committed partner in global climate action.

VI. Means of Implementation

Mitigation Finance Needs

Due to the uncertainties that characterize the Libyan context there are several issues captured in the “conditionality” required for implementation. First among these is the country's limited capacity to mobilize all the financing required for a mitigation program of this scale from its own national resources. The unfavorable economic context since 2011 has led the Libyan government to generally opt for budgetary trade-offs that prioritize vital expenditures and investments. This initial characterization of the concept of conditionality reflects Libya's need for rapid access to international financing resources on the best possible terms, particularly those specializing in climate policy support.

The second criterion characterizing the concept of conditionality in the Libyan context stems from Libya's need for substantial support in terms of technical assistance, capacity building, and technology transfers.

The third factor justifying the consideration of conditionality in the Libyan context stems from the need for Libya to establish effective mechanisms for addressing technical risks and overcoming structural inertia that could prevent projects from reaching their full potential or even jeopardize their success in implementing mitigation programs and achieving GHG emission reduction targets.

The fourth factor is in regard to balancing the trade-offs in utilizing natural resources such as water. This is particularly important for the tree planting programs which can only be implemented if sufficient sustainable water is secured.

Private Sector Engagement and Blended Finance for Adaptation

Private investment in adaptation is currently nascent in Libya, constrained by regulatory uncertainty, political risk and shallow financial intermediation. Still, there is near-term potential where commercial incentives align with resilience outcomes, notably in efficient irrigation and agro-logistics, decentralized water treatment and reuse, nature-positive land restoration with sustainable value chains, and renewable energy solutions serving water and rural systems. To crowd in capital, Libya will structure adaptation pipelines with clear revenue models and measurable resilience outputs, distinguishing CapEx (infrastructure, digital systems, ecosystem restoration) from OpEx (extension, monitoring, local governance, MEL), and using standardized, constant-price costing bands to guide sequencing and partner dialogue.

A blended-finance approach is envisaged: concessional public and climate funds de-risking private participation through guarantees, first-loss tranches and results-based disbursements; PPP contracts embedding resilience clauses and performance KPIs; green instruments (including sukuk) for bankable assets; and targeted credit lines and parametric insurance for MSMEs and farmers. Domestically, the Environmental Protection Fund and public banks can act as on- lenders and co-financiers, channeling resources toward municipalities, women and youth-led enterprises, and underserved regions. This framework will be anchored in transparent MRV/MEL systems and climate budget tagging, enabling alignment with GCF/GEF/AF access modalities and progressive scaling as legal, fiduciary, and project-preparation reforms mature.

VII. Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) and Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning Framework (MEL)

Libya’s approach to transparency under the Paris Agreement is based on the parallel development of a Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) system for mitigation and a Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (MEL) system for adaptation. Together, these systems form the backbone of Libya’s Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF), ensuring credibility, comparability, and continuous improvement in national climate action.

MRV System for Mitigation

The MRV system will track the design and implementation of mitigation measures across all major emitting sectors — energy, transport, industry, agriculture, waste, and land use. Implementation will be coordinated by national institutions under the guidance of the National Climate Change Committee (NCCC) and its Executive Office (EO-NCCC), which will oversee data collection, validation, and reporting to ensure a consistent national framework.

  • Measurement: Based on IPCC 2006 Guidelines (and 2019 refinements), national and sectoral data will be compiled using standard methodologies. Emissions data will be verified for accuracy and completeness, drawing on both national statistics and international datasets when necessary.
  • Reporting: Sectoral data and progress on policy implementation will be compiled into national GHG inventories and Biennial Transparency Reports (BTRs). Reports will include qualitative and quantitative updates on mitigation measures, sectoral emissions trends, and implementation progress toward unconditional and conditional NDC targets.
  • Verification: Verification will be conducted through inter-ministerial technical review and independent expert assessment. The MRV process will harmonize with international methodologies and will evolve over time as national data systems mature.

The MRV system ensures that Libya’s mitigation actions are quantifiable, transparent, and verifiable. The system will evolve iteratively through successive NDC and BTR cycles to ensure fulfilment of requirements under the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF). To meet this target an institutional capacity building program will be developed to train national stakeholders in emissions accounting, data management, and climate reporting. This system will evolve over time and is expected to include data from the forthcoming National System of Energy Statistics, which will allow for the development of regular energy balances and more detailed emissions data tracking.

MEL System for Adaptation

In parallel, Libya is establishing a Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (MEL) system to assess progress on adaptation. The MEL system will track the implementation and effectiveness of resilience-building measures, ensuring that adaptation is integrated into national planning and contributes to the Global Goal on Adaptation and the ETF.

  • Monitoring will collect data on the implementation of adaptation projects, such as hectares of land restored, operational water–energy–food nexus hubs, or percentage reduction in urban flood losses.
  • Evaluation will assess outcomes using vulnerability, resilience, and equity indicators to measure whether interventions are reducing risks and supporting vulnerable groups.
  • Learning will ensure that lessons from implementation are reintegrated into future NDCs, the National Adaptation Plan (NAP), and sectoral planning cycles.

The emerging MEL system will build on national platforms such as the National Adaptation Intelligence and Coordination Platform (NAICP) and the Climate Risk Intelligence and Decision-Support System (CIDSS), ensuring compatibility with ETF reporting requirements. To ensure inclusive resilience, Libya will mainstream the rights and needs of persons with disabilities across adaptation planning, implementation, and monitoring.

The development of Libya’s First NDC emerged from a collaborative process spearheaded by the National Climate Change Committee chaired by the Ministry of Environment. This created a territorial framework that integrates physical and socio-economic dimensions of climate risk. This enables differentiated analysis and informed targeted adaptation strategies tailored to the specific climate risks of each terrestrial region within Libya. From this analysis, prioritized adaptation measures were identified and developed to promote risk-informed development and anticipatory adaptation.

National and international experts contributed technical expertise, conducting in-depth analyses to pinpoint climate mitigation and adaptation strategies best suited to Libya’s specific needs, including addressing water scarcity and combating desertification.

Both the mitigation and adaptation sides were then brought together to create synergies between them in an effort to maximize the co-benefits contributing to sustainable national development. The plans outlined in the NDC will be further developed in subsequent Biennial Transparency Reports and future NDCs. This approach underscores Libya’s commitment to a participatory and evidence-based climate planning process, resulting in an NDC that is both ambitious and practical.

5. Assumptions and methodological approaches

5(a)

Assumptions and methodological approaches used for accounting for anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and removals corresponding to the Party’s nationally determined contribution, consistent with decision 1/CP.21, paragraph 31, and accounting guidance adopted by the CMA;

Libya’s accounting for anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and removals corresponding to its NDC is consistent with decision 1/CP.21, para. 31, and CMA-adopted accounting guidance, and is aligned with the Paris Agreement Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF).

Libya accounts GHG emissions and removals for Energy, IPPU (incl. cement and iron & steel), and Waste (solid waste disposal sites and wastewater). Gases covered are CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and HFCs (other F-gases to be added as robust data become available). The geographical boundary is national; timeframe is 2026–2035 with a single-year target in 2035. Targets, expressed in terms of reduction in carbon intensity, are expressed relative to the level of such carbon intensity in 2023.

Inventory guidelines and GWPs. Libya compiles and will report emissions and removals in accordance with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (using the 2019 Refinement where applicable) and applies 100-year Global Warming Potentials to convert gases to CO₂e (HFC species- specific GWPs). Where country-specific data support higher tiers, they are used; otherwise Tier 1 defaults are applied.

5(b)

Assumptions and methodological approaches used for accounting for the implementation of policies and measures or strategies in the nationally determined contribution;

The emissions under the NDC-Mitigation scenario anticipated for 2035 result from the implementation of all policies and measures in the various emitting sectors. The same assumptions and approaches as those used in the GHG inventory are used to account for the results of the implementation of policies/measures/strategies in the NDC.

Mitigation assessment is carried out using a bottom-up approach, starting with the identification of mitigation measures and their assessment in terms of the quantities of GHG avoided. Emission reductions are quantified with reference to a business-as-usual scenario. The results are then compiled by sector and aggregated at national level. The trajectory of the mitigation scenario is derived by subtracting, year by year, the mitigation results from business-as-usual scenario.
