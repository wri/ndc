EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF MOROCCO’S NDC 3.0 (2026-2035)

The Kingdom of Morocco has the honour of submitting its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) for the period 2026-2035 to the UNFCCC Secretariat, in accordance with the Paris Agreement, in particular its Articles 4.2 and 4.11, and with paragraph 24 of decision 1/CP.21.

Although a low emitter of Greenhouse Gases (GHG), Morocco remains highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Morocco’s Fifth National Communication (NC5) projects a marked shift towards a hotter and drier climate by 2050. Mean annual temperatures are expected to rise by more than 3 °C nationally, with increases of up to 5 °C in some regions of the country. At the same time, annual precipitation is expected to decline across the whole territory, with decreases mainly between 5 and 15 %, reaching up to 20 % in some areas.

This trend is likely to deplete water resources, increase the variability of rainy seasons and heighten vulnerability to drought episodes. For example, 2023 proved to be the driest year in at least eight decades, with a rainfall deficit estimated at 48 %. Such a situation aggravates water stress that is already particularly worrying: renewable resources have fallen from 2 560 to about 620 m³ per inhabitant over the last six decades (1960-2025), placing the country at a critical level, very close to the absolute scarcity threshold of 500 m³.

These changes are reflected in an increase in the frequency and intensity of heatwaves, a resurgence of forest fires, heightened heat stress and growing pressure on water resources. In this respect, in 2023 Morocco recorded a total of 26 extreme weather events that gave rise to warning bulletins, including intense thunderstorms (35 %), heatwaves (30 %), snowfall (19 %) and strong winds (16 %).

Given the severity and high cost of these impacts, adaptation stands out as a top-order strategic priority. In this regard, Morocco reaffirms its determination to contribute actively to the global response to the challenge of climate change, within the framework of sustainable development and the fight against poverty.

From this perspective, Morocco’s national climate change policy is institutionally anchored in the National Sustainable Development Strategy (SNDD 2035), which promotes balanced and sustainable development consistent with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 2030), the New Development Model (NMD 2035) and the national low-carbon roadmap to 2050. This strategy covers six major areas of transformation directly linked to climate issues, including: water, energy and food security, the development of a competitive and low-carbon economy, and the enhancement and resilience of natural resources and ecosystems in the face of climate change. It also relies on several levers of change, namely: human capital, digital technology, innovation, research, regulatory reforms, economic and fiscal instruments, and integrated territorial planning.

In addition to the SNDD, Morocco’s policy is expressed through other strategic documents, in particular the National Climate Plan (PCN) and the National Strategic Adaptation Plan (PNSA), the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), specific sectoral strategies, as well as Territorial Climate Plans and Regional Adaptation Plans. The ultimate objective is to make the national territory more resilient to climate change while ensuring a transition to a low-carbon economy.

To strengthen the implementation of its climate policy and raise its ambition, Morocco undertook a process of developing NDC 3.0 based on a participatory and inclusive approach involving the various stakeholders, in particular public institutions, the private sector, civil society, trade unions, the regions, as well as women and young people. This process was supported by the National Commission on Climate Change and Biological Diversity, which reviewed and validated the results of NDC 3.0. It exercised its prerogatives of consultation, coordination and monitoring of the implementation of Morocco’s climate commitments.

NDC 3.0 marks significant progress compared with NDC 2.0 published in 2021, raising ambition on GHG emission mitigation, notably by taking new GHGs into account, and aiming to further strengthen adaptive capacity and resilience by including new sectors and areas (Equipment - Transport and Biological Diversity).

In line with the recommendations of the First Global Stocktake of 2023 – tripling global renewable energy capacity by 2030, doubling the rate of energy efficiency improvement, and phasing down coal production and use – NDC 3.0 represents the highest possible level of ambition. Taking national circumstances into account, it sets targets and implementation measures in keeping with the principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities of countries.

The national mitigation potential of NDC 3.0 amounts to a 53 % reduction in GHG emissions by 2035, compared with the 45.5% set in NDC 2.0 for 2030. This target is set relative to emissions projected for the same year under a business-as-usual reference scenario. It comprises an unconditional reduction of 21.6 % by 2035, compared with the 18.3 % targeted in NDC 2.0 for 2030, which will be implemented through secured financial resources aligned with the State’s budget planning. Likewise, this NDC 3.0 includes a conditional reduction target of 31.4 % by 2035, compared with 27.2 % for NDC 2.0 by 2030, the achievement of which will depend on mobilising the required international support. NDC 3.0 was also an opportunity to better structure adaptation and resilience-building projects in several sectors.

In this context, a portfolio of 90 mitigation projects has been drawn up, requiring estimated financing of USD 60 billion. The objectives of NDC 3.0 are fully in line with the implementation of the national low-carbon roadmap, which steers Morocco’s policies towards a sustainable, low-carbon development model by 2050.

Furthermore, given the country’s vulnerability to the impacts of climate change, NDC 3.0 strengthens action on early warning, climate risk prevention and adaptation, with a view to reducing the loss and damage the country is already suffering.

To this end, 80 sectoral adaptation objectives have been identified for 2035. These objectives are broken down into 107 adaptation projects, divided into 61 unconditional and 46 conditional projects. It should be noted that Morocco’s NDC 3.0 provides for an integrated approach to the prevention and management of the risks of extreme events, in order to ensure the safety and protection of people, property and ecosystems. Adaptation financing needs are estimated at a total of about USD 36 billion.

NDC 3.0 builds on the Climate Finance Development Strategy (SDFC 2030), recognising the role the private sector can play and aiming to promote climate finance through three key pillars: (i) improving access to finance for climate projects, in particular through innovative financial instruments and mechanisms, (ii) strengthening the enabling environment for climate investment and (iii) promoting a just and inclusive transition by building capacity and facilitating access to green finance for sustainable institutions and enterprises.

The Kingdom of Morocco makes an innovative methodological contribution to the NDC revision process by highlighting the importance of economic efficiency and of the environmental and social co-benefits of climate action. In this respect, the use of the sectoral average abatement cost indicator (USD/t CO2eq reduced) is not only a tool for comparing the country’s sectoral performance in reducing GHG emissions, taking into account the scale of investment required and the volume of associated reductions, but would also make it possible to compare that performance between countries, between groups of countries or globally.

The use of this indicator would make it possible to aggregate countries’ efforts in the highest-emitting sectors and to estimate the progress still to be made in reducing GHG emissions. It would provide a basis for raising our collective ambition, while steering investment and financial flows towards a low-carbon trajectory by 2035. Finally, it would make a useful contribution to the second Global Stocktake in 2028, to guide joint action in the face of the climate emergency.

Moreover, Morocco has shown strong interest in carbon market mechanisms in order to mobilise additional finance. To harness this potential in practice, the country has already taken several steps to operationalise the cooperative approaches of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement and is engaged in developing eligible activities on its territory.

In addition, the Kingdom has deployed, as a first experience, an unprecedented effort to align the climate ambitions set out in its NDC with its national budget planning. By linking the development of NDC 3.0 with the Three-Year Budget Programming (PBT 2026-2028), the country ensures greater consistency between climate commitments and the public resources mobilised. This exercise strengthens the visibility, credibility, transparency and fiscal sustainability of Morocco’s climate commitments. It thus illustrates a climate-friendly model of financial governance, placing the climate transition at the heart of public financial management and strengthening the confidence of financial partners. This mechanism is being deployed at territorial level in order to involve local authorities and stakeholders and to anchor the climate transition in all territories.

NDC 3.0 is deeply rooted in the social dimension and promotes a just and inclusive transition. It incorporates the principles of equity, gender equality and employability. Its preparation was a broadly participatory process, marked by the intensive mobilisation of all political and societal stakeholders: young people, trade unions, gender focal points and representatives of the territories.

By placing these social and territorial priorities at the centre of its NDC 3.0, Morocco reaffirms its role as a dynamic actor in the global climate agenda. Its commitment is not limited to the ambition of mitigating GHG emissions, adaptation and building resilience, but extends to social justice and equity, ensuring that every citizen and every territory benefits from the positive spin-offs of this action.

1-INTRODUCTION

The Government of Morocco presents its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) for the period 2026-2035, hereinafter referred to as NDC 3.0, in accordance with Articles 4.2 and 4.11 of the Paris Agreement, paragraph 24 of decision 1/CP.21 and other relevant provisions of the Agreement.

Morocco frames its climate action within the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, with the aim of a sustainable transformation of the Kingdom while contributing responsibly to the global response to the threats posed by climate change, by implementing policies, strategies and plans that are inclusive, economically efficient, environmentally sustainable and socially equitable, in close alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) adopted by the United Nations (UN).

It is within this overall framework that Morocco’s new NDC (NDC 3.0) is set, constituting the updated expression of the Kingdom’s climate ambition. It takes into account all the relevant SDGs of the 2030 Agenda, in particular SDG 13, which aims to “Take urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts”. NDC 3.0 is part of an integrated and holistic approach to sustainable development, as advocated by His Majesty King Mohammed VI.

The global goals of the Paris Agreement must guide countries’ action and strengthen international cooperation. In doing so, States contribute to the implementation of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), building on its principles, notably equity, common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, while taking into account the diversity of national contexts.

Although a small contributor to global emissions, Morocco has always maintained that the commitment of all countries is an indispensable condition for achieving the ultimate objective of the UNFCCC, namely stabilising greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere in order to prevent any dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system.

Firmly committed to the Paris Agreement, which is universal and applicable to all, Morocco ratified it in September 2016, a few months before hosting and presiding over the 22nd Conference of the Parties (COP22) in Marrakech in November 2016. This COP was also marked by the very first meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (CMA.1).

Morocco is thus the first African country to have hosted and presided over two Conferences of the Parties to the UNFCCC (COP7, Marrakech, November 2001 and COP22, Marrakech, November 2016). Aware that climate change is a challenge that is both global and cross-cutting, the Kingdom calls for strong mobilisation of all actors, at all levels, taking into account the capacities of each. This requires multi-level, multi-sector and multi-actor climate action, engaging all public and private parties in all sectors of the economy and society.

Continuing its role in climate action, Morocco actively supports the “Action Agenda” put in place since the adoption of the Paris Agreement, which aims to accelerate and scale up the fight against climate change. This involvement is reflected in making the most of existing partnerships, encouraging new initiatives, and identifying good practices and climate action opportunities to achieve a sustainable transformation.

Following on from the Lima-Paris Action Agenda (LPAA) of COP20 and the Action Agenda of COP21, the Moroccan Presidency of COP22 proposed the “Marrakech Partnership”, consolidating the foundations of the Action Agenda and helping to accelerate global climate action. Initially planned for the period 2017-2020, the success of the Marrakech Partnership led the Contracting Parties, on Morocco’s proposal, to extend it to 2030 at the Baku Conference (COP29/CMA.6, Azerbaijan, November 2024).

In 2016 Morocco submitted its first NDC, which was an improved version of its Intended Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC) proposed before the adoption of the Paris Agreement. This NDC aimed to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 42 % in 2030 compared with the “business as usual (BAU)” scenario, for an overall investment of around USD 50 billion, of which 24 billion was to be provided through international support. Aware of its high vulnerability to climate change, Morocco included sectoral adaptation objectives to strengthen resilience.

In 2021, the Kingdom significantly raised its mitigation ambition with an updated NDC, setting an emission reduction target of 45.5 % in 2030, including an unconditional target of 18.3 %, for a total cost of USD 38.8 billion. The adaptation communication was also strengthened, with sectoral objectives whose implementation would require USD 40 billion.

The first Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement, concluded at the Dubai Conference (COP28/CMA5), shows that global mitigation ambitions remain insufficient to achieve the goals of the Paris Agreement. The stocktake stressed the urgency of acting to keep the goal of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C within reach and the need for global emissions to peak before 2025.

In response, Decision 1/CMA.5 encourages Parties to set economy-wide emission reduction targets covering all greenhouse gases, sectors and categories. It also encourages tripling global renewable energy capacity and doubling the global average annual rate of energy efficiency improvement by 2030. The decision further calls for phasing down unabated coal. It also aims to limit dependence on fossil fuels in energy systems, to promote low- or zero-emission technologies (paragraph 28) and to encourage sustainable lifestyles and consumption patterns (paragraph 36), among other measures.

In its new NDC, Morocco takes due account of the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake adopted at CMA5 (Decision 1/CMA.5). This NDC therefore reflects a strengthening of national climate ambition, following a trajectory compatible with the global 1.5 °C goal and carbon neutrality by 2050. It draws on the latest scientific data, in particular those of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC). NDC 3.0 includes an emission reduction target covering the whole economy, all greenhouse gases, all sectors and all categories.

The elements of Decision 1/CMA.5 are integrated, as far as possible, into the national implementation framework embodied in the national Low-Carbon roadmap to 2050 and the sectoral roadmaps, which are the pillars for operationalising Morocco’s NDC to 2035.

ii (a). National circumstances, such as geography, climate, economy, sustainable development and poverty eradication,

Geographical position Located on the southern shore of the Mediterranean, at the north-western tip of Africa, at the gateway to Europe and on the western edge of the Arab-Muslim world and the Maghreb, Morocco occupies a prime geostrategic position and has always been a crossroads of civilisations. Morocco’s particular geographical position gives it a remarkable range of bioclimates, from humid to desert. This results in the great bio-ecological diversity it enjoys, which is among the most significant in the Mediterranean basin.

Climate Morocco’s climate is highly varied: it is arid and desert in the southern provinces and south-east of the Atlas range, temperate in the northern half, with semi-arid zones appearing notably in Souss, Haouz and the Oriental. Mean annual rainfall decreases from north to south and from north-west to south-east. Morocco is among the countries most affected by climate change and its impacts.

According to data from the Directorate General of Meteorology (DGM), the period 2019–2022 was one of the driest Morocco has experienced since the 1960s, with an average rainfall deficit estimated at 32 %. In 2023, this trend worsened markedly. Some regions recorded deficits of up to 80 % in spring, and the national annual deficit reached 48 %, making 2023 the year with the greatest rainfall deficit since 1980. Over the whole period 2019–2023, the average deficit was about 35 %, marking the longest and most severe run of consecutive drought ever recorded in Morocco. This situation confirms the onset of chronic drought, reinforcing the country’s structural water stress. At the same time, temperatures have intensified. Several exceptionally hot years have been recorded since 1981, with notable temperature anomalies: +1.1 °C in 2010, +1.2 °C in 2017, +1.4 °C in 2020, +0.9 °C in 2021, +1.63 °C in 2022 and +1.77 °C in 2023.

2023 also saw an absolute record of 50.4 °C in Agadir, the highest temperature measured in Morocco since the beginning of the 20th century. In 2024 the trend continued, with the first four months showing the highest mean temperatures since 1981.

These developments confirm a rapid worsening of climate risks, combining persistent droughts and extreme heatwaves, with major impacts on water resources, agriculture, ecosystems and people’s living conditions.

NDC 3.0 projects a marked shift towards a hotter and drier climate by 2050, under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. Mean annual temperatures are expected to rise by more than 3 °C nationally, with increases of up to 5 °C in some regions. Summer is identified as the most critical season, with temperature rises of up to 2 °C, which will increase the frequency and intensity of heatwaves, fire risks, heat stress and pressure on water resources.

At the same time, annual precipitation is expected to decline across the whole territory, with decreases mainly between 5 and 15 %, reaching up to 20 % in some areas. Winter is the most affected season, with a marked reduction in rainfall, including in the regions that are traditionally wettest. This trend will deplete water resources, increase the variability of wet seasons and heighten vulnerability to droughts.

National economy1 The Moroccan economy is diverse and dynamic, with traditional sectors such as agriculture and handicrafts but also modern industries such as automotive manufacturing, aeronautics, agri-food and phosphate production, tourism and renewable energy.

Economic reforms have stimulated investment and improved infrastructure to promote sustainable development. In 2023 the economy returned to growth, rising by 3.2 %, supported by an improvement in agricultural value added. However, non-agricultural GDP fell slightly, from 3.1 % in 2022 to 2.8 % in 2023. As for 2024, the first quarter was characterised by economic growth of around 2.9 %.

The Moroccan economy is particularly sensitive to the impacts of climate change because it depends on sectors such as agriculture, fisheries and tourism. The agricultural sector, which accounts for a large share of GDP and employs a large part of the population, suffers directly from frequent droughts, declining rainfall and soil degradation. Fisheries, one of the levers of the blue economy and essential for coastal communities and exports, are affected by rising temperatures and ocean acidification, which reduce marine productivity. Tourism, vital to the economy, is threatened by the degradation of natural ecosystems, desertification and the loss of attractiveness of natural sites.

Morocco applies the methods set out in the 2006 guidelines of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) for accounting for anthropogenic GHG emissions and removals. This approach is consistent with the provisions of paragraph 31 of decision 1/CP.21.

The projections are prepared using the Low emissions analysis platform (LEAP) model, based on macroeconomic, demographic and sectoral assumptions aligned with national data and scenarios.

At the same time, a project is under way to develop a specific module on climate change adaptation, which will also make it possible to monitor adaptation actions through the MRV platform.

This initiative reflects Morocco’s determination to strengthen transparency and to ensure the clarity, consistency and traceability of the climate information reported, in accordance with the provisions of Article 13 of the Paris Agreement and the methodological guidance set out in decision 4/CMA.1.

Aware of the crucial importance of integrating climate ambitions into its budget planning, the Kingdom of Morocco has undertaken a considerable effort to align its climate ambition with the public resources that will allow it to honour its unconditional commitment on GHG mitigation and on strengthening resilience and adaptation.

For the investment-related budget component, the scope of this alignment covers all adaptation sectors, namely water, agriculture, forestry, biodiversity, health, hydrometeorological, climate and ocean monitoring and observation, housing, urban planning and spatial planning, equipment and transport, sensitive environments (coastal zones, mountains, oases), and fisheries and aquaculture, on the one hand, and the mitigation sectors, notably electricity generation, industry (excluding cement and phosphates), cement, phosphates, buildings, transport, waste, agriculture and forests, on the other.

This budgetary convergence and readability will make it possible to better identify and track climate-related financial flows in public expenditure statements. They durably place the climate transition at the heart of public financial management, strengthening the international credibility of NDC 3.0 and demonstrating to financial partners that Morocco’s commitments rest on a real capacity to mobilise domestic resources.

This alignment reflects a climate-compatible model of financial governance which, by placing budget planning at the heart of the national response to climate change, demonstrates the Kingdom of Morocco’s ability to reconcile climate ambition, budgetary credibility and transparency. It also confirms the country’s ability to finance and track its commitments in a transparent and accountable manner.

In this sense, Morocco is positioning itself as one of the first developing countries to have financial governance adapted to climate challenges, able to combine ambition, budgetary rigour and commitment under the Paris Agreement.

3.2.1. Gender and equality:

A climate transition based on equality

The Kingdom of Morocco considers that gender equality is not a secondary principle but a strategic lever for implementing the sustainable development goals and climate action. By integrating this approach into its climate policy, the country aims to reduce structural inequalities, strengthen women’s resilience to the effects of climate change and enhance their role in the climate transition of the territories. Morocco also intends to continue its national and international mobilisation to promote equitable climate action and to strengthen bilateral and multilateral cooperation in areas where climate and gender intersect.

Gender and climate vulnerability in Morocco

The Kingdom of Morocco recognises that climate change exacerbates gender inequalities. Women and girls are disproportionately affected by climate impacts, especially in rural areas, owing to several structural factors, in particular: their heavy dependence on natural resources for subsistence (water, farmland, forests), their role in unpaid care activities, which often become more burdensome after climate disasters, and their limited access to means of empowerment (land ownership, education, finance).

Women in fact play a decisive role in the resilience of their communities, notably through their involvement in strategic areas such as sustainable agriculture and fisheries, ecological forest management, integrated water resource management, the circular economy and renewable energy. Their know-how, territorial roots and experience in natural resource management make women clearly important actors in accelerating the ecological transition. Recognising this reality, Morocco continues its commitment to making gender equality a strategic lever, indispensable to any effective, equitable and sustainable climate action.

Policy and institutional frameworks for gender integration

At the legal and institutional level, Morocco has made significant progress in promoting gender equality. The 2011 Constitution was a decisive step, enshrining in its Article 19 the principle of equality between women and men, followed by the Government Plans for Equality (PGE I, II and III). The most recent, PGE 2023-2026, is structured around three strategic axes: women’s empowerment and leadership, protection and prosperity, and rights and values. Although its implementation is entrusted to the Ministry of Solidarity, Social Inclusion and the Family, the plan is supported by a governance and steering mechanism designed to ensure its effective implementation. In addition, since 2015 Morocco has institutionalised Gender-Responsive Budgeting (GRB) under the organic finance law. Following the extension of this approach to all ministerial departments in 2024, the Ministry of Economy and Finance, through its Centre of Excellence for GRB, has developed a gender budget tagging methodology that makes it possible to track precisely the resources allocated to initiatives promoting equality. This approach places Morocco at the forefront of international GRB practice. At the same time, the High Commission for Planning (HCP), the national body responsible for producing and disseminating official statistics, has been working since 2019, under the UN Women global programme “Women Count”, to make gender statistics available, accessible and analysed.

International commitments and gender-responsive climate diplomacy

At international level, Morocco is firmly committed to gender-responsive climate diplomacy. The Kingdom has ratified the Paris Agreement and aligned itself with several international initiatives such as the Lima Work Programme on Gender, as well as the Sustainable Development Goals, in particular SDG 5 on gender equality and SDG 13 on climate action.

At COP28 in Dubai, Morocco reaffirmed its commitment by signing the Declaration on the “Gender-Responsive Just Transitions and Climate Action Partnership”. These commitments are reflected concretely in its NDC 3.0, where gender equality becomes a cross-cutting principle.

Morocco is determined to follow the guidance of the Lima Work Programme on Gender and its action plan (which was extended for 10 years at COP29 in Baku at the end of 2024), as well as the new gender action plan under negotiation.

Gender: an essential component of NDC 3.0

Building on its significant progress on gender equality, Morocco undertook a structured approach to fully integrate the gender dimension into the design and implementation of NDC 3.0. To this end, an in-depth consultation was organised, involving in particular the interministerial gender network and the national focal points on this theme. The aim was to gather analyses, experiences and recommendations to ensure that climate policies take account of the differentiated needs and realities of women and men.

The recommendations from this consultation highlighted several priority areas, namely:

  • Institutional governance: Morocco recognises the importance of establishing a gender-climate coordination platform responsible for coordinating the gender-climate policies of the various actors concerned, in particular the Ministry of Solidarity, Social Inclusion and the Family (MSISF), the National Committee for Gender Equality and the Advancement of Women and the interministerial network. This platform will ensure the participation of women, particularly those from rural areas, in climate governance mechanisms at local and national levels;
  • Operational planning and implementation: Systematically integrating gender impact into all climate actions will make it possible to involve women and to value their knowledge in order to design actions adapted to their needs, notably in terms of access to resources and technology transfer. Morocco is also continuing to develop specific programmes for women’s economic empowerment in green sectors, in order to strengthen their resilience and their role in climate change mitigation and adaptation projects;
  • Inclusive finance: Morocco is working to extend gender-responsive budgeting to climate finance mechanisms in order to make these projects more attractive to international finance. This includes guaranteeing women equitable access to finance, green training programmes, and innovation and sustainable entrepreneurship tools;
  • Monitoring, evaluation and data production: integrating sex-disaggregated indicators into the national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system and using sex-disaggregated data will make it possible to assess the differentiated impacts of climate policies on women and men and to adjust strategies dynamically according to the specific needs of populations. In addition, collecting gender data in key sectors can foster better traceability of the actions undertaken, improved accountability and more effective targeting of public action, and highlight inequalities and women’s contributions to society and the economy.

3.2.2. Youth participation and inclusion

Morocco’s NDC 3.0 is marked by a strong and unprecedented determination to involve Moroccan youth in its development process, recognising their central role as fully fledged actors in climate action.

For the first time, a structured participatory process was conducted to listen to the voice of Moroccan youth and gather their views and perceptions on translating the country’s national climate ambition, in order to highlight the efforts made to combat climate change. Aware of the crucial role of young people in the ecological transition, the Mohammed VI Foundation for Environmental Protection, under the effective presidency of Her Royal Highness Princess Lalla Hasnaa, and in partnership with the Ministry of Energy Transition and Sustainable Development and the Ministry of National Education, Preschool and Sports, coordinated an ambitious participatory process that took place in three stages:

  • Outreach: an interactive webinar and presentation of the NDC 3.0 process to all young people involved in the Young Reporters for the Environment (JRE), African Green Universities & Youth Education Network (AGUYEN) and African Youth Climate Hub (AYCH) communities, including middle-school, secondary-school and university students and young climate project developers;
  • Consultation: questionnaires for middle- and secondary-school pupils and university students, each with around thirty questions, were circulated to gather their knowledge, perceptions and recommendations on climate issues and NDC 3.0, as well as on the regular involvement of young people and the consideration of their proposals;
  • Finalisation of recommendations: a national co-construction workshop enabling young people to analyse the results of the consultation and jointly prepare a structured contribution to the new NDC.

Nearly 1000 young people from Morocco’s 12 regions took part in this process, showing a real willingness to seize the opportunities offered by the energy transition and to strengthen Morocco’s position as a regional leader able to export its know-how and create green jobs. They also showed a keen awareness of the local impacts of climate change, such as drought, water scarcity and biodiversity loss. Finally, their engagement revealed a strong determination to become actively involved in solutions, expressing their priorities, formulating concrete proposals and affirming their ambition to contribute fully to the transition and to climate action.

The young people’s contributions produced structuring recommendations, including: the development of renewable energy, the systematic integration of environmental education into school and university curricula, equitable access to climate information, and the creation of participatory platforms and support for green entrepreneurship.

The young people also made concrete proposals to better raise awareness among and mobilise their peers: creating mobile apps for real-time monitoring of energy and water consumption, developing digital educational games, designing environment-focused STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics) training modules, and setting up interactive dashboards for waste management or crop yield forecasting. These innovations highlight young people’s interest in fun, practical and multi-sector tools.

Beyond this preparatory phase, the young people expressed their wish to institutionalise their participation through several youth governance mechanisms, in particular the National Youth Climate Council, regional monitoring committees, annual co-construction workshops and an incubator dedicated to youth-led projects, building on the work of the CNCCDB.

This co-construction dynamic reflects a paradigm shift and is the result of a long process of involving and mobilising young people, who are no longer seen as mere beneficiaries or targets of awareness-raising but as genuine partners in the ecological transition. Morocco is thus sending a strong signal in favour of shared climate governance based on transparency, innovation and intergenerational equity.

3.2.3. Equity and spatial justice: an integrated territorial approach

Despite its national scope, NDC 3.0 has strong territorial roots. During its preparation, Morocco’s regions were consulted in order to capture the adaptation projects undertaken at regional level.

In this context, a specific questionnaire was prepared and sent to the regions to identify ongoing and planned adaptation and mitigation projects at regional level. Following this exchange, the information collected was processed and analysed, and a meeting was then held with representatives of the Regional Directorates of the Environment (DRE). This was followed by an in-person consultation workshop with representatives of the Regional Councils (CR).

In this regard, the Kingdom’s 12 regions have engaged in climate action by preparing Territorial Climate Plans (PCT). Some regions also have Adaptation Action Plans, notably the Oriental, Drâa-Tafilalet, Souss-Massa, Beni Mellal Khénifra and Marrakech-Safi regions. These projects mainly target sensitive environments, in particular oases, through actions to combat sand encroachment and fires and to rehabilitate and plant species adapted to local conditions, as well as the coastline, through protection and sustainable management initiatives. In addition, regional adaptation projects have also been identified in the health sector, through support for healthcare provision in vulnerable rural areas, and in the biodiversity sector, through tree planting in the form of green belts aimed at strengthening local biodiversity.

It should also be mentioned that NDC 3.0 presents national projects, mainly in the water sector, that are deployed at regional level and cover several regions of the Kingdom. These include the deployment of wastewater reuse for irrigation and for watering green and agricultural areas, covering the Tanger-Tétouan-Al Hoceima, Oriental, Béni Mellal-Khénifra, Casablanca-Settat, Drâa-Tafilalet, Fès-Meknès, Marrakech-Safi, Rabat-Salé-Kénitra and Souss-Massa regions. Added to this are the construction of seawater desalination plants, involving the Tanger-Tétouan-Al Hoceima, Oriental, Rabat-Salé-Kénitra, Marrakech-Safi, Souss-Massa, Guelmim-Oued Noun and Laâyoune-Sakia El Hamra regions, and the improvement of drinking and industrial water distribution networks, covering the Oriental, Casablanca-Settat, Fès-Meknès, Rabat-Salé-Kénitra, Drâa-Tafilalet, Souss-Massa, Laâyoune-Sakia El Hamra and Guelmim-Oued Noun regions. Likewise, some national-scale projects include concrete measures implemented at regional level, such as the conservation and enhancement of natural ecosystems and protected areas, targeting in particular parks located in the Fès-Meknès and Marrakech-Safi regions.

In addition, some initiatives are led at local level, particularly in the water sector. They concern the mobilisation and sustainable management of water resources in specific areas such as the cities of Tangier, Oujda and Meknes. Other projects apply to river basins and include groundwater management, dam construction, inter-basin connections to strengthen water security, modernisation of multi-service canals, stormwater harvesting, pollution control and the promotion of “REUSE”. These projects concern most of the country’s river basins, in particular: Sebou, Oriental, Loukkos, Oum Er-Rbia, Bouregreg, Tensift, Drâa-Tafilalet, Souss-Massa, Guelmim-Oued Noun and Sakia El Hamra-Oued Eddahab. These same basins are also concerned by all the regional projects mentioned above.

This territorial breakdown is of crucial importance as it reflects Morocco’s ambition to increase resilience not only nationally but also at regional and local levels. This is fully in line with the advanced regionalisation and territorial autonomy agenda launched by Morocco. Including regional projects in NDC 3.0 gives the regions concerned the opportunity to benefit from technical and financial support for implementing the climate projects identified.

3.2.4. Employment and Human Development

The Kingdom of Morocco considers decent employment and human development to be inseparable fundamentals of the implementation of its NDC 3.0. The transformation to a green and resilient economy is a unique opportunity to create quality jobs, strengthen skills, secure livelihoods and reduce social and territorial inequalities.

Decent and inclusive jobs

NDC 3.0 places the creation and retention of green and sustainable jobs at the heart of public policy, targeting strategic sectors such as renewable energy and energy efficiency, sustainable agriculture, waste management, the circular economy and eco-construction. A national training and retraining plan would help the workforce acquire new skills, giving priority to young people, women and workers in vulnerable sectors.

Social protection and formalisation

Morocco plans to deploy its climate policies as levers for strengthening social protection and including workers in the informal economy. The deployment of NDC 3.0 projects gives priority to strengthening workers’ access to social rights and economic security, thus contributing to improving their working conditions.

Entrepreneurship and economic resilience

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), cooperatives and social and solidarity economy actors are essential drivers of green job creation and territorial resilience. Morocco strongly confirms its ambition to place the circular economy at the heart of its sustainable development model, notably by rethinking its resource management plans towards optimised, short circuits based on reduction at source, reuse and recycling. Appropriate financial mechanisms (green loans, grants, guarantees) and technical support will strengthen SMEs’ contribution to the transition and their capacity to generate sustainable added value.

Social dialogue and fundamental rights

Morocco is counting on strengthened and inclusive social dialogue and plans to pursue it through platforms bringing together the State, trade unions and employers’ organisations, in order to ensure that climate policies fully integrate the dimensions of employment, occupational health and safety, and respect for workers’ fundamental rights. By reaffirming this commitment, Morocco expresses its ambition to make the ecological transition not only a climate response but also a lever for social justice, shared prosperity and dignity for all its citizens.

4- NDC 3.0 development process

The development of Morocco’s NDC 3.0 rests on several principles designed to ensure the ambition, feasibility and quality of the country’s climate commitments. This process is deeply rooted in national development priorities, taking account of the country’s vulnerability and capacities, while meeting the requirements of the Paris Agreement.

National ownership

NDC 3.0 is led by Moroccan institutions with the effective involvement of the stakeholders concerned, including ministerial departments, local authorities, economic and financial operators, civil society, and young people and women. Strengthened interministerial coordination was an undeniable lever for ensuring consistency between public policies, encouraging sectoral synergies and facilitating the implementation of climate measures.

For this NDC 3.0, the preparation process was marked by the broadening of the scope of climate action in terms of mitigation and adaptation, respectively the rail transport and steel sub-sectors and the Biodiversity and Transport-Equipment sectors.

NDC 3.0 draws on robust, up-to-date data to set objectives that are clear, feasible, measurable and therefore credible. It aims to raise ambition, taking into account the national context and the lessons learned from implementing previous contributions.

Given its high exposure to the effects of climate change, Morocco makes adaptation the absolute priority of its NDC 3.0, aiming for the resilience of people, territories, infrastructure and ecosystems through an integrated approach sensitive to local specificities.

At the same time, a strategic balance between mitigation and adaptation is sought, consistent with the imperatives of sustainable development and the transition to a low-carbon economy. Finance occupies a central place in the process. Mobilising sufficient financial resources, national and international, public and private, is an essential condition for implementing the objectives set in the NDC. To this end, the Ministry of Economy and Finance was involved from the preparatory planning phase of NDC 3.0, as were the private and financial sectors. These are partners capable of mobilising investment and catalysing innovation to implement feasible solutions at scale.

It should also be recalled that NDC 3.0 is aligned with Morocco’s national strategies and policies and its international commitments, in particular the SDGs. It integrates the social, equity and gender dimensions across the board, affirming the determination to make climate action a lever for social justice and improved well-being. Finally, transparency and rigorous monitoring of commitments will be ensured through measurement, reporting and verification mechanisms, guaranteeing the credibility of the NDC and allowing continuous tracking of progress, in line with the requirements of the Paris Agreement.

5.5. Arrangements and priorities for the use of the carbon-market-based instruments provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement

Morocco is very interested in the potential offered by carbon market mechanisms to mobilise new finance through the cooperative approaches provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, in particular to finance its mitigation projects whose implementation is conditional on obtaining international financial support. This approach is in line with the interest of many countries, which see collaboration with Morocco as an opportunity to raise the ambition of their own mitigation commitments. To date, Morocco has signed bilateral agreements or Memoranda of Understanding with several countries, including Singapore, Norway, Switzerland and South Korea.

To harness this potential in practice, Morocco has already taken several steps to operationalise the cooperative approaches of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement on its territory, including:

  • Raising awareness and training all stakeholders to ensure the operationalisation of cooperative approaches under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement;
  • Establishing a national Article 6 framework that clearly defines the eligibility criteria, procedures, modalities and allocation of roles and responsibilities for issuing authorisation decisions (Activities, Entities and Transfer of ITMOs/Carbon Credits);
  • Strengthening the engagement of actors, in particular the private sector, with the development of a dedicated practical guide to facilitate their understanding of and involvement in the carbon market in Morocco.

It should be noted that Morocco has accelerated the process of drafting and adopting a climate law, which will provide an explicit legal basis for adopting regulatory measures to govern the use of carbon-market-based mechanisms and will specify the legal nature of units representing carbon assets, which will make it possible to recognise that they may be owned by public or private, national or foreign persons. These legislative provisions will significantly strengthen the legal certainty of transactions on both the compliance and the voluntary carbon markets.

6- Adaptation

In accordance with Article 7 of the Paris Agreement, Morocco has chosen to communicate its progress on climate change adaptation through several strategic documents. These highlight Morocco’s contribution to the cross-cutting targets of the GGA. This choice is explained by the Kingdom’s high vulnerability to the impacts of climate change, in particular rising temperatures, changing precipitation patterns and increasing aridity. These phenomena are accompanied by an increase in the frequency and scale of extreme weather events, whether sudden or slow-onset, such as severe droughts, floods, forest fires, heatwaves and cold waves, storms, landslides, locust invasions, snowstorms and sea-level rise. A significant share of the Moroccan population and several key sectors of the national economy remain particularly vulnerable to these effects. Building resilience and adaptation are therefore a national priority.

The Fifth National Communication combined with the First Biennial Transparency Report (CCN-RBT1) presents an update of climate trends at national level, an assessment of impacts, vulnerabilities and risks by sector, and a progress report on the adaptation measures implemented. NDC 3.0, focused on the Kingdom’s ambitions and projects, allows Morocco to highlight its adaptation efforts and bring them to the attention of the international community. Adaptation planning is carried by the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and the National Strategic Adaptation Plan (PNSA). The latter, like NDC 3.0, lists several incremental and transformational adaptation projects already being implemented, with measured progress. For monitoring and evaluation, the adaptation projects identified under NDC 3.0 are accompanied by indicators to track progress towards results. Morocco also already has an MRV system, within which an adaptation-specific module is currently being developed to allow progress in implementing the projects included in the NDC to be tracked.

At regional level, the Kingdom’s twelve regions have Territorial Climate Plans (PCT) or Climate-Resilient Adaptation and Development Plans (PADCR), which are a regional expression of national policy. These plans include an in-depth assessment of vulnerabilities and risks by sector, an adaptation plan with concrete projects, and a monitoring and evaluation mechanism to track the implementation of these projects. At the same level, the Regional Environment and Sustainable Development Information Systems (SIREDD) include a module dedicated to climate change, with indicators by sub-theme. A project is currently under way to add a module specific to climate change adaptation in five of the twelve SIREDDs.

6.3. Linking adaptation projects with disaster risk reduction

To deal with disaster risk, which is a major challenge for Morocco, the response is to put in place and systematically follow an integrated, multi-level approach to risk prevention and management, in a cross-cutting and participatory manner, in order to ensure the safety of people and property and the protection of ecosystems, and to promote social cohesion and civil peace. This requires governance that links disaster risk reduction (DRR) and adaptation. These two fields share common objectives, notably reducing vulnerability, anticipatory management of hazards and strengthening capacities to cope with extreme events.

In this respect, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015 - 2030), adopted at the 3rd United Nations World Conference, is a major reference to guide the action of all countries. It marks a turning point by promoting a proactive approach based on prevention, the reduction of vulnerabilities and the integration of risk management into sustainable development policies.

Morocco, which is particularly exposed to climate and hydrometeorological risks, is increasingly suffering the combined effects of climate change and anthropogenic pressures. Between 2022 and 2023, the water deficit reached up to 81 % in some basins, leading to a 67 % fall in cereal production. In 2023, an absolute temperature record was recorded in Agadir (50.4 °C), while more than 6 000 hectares of forest were ravaged by fires in the Oriental region. Added to this are the floods of 2021, 2023 and 2024, which caused major damage in several cities (Tangier, Casablanca, Taroudant and Errachidia), with loss of life, population displacement and major material damage.

In the face of this situation, Morocco has adopted a National Natural Disaster Risk Management Strategy (2020–2030), set up a National Steering Committee and operationalised several financial mechanisms. In this context, the Fund to Combat the Effects of Natural Disasters (FLCN) financed 323 projects between 2015 and 2023, for an amount of 4.73 billion dirhams, benefiting more than 33 million people. At the same time, the Solidarity Fund against Catastrophic Events (FSEC) mobilised USD 300 million after the 2023 earthquake.

Morocco is also working to integrate risk reduction into various sectoral policies and territorial planning documents. Efforts are also being made to strengthen early warning systems, raise public awareness and develop institutional and local capacities.

Annex 3 presents, by way of example, a number of NDC 3.0 adaptation projects that contribute directly to disaster risk reduction.

7. Loss and damage associated with climate change

7.1. Status of loss and damage

Loss and damage associated with climate change refers to the negative – often irreversible or residual – effects of climate impacts caused both by sudden extreme events (cyclones) and by slow-onset phenomena (sea-level rise) for which adaptation is not possible.

Although no official definition has been adopted under the UNFCCC, loss and damage is generally understood as damage exceeding the mitigation and adaptation capacities of human societies and ecosystems. The notion covers both economic and non-economic losses. The first type refers to financially quantifiable losses, such as the destruction of housing, infrastructure, crops or livelihoods, while the second concerns losses that cannot be assessed in monetary terms, such as the loss of human lives, cultural identity, territories or social ties.

In Morocco, the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme climate events are causing major human, economic and environmental loss and damage. Historical data show that since 1984 about 90 % of the disasters recorded in the country, as well as 72.5 % of loss of life and 75 % of economic losses, have been linked to hydrometeorological and climatic phenomena such as droughts, floods and storms (IRES, 2011). However, in the absence of a national database allowing reliable loss and damage data to be tracked and reported, use was made of open-access international databases and of complementary studies, which are not exhaustive. More specifically, EM-DAT 6 made it possible to record the major and most impactful events that occurred in Morocco between 1960 and 2025, while UNDR-DesInventar (Sendai) listed those recorded over the period 1990 to 2014.

7.2. Loss and damage by sector

The impacts of climate change in Morocco affect all economic and social sectors, generating losses that often exceed the adaptive capacity available.

The water sector is on the front line, with prolonged droughts causing a critical fall in dam reservoir levels and reduced groundwater recharge. Siltation also causes an annual loss estimated at several tens of millions of cubic metres. Added to this are recurrent floods, identified among the main climate risks in the CESE report (2023). They caused, for example, more than 100 deaths in Ourika in 1995 and 47 deaths in Guelmim in 2014, and led to the displacement of nearly 22 000 people between 2008 and 2024, according to the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC).

These events also caused the suspension of many economic activities in cities such as Tangier, Casablanca and Agadir. This situation justifies the implementation of a national flood management programme, with planned investment of 5.7 billion dirhams by 2050 to strengthen resilience to this risk.

The agriculture sector is particularly exposed. The 2022 drought, the most severe in four decades, caused a sharp fall in agricultural GDP, which accounts for 14 % of national GDP. It also caused losses of up to 500 000 tonnes of fruit per year in several key areas such as the Gharb, Haouz and Saïss.

The reduction in cultivated areas, over-exploitation of groundwater and rising energy costs for pumping aggravate the precariousness of family farms, thereby fuelling rural exodus. This increased pressure on livelihoods falls disproportionately on women, who make up 40 % of the agricultural workforce but own only 5 % of farms. Those affected by drought-related crop failures travel long distances every day (more than 190 km) to reach job opportunities. This situation is pushing many rural women to migrate.

The fisheries and aquaculture sector is severely affected by the disappearance of natural stocks such as clams and mussels, the degradation of marine habitats and mass mortalities linked to marine heatwaves. These phenomena cause significant economic losses for aquaculture units, especially the most financially vulnerable. In the forestry sector, 17 711 fires destroyed about 178 773 hectares between 1960 and 2019. This trend is continuing, with more than 10 000 additional hectares destroyed by fire between 2021 and 2023.

In addition, nearly 50 % of watersheds are at high risk of erosion, which causes significant loss of fertile soil and contributes to reservoir siltation, compromising the ecological and productive functions of forests. Sensitive environments (coastline, oases and mountain areas) are under growing pressure. The coastline is affected by erosion whose cost is estimated at USD 434 million. In the oases, between 4 000 and 4 500 palm trees were lost in 2021, while mountain areas are experiencing increasingly scarce water resources, threatening food security and the stability of infrastructure.

As regards biodiversity, climate pressures are causing wetlands to shrink, protected areas to degrade, soil erosion, the disappearance of native plant species and the weakening of ecological infrastructure. These effects reduce the capacity of ecosystems to perform their regulatory role.

The consequences for the health sector are serious. Some 1 690 deaths linked to extreme climate events have been recorded, including 21 deaths in 24 hours during a heatwave in Béni Mellal in 2024. Added to this are the degradation of health services, the increase in climate-related diseases and the overloading of health facilities. The housing sector suffered the destruction of 4 043 buildings between 1990 and 2014 as a result of floods and fires, as well as losses estimated at USD 8 million due to coastal erosion.

Finally, in the equipment and transport sector, essential infrastructure is suffering growing material and functional losses. These losses disrupt mobility, access to basic services and supply chains, particularly in isolated areas.

These vulnerabilities are all the more worrying because they are aggravated by the current limitations of the climate, meteorological and ocean monitoring system, which is still not extensive or effective enough to anticipate extreme events efficiently. This increases human and economic losses throughout the territory.

  • Projects in the forest sector show a very low cost (38 USD/t CO2eq). This sector combines low investment intensity with high sequestration potential, making it a prime candidate for “quick wins” and environmental co-benefits.
  • Finally, cement and industry excluding cement and phosphates show intermediate costs (82 to 91 USD/t CO2eq), reflecting the challenge of progressively decarbonising sectors that are high emitters but essential to economic competitiveness.

Comparing the two graphs makes it possible to identify differentiated profiles:

  • Sectors with high investment needs but a competitive abatement cost (electricity), which are levers for climate competitiveness.
  • Sectors with a high abatement cost and massive financing (transport), which require strengthened planning and international support.
  • Sectors with a low abatement cost but modest financing (forest), which offer quick opportunities and multiple co-benefits and constitute a strategic reserve of “catalytic” projects.

Finally, these graphs in fact map out structural trade-offs. They also reveal the tensions between massive investment and carbon efficiency, heavy transitions and quick wins, and between dependence on international finance and domestic levers of resilience.

Annex 4: List of adaptation projects with a potential impact on human mobility (migration)

Climate change is an increasingly decisive factor in migration dynamics, making some areas less liveable owing to the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme events such as droughts, floods and storms. These disruptions compromise food security, access to water and livelihoods, prompting people to move. Morocco is affected both by internal displacement linked to the effects of climate change and by growing migratory pressure as a country of transit or settlement. These movements have major economic, social and health consequences, particularly in urban areas, which face increased pressure on infrastructure, basic services and the labour market. In this context, some projects identified under NDC 3.0 could help limit the drivers of human mobility by strengthening the resilience of territories, improving living conditions, creating local economic opportunities and reducing vulnerability to climate hazards.
