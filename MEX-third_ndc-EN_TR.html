Foreword

The climate emergency defines our times, and the response we choose to confront it will mark our generation and those to come. The First Global Stocktake of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change underscored with undeniable clarity that emission pathways remain misaligned with the goal of limiting the increase in the global average temperature to 1.5 °C by the end of this century. It urged the immediate closing of the existing gap between science and action. Mexico has not been indifferent to this call. We reaffirm our unrestricted commitment to the Paris Agreement, ten years after its creation, and to a just transition that leads us to achieve net zero emissions by 2050, while protecting our communities and caring for our ecosystems.

Mexico is living a key moment in its history. We are building the Second Storey of the Fourth Transformation and consolidating a development model based on Mexican Humanism, which recognizes that economic growth must go hand in hand with the protection of the environment and the well-being of people. The process for updating our Nationally Determined Contribution is a faithful reflection of this profound transformation the nation is undergoing, and a reminder of the struggle we have sustained for decades and from different fronts to guarantee the right to a healthy environment. This NDC, the third, represents an unprecedented opportunity to move from an extractivist development model that generates inequalities to an environmentalist and humanist one that respects planetary boundaries and ensures shared prosperity for present and future generations of Mexican women and men.

Faced with a climate crisis that admits no further delay, much less its denial, Mexico's NDC 3.0 is more than a public policy document: it is a call to collective action, to effective collaboration between sectors and to shared responsibility to guarantee a fairer, more inclusive and, above all, more sustainable future. Its ambition and its anchoring in national planning instruments make it a true roadmap for achieving collective well-being and the protection of the nature that sustains us.

This far-reaching instrument articulates its five components -mitigation, adaptation, loss and damage, cross-cutting climate policy, and enabling environment and means of implementation- under a logic of interdependence, in which the eradication of poverty and inequalities, the conservation of biodiversity, economic development and territorial strength in the face of disturbances form part of one and the same effective transformation strategy. Part of the ambition of this NDC lies in the way it was built; it was prepared through an unprecedented, broad and deliberative participatory process, together with more than fifty federal agencies, state and municipal governments, Indigenous Peoples and Afro-descendant communities, civil society organizations, academia, the private sector, women and young people. This instrument not only reflects the country's diversity, but also its determination to act jointly and with commitment. Mexico, being the fifth most megadiverse country in the world, assumes a leading responsibility in global climate action. We have committed to achieving carbon neutrality by mid-century and to publishing, as soon as possible, our first National Adaptation Policy. These goals go beyond the technical imperative: they are the expression of the political will and ethical awareness involved in reconfiguring economic, energy, industrial, agricultural and urban systems; mobilizing public and private resources and scaling up technological innovation; and, at all times, ensuring that no one is left behind. The success of this policy also depends on our capacity to protect and restore natural carbon sinks such as our forests, rainforests, wetlands and marine ecosystems. And, the fundamental barriers against climate change, such as mangroves.

This Nationally Determined Contribution 3.0 takes a decisive step towards aligning our climate policies with a long-term vision of State, grounded in climate justice and substantive equality. In parallel, we will seek to strengthen the legal, institutional and governance instruments that will allow us to materialize these transformations in the national territory, and to articulate them with the National Development Plan 2025-2030, the sectoral and territorial plans, and the multilateral commitments our country has assumed. Its effective implementation will depend on the active, vigilant and critical participation of all the people, organizations and institutions that make up our homeland.

With the NDC 3.0, Mexico reaffirms its vocation as a committed actor and its place as a proactive leader in international climate governance, capable of influencing the course of the planet with action, ambition and coherence. I call for this document not only to be consulted, but to be taken up as its own by all of Mexican society. In a world that demands boldness, solidarity and a vision of the future, Mexico is ready to lead by example.

Presentation

Mexico renews its commitment before the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (CMNUCC) by presenting the update of its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0, in accordance with the General Law on Climate Change (LGCC) and Article 4 of the Paris Agreement.

Mexico's NDC 3.0 has an implementation horizon up to 2035, and reflects the ambition of a country in the midst of a process of transformation towards a more inclusive, just, resilient and sustainable development model. With this NDC, Mexico recognizes its responsibility -common but differentiated- to contribute to the global fight against the climate crisis, while responding to the various national circumstances that make the country one of the most vulnerable to its negative impacts, and to the challenges involved in mainstreaming the gender, human rights and environmental justice approach in climate action. In this way, the country is committed to aligning development policies that accelerate the climate response while advancing social objectives.

The mitigation component has been strengthened, presenting a greater level of ambition in two respects. First, the 2035 target is presented for the first time in absolute terms, in order to improve transparency and accountability. This target continues to cover all greenhouse gases (GEI) and all sectors of the economy: (1) transport, (2) electricity generation, (3) industry, (4) agriculture and livestock, (5) waste, (6) oil and gas, (7) land use, land-use change and forestry, and (8) residential and commercial. Second, the 2035 target was determined on the basis of the long-term objective to which Mexico has committed of achieving net zero emissions by mid-century. For this reason, Mexico presents its targets in net terms for the first time, committing to achieve net emissions of between 364 and 404 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (MtCO2e) in 2035 unconditionally. Mexico could reach a net emissions level of between 332 and 363 MtCO2e conditional on the mobilization of financing, technology transfer and capacity building through international cooperation.

The adaptation component also presents a greater level of ambition by strengthening the measures and lines of action of the five pillars presented in the NDC 2.0: (a) prevention and management of negative impacts on the human population and the territory; (b) resilient productive systems and food security; (c) conservation, restoration and sustainable use of biodiversity and ecosystem services; (d) integrated water resources management with a climate change approach; and (e) protection of strategic infrastructure and tangible cultural heritage. It also incorporates a new pillar, pillar (f) addressing the links between security and climate change, which seeks to strengthen the capacity to respond to socio-environmental conflicts and other threats to human security linked to and exacerbated by the impacts of climate change. This component will be the basis for the development of Mexico's first National Adaptation Policy (NAP), which is expected to be published in 2026.

In the NDC 3.0, Mexico strengthens the comprehensiveness of its climate policy by incorporating three new components. The first of these is the loss and damage component, which recognizes the importance of preventing, minimizing and addressing the negative effects of climate change that persist despite mitigation and adaptation efforts. This new component is made up of five pillars: (a) response to climate change-related emergencies and disasters and humanitarian action; (b) risk transfer and retention; (c) reconstruction with a risk prevention and resilience approach; (d) human mobility linked to the impacts of climate change; and (e) economic and non-economic losses (tangible and intangible).

Likewise, the NDC 3.0 broadens its scope by incorporating a cross-cutting climate policy component, which seeks to operationalize the gender perspective, the human rights approach, just transition and intergenerational equity in the implementation of climate policy, as well as to guarantee the effective consideration and inclusion of priority populations.

Lastly, the enabling environment and means of implementation component seeks to trigger concrete actions to mobilize financing, facilitate technology transfer and build capacities at all levels, as well as to identify necessary interventions -including actions on regulatory reform- to guarantee the enabling conditions that allow the effective implementation of the NDC.

Mexico's NDC 3.0 is grounded in the priorities of the current administration, set out in the National Development Plan 2025-2030, which establishes four general pillars (Governance with justice and citizen participation; Development with well-being and humanism; Moral economy and work; and Sustainable development) and three cross-cutting pillars (Substantive equality and women's rights; Public innovation for national technological development; and Rights of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities), as well as in the One Hundred Commitments for the Second Storey of the Fourth Transformation of the Presidency of the Republic. In particular, Plan Mexico, Strategy for Equitable and Sustainable Economic Development for Shared Prosperity, sets out the guidelines so that the transition towards a low-emission, climate-resilient economy is taken up as an opportunity and a driver of development, promoting investment in strategic sectors to increase the national content of production, create decent jobs, foster technological innovation and provide fundamental co-benefits for poverty reduction.

Along these lines, the NDC 3.0 reaffirms the commitment of the Government of Mexico to the 2030 Agenda and its 17 Sustainable Development Goals, recognizing that climate action is intrinsically linked to development and, if deliberately designed for that purpose, can have a multiplier effect on health and social well-being, the correction of structural inequalities, the protection of biocultural heritage and the increase of economic competitiveness. Thus, Mexico is committed to sustainable development in harmony with climate action, in which adaptation is redefined not as a cost but as a catalyst for the country's productivity, growth and inclusion.

This NDC was built through a broad participatory process -in compliance with Mexico's obligations under the Escazú Agreement- in which all voices were heard. Its implementation must be carried out under the same principles, guaranteeing the meaningful participation of all people: women in all their diversity, Indigenous Peoples and Afro-Mexican communities, children and youth, older persons, migrants, persons with disabilities, the LGBTIQ+ population, and all population groups in situations of vulnerability, making effective the mandate of "leaving no one behind". For this reason, the NDC 3.0 is directly linked to other key instruments for launching inclusive and transformative climate action, in particular the Strategic Plan on Gender, Human Rights and Climate Change.

With the third edition of its NDC, Mexico not only reaffirms its commitment to the Paris Agreement, but also assumes its leadership before the international community, emphasizing the importance of global solidarity and cooperation to address the greatest challenge facing humanity as a whole. The challenge is enormous, but so is the opportunity to move towards a more egalitarian, just and sustainable future, in which each and every one of us is jointly responsible.

Alicia Bárcena Ibarra Minister of the Environment and Natural Resources

National Context

Mexico ranks eleventh worldwide in terms of population and thirteenth in terms of the size of its economy, and is also recognized as the fifth most megadiverse country in the world (after Brazil, Colombia, China and Indonesia).

Because of its location between two oceans, its biophysical characteristics and its socioeconomic conditions, the national territory presents a high degree of vulnerability to the effects of climate change. According to the University of Notre Dame Global Adaptation Index (ND-GAIN), Mexico currently ranks 100 out of 187, and its vulnerability has been increasing in recent years (in 2020 it ranked 95). Under any global warming scenario, including 1.5 ºC, the projected impacts are extremely worrying for Mexico, which is already suffering the increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme hydrometeorological phenomena, changes in precipitation patterns, the rise in mean temperature and in sea level, as well as the effects on the most vulnerable communities, biodiversity and water availability. These impacts represent significant risks for the country's food security, strategic infrastructure, public health and productive systems.

Mexico is warming faster than the global average, with a temperature increase rate of 3.2ºC per century compared with the global temperature increase rate of 2ºC per century, which is an unavoidable wake-up call to act now.

The challenges for adaptation are accentuated in a country where around 38.5 million people are living in multidimensional poverty -that is, they are not guaranteed the exercise of at least one of their social rights, such as education, health, social security, housing and food- and 7 million live in extreme poverty. In addition, rural and indigenous communities, as well as women and young people, face conditions of greater vulnerability, which is why it is essential to approach national policy from a climate justice perspective.

The economic context is characterized by the need to reconcile growth with emissions reduction, through the energy transition, the diversification of the productive matrix, efficiency in the use of resources -with a strong push for the circular economy-, and an unprecedented commitment to technological innovation with high national content. Climate policy must act as a driver of development and be linked in a cross-cutting manner with commitments to social well-being, substantive equality and the preservation of biocultural heritage.

Although Mexico has a solid legal framework, this NDC 3.0 is being developed at a key juncture, in which various regulatory and public policy instruments are being updated to strengthen long-term planning, the harmonization of instruments under a multilevel governance approach, and consistency with international commitments. This context represents a unique opportunity to align the vision and priorities of national climate change policy, the financing needed for its implementation, and the monitoring, reporting and verification systems, as well as the monitoring, evaluation and learning systems, that account for its effectiveness and results.

Regulatory, policy and institutional framework

Right to a healthy environment and human rights related to climate change

Human rights are at the centre of the Mexican legal system. The Political Constitution of the United Mexican States (CPEUM) recognizes the normative supremacy of all international human rights treaties formally adopted by the Mexican State, raising the importance of the right to a healthy environment, as well as all those rights threatened by climate change, in accordance with the principles of universality, interdependence, indivisibility and progressivity.

Our country is a Party to the main international human rights treaties, including: the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966); the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966); the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW, 1981); the Convention on the Rights of the Child; the International Convention on the Protection of the Rights of All Migrant Workers and Members of Their Families; and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

It is also a Party to regional human rights treaties, among which the American Convention on Human Rights and the Regional Agreement on Access to Information, Public Participation and Access to Justice in Environmental Matters in Latin America and the Caribbean (Escazú Agreement) stand out. Mexico has ratified 78 Conventions of the International Labour Organization (ILO) that guarantee the individual and collective rights of workers, including Convention 169 on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples, as well as the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. In addition, Mexico is subject to the jurisdiction of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights.

Article 4 of the CPEUM expressly recognizes that every person has the right to a healthy environment for their development and well-being, and that the State shall guarantee respect for this right, together with other rights linked to climate change, such as the right to health, to water, to food, to housing and to sustainable mobility. To this are added article 25, which establishes the mandate for comprehensive and sustainable national development; article 27, which regulates the ownership and management of natural resources under conservation criteria; and article 73, which empowers the Congress of the Union to issue laws on environmental matters. Together, these provisions form a constitutional framework that consolidates fundamental environmental rights and the mandate to advance towards sustainable development.

Climate change regulatory framework in Mexico

Mexico is a Party to the binding international treaties on climate change: the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (CMNUCC, 1992), which establishes the bases for global climate action under the principles of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities, and the Paris Agreement (2015), which provides for the progressive updating of the Nationally Determined Contribution.

Within the national regulatory framework, the General Law on Climate Change (LGCC), published in June 2012, constitutes the fundamental legal framework of Mexico's climate policy and represents a historic milestone, being the first national climate change law of a developing country and the second in the world. This law establishes the regulation of emissions of greenhouse gases and compounds, as well as provisions on adaptation to safeguard the population, ecosystems and productive sectors. The LGCC defines the governance framework and the planning instruments for the development of national climate change policy and, since its 2018 reform, recognizes the NDC as one of its guiding instruments, thus establishing a direct bridge between Mexico's international commitments under the Paris Agreement and its domestic regulatory framework.

Policy instruments

National climate change policy in Mexico is articulated with national planning and with the objectives and instruments defined in the LGCC. The National Development Plan (PND) 2025-2030 constitutes the guiding framework for public policies. Based on the PND, the Sectoral Programme for the Environment and Natural Resources 2025-2030 translates the general objectives into specific lines of action. This programme establishes measures to conserve ecosystems, strengthen water management, promote the circular economy and advance the energy transition. In this way, it constitutes the operational bridge between the PND and national climate change policy, by reflecting the mitigation and adaptation targets in the budgets, mechanisms and indicators of sectoral implementation.

The LGCC establishes the following planning instruments for national climate change policy:

  • The National Climate Change Strategy (ENCC) is the trans-administration instrument that defines the 10-, 20- and 40-year vision, ensuring the continuity of policies beyond terms of government, in order to address the effects of climate change and transition towards a competitive, sustainable and low-carbon economy. The ENCC was updated in 2024, with a vision oriented towards carbon neutrality and climate resilience by mid-century, articulating mitigation and adaptation actions with a cross-cutting climate policy component. The ENCC must be reviewed at least every ten years on mitigation and every six years on adaptation.
  • The Special Climate Change Programme (PECC) constitutes the six-year roadmap of the Federal Public Administration on climate change. It details objectives, strategies, lines of action and the monitoring framework for following up on the responsibilities and implementation deadlines of each agency, and it strengthens the coordination of actions, the dissemination of results and the estimation of costs. The PECC 2026-2030 is under development and will be published during the first half of 2026.
  • The National Adaptation Policy, aimed at consolidating a comprehensive framework for climate risk management and social, economic and environmental resilience, began to be prepared in 2025. In accordance with the LGCC, it must integrate actions to reduce vulnerabilities to extreme climate phenomena,

prioritizing populations at risk and strategic territories in all development sectors. This policy must be based on instruments for diagnosis, planning, measurement, monitoring, reporting, verification and evaluation.

Institutional framework

The Organic Law of the Federal Public Administration (LOAPF) provides that SEMARNAT is responsible for conducting the policy of the environmental sector, a power it exercises concurrently with other agencies and orders of government, under a scheme of inter-institutional shared responsibility. For its part, the LGCC establishes that it is up to the Federation to formulate and conduct national policy on the matter; to develop, coordinate and apply climate policy instruments; to design, together with society, climate change planning instruments; and to put in place their monitoring and evaluation.

The LGCC establishes the National Climate Change System (SINACC) as the main multi-actor and multilevel coordination mechanism on climate change. It is made up of the Inter-Ministerial Commission on Climate Change (CICC), which coordinates the action of the various State Ministries of the APF; the National Institute of Ecology and Climate Change (INECC), which provides technical and scientific support; the Congress of the Union, which provides regulatory and budgetary backing; the state governments and municipal authorities, responsible for implementing their powers in accordance with local realities; and the Climate Change Council (C3), an advisory body that contributes the perspective of academia, civil society and the private sector.

The CICC promotes the coordination of the actions of the agencies and entities of the APF on climate change. It currently includes the heads of the Ministries of the Interior; Foreign Affairs; the Navy; Finance and Public Credit; Welfare; the Environment and Natural Resources; Energy; Economy; Agriculture and Rural Development; Infrastructure, Communications and Transport; Public Education; Health; Agrarian, Territorial and Urban Development; Tourism; and Security and Citizen Protection. At its first ordinary session of 2025, the CICC decided to incorporate the Ministry of Women, the Ministry of National Defense, and the Ministry of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation.

The CICC carries out its functions through five working groups: the Working Group on Adaptation Policies (GT-ADAPT), which, by decision of the CICC, now also includes a subgroup on Loss and Damage; the Working Group on Mitigation (GT-MITIG); the Working Group on Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation (GT-REDD); the Working Group on International Negotiations (GT-INT); and the Working Group on Climate Finance (GT-FIN).

In accordance with the LGCC, state governments are responsible for formulating, conducting, evaluating and updating their climate change policies. Their powers include promoting scientific research, environmental education and the preparation of risk atlases, as well as integrating mitigation and adaptation measures into territorial and sectoral planning. At present, 29 federative entities have state climate change laws, and most have specific programs and active state councils. For their part, within the framework of article 115 of the Constitution, municipalities formulate and implement local policies and actions in line with the PND, the ENCC, the PECC and the state programs.

Throughout the process of developing NDC 3.0, constant and meaningful involvement of the agencies and entities of the APF was promoted, together with the active participation of state and municipal governments, representatives of the private sector and of financial organizations, academia, civil society and international organizations, as well as of differentiated populations and the general public. Cross-Cutting Issues Component

Climate change not only alters weather patterns and threatens ecosystems, it also deepens the social crisis arising from an exhausted development model, which puts at risk the living conditions of present and future generations. Faced with this challenge, NDC 3.0 aims to lay the foundations for driving a just transition towards an alternative development model. It is also a reflection of what Mexico has called a humanist environmental and ecological policy, with the determination to become a global reference point for climate action centred on social justice.

This cross-cutting climate policy component seeks to establish concrete measures and lines of action so that NDC 3.0 in its entirety is implemented with an intersectional gender perspective and a human rights approach, while at the same time promoting the just transition of the workforce and intergenerational equity. The component also contains specific lines of action for attention to priority populations, in order to respond to the realities and needs of those who directly and disproportionately experience the impacts of the climate crisis.

This vision places at its centre the equitable distribution of the benefits and the fair sharing of the costs of climate action, as well as the prevention of possible adverse impacts on the most vulnerable populations, which tend also to be those that contribute least to climate change. The commitment to leave no one behind is indispensable, not only to achieve more effective climate action, but also to build fairer, more peaceful and more sustainable societies, where all people can live with dignity. In this context, the cross-cutting climate policy recognizes that Mexico is a pluricultural and multi-ethnic country, where different cultures, languages and worldviews coexist, making it one of the countries with the greatest cultural diversity and wealth in the world.

With this vision, Mexico adopted the Strategic Plan on Gender, Human Rights and Climate Change (PEGDHCC) as a public policy instrument that guides and gives substance to the cross-cutting climate policy. In accordance with Mexico's obligations under the international human rights and climate change treaties, the PEGDHCC defines a course of action that the country undertakes to implement in order to guarantee a climate policy with a gender and human rights approach in all the substantive components of climate action: mitigation, adaptation, loss and damage, and means of implementation.

The cross-cutting climate policy is developed on the basis of the following axes:

  • Axis A. Intersectional gender perspective. Owing to social norms and gender stereotypes, people of different genders are not only disproportionately affected by climate change, they also face inequalities and limitations in access to opportunities, resources and information that would allow them to participate actively in decision-making. This axis establishes actions to promote a feminist and intersectional climate policy, one that takes into account the differentiated causes and impacts of climate change, as well as the role of people of different genders as key agents of change in articulating

effective responses. With that outlook, this axis promotes measures so that NDC 3.0 contributes decisively to identifying, questioning and combating the stereotypes and social norms that sustain gender inequalities, and drives the leadership of women in all their diversity in the construction of national climate policy.

  • Axis B. Human rights approach. The impacts of climate change threaten the full exercise of human rights and deepen structural inequalities. Likewise, when climate action policy does not incorporate a human rights approach in its various phases —design, implementation and monitoring— it may generate unintended adverse effects, reproduce conditions of social injustice and leave people and communities in a situation of vulnerability with respect to the guarantee of their rights. This axis establishes actions to ensure that all the components of national climate action are aligned with Mexico's international and national obligations on human rights.
  • Axis C. Intergenerational equity. The decisions of present generations have a direct bearing on the quality of life and the exercise of the rights of future generations. It is therefore essential to recognize the intergenerational dimension of the actions adopted today for those who will inhabit the planet in the future. This axis establishes actions so that national climate policy guarantees intergenerational equity, grounded in respect for the rights of future generations, in recognition of the link between humanity and nature, and in the application of the best available scientific knowledge. With that perspective, the aim is to ensure that the principles of sustainability and justice are integrated into short-, medium- and long-term decision-making processes, thereby contributing to responsible climate action based on solidarity between generations.
  • Axis D. Just transition towards a sustainable economy. The transition towards a resilient, low-emission economy requires structural transformations covering all productive activities and all sectors of society. It is therefore essential to ensure that this transformation does not reproduce or deepen existing inequalities, but rather generates opportunities to promote shared prosperity, the creation of new green jobs, technological innovation and conditions of social justice. This axis promotes actions aimed at advancing a just transition —particularly of the workforce— in order to reduce possible adverse impacts and maximize the social, economic and environmental benefits of climate action.
  • Axis E. Attention to and protection of priority populations. On the basis of a diagnosis of structural gaps (Annex I), this axis recognizes the priority populations that are in the greatest situation of vulnerability to the impacts of climate change in Mexico. From this perspective, measures are established that recognize their differentiated needs and their rights, and

that guarantee their representation and effective participation in decision-making processes related to climate action.

These five axes are interdependent and complementary, and have a twofold purpose in NDC 3.0:

I. To incorporate guiding principles that steer the design, implementation and monitoring of climate action, and II. To adopt actions that integrate those principles into each of the components of national climate policy.

Axis. A. Intersectional gender perspective

In Mexico, gender inequalities are deeply rooted in social, political and economic structures. The conditions of access to opportunities, security, resources and decision-making power are, to a large extent, determined by gender: the set of social norms that creates a binary man-woman system and attributes specific characteristics, expectations and roles to men and women in a given society and at a given time. This system has historically placed men in a position of superiority, generating structural conditions of inequality for people of different genders. For this reason, in the face of the impacts of climate change, women and girls tend to be disproportionately affected.

According to data from the National Council for the Evaluation of Social Development Policy (CONEVAL), in rural areas, where the impacts of climate change are most severe, women account for 52.9 % of people living in extreme poverty, which reduces their possibilities of coping with the impacts of climate change. Likewise, women living in territories of high climate exposure —among them indigenous, Afro-Mexican women and women from rural, peripheral and coastal areas of the country— face the intersection of multiple forms of discrimination, including structural racism, gender inequality and economic exclusion. In the communities most affected by the climate crisis, women and girls play a central role in the management of natural resources and take on most of the domestic and care work. According to the National Survey on Time Use (ENUT), in 2019 women devoted 30.8 hours per week on average to domestic work, almost three times more than men. During climate emergencies, these domestic and care responsibilities increase, which limits their possibilities of fully exercising their rights to education, work and health, as well as of participating in decision-making processes.

In addition, there is a proven link between the impacts of climate change and the increase in gender-based violence. Women and girls in all their diversity, as well as the LGBTQ+ population, face a greater risk of being victims of gender-based violence in disaster contexts. On many occasions, the lack of adequate shelters guaranteeing conditions of safety for women, girls and the LGBTQ+ population increases their exposure to situations that put at risk their life, integrity and physical and mental health. Similarly, discrimination on grounds of gender prevents women and the LGBTQ+ population from accessing health services and fully exercising their sexual and reproductive rights.

Moreover, women environmental defenders tend to be doubly stigmatized and criminalized, so it is essential to create adequate conditions of safety and well-being that allow them to carry out their activities —crucial for the preservation of ecosystems— as well as to advance in the recognition and protection of their rights.

This axis seeks to transform relations between people of different genders through an intersectional approach, in order to advance towards substantive equality as a central element of national climate policy.

A.1.1. Ensure that the Strategic Plan on Gender, Human Rights and Climate Change guides all climate planning instruments at the national level and is renewed periodically in order to advance substantive gender equality, in line with Mexico's commitments under the Enhanced Lima Work Programme on Gender and its Action Plan. A.1.2. Develop indicators that make it possible to monitor, report on and evaluate the impacts of climate action on progress in gender equality. A.1.3. Identify and address all forms of discrimination against women that may persist in climate change plans, laws and programs, and ensure that the rights of women and girls are a primary consideration in the development of climate action.

A.2. Promote the leadership and full participation of women in all their diversity in decision-making on access to and management of the natural assets and ecosystem services needed for effective climate action.

A.2.1. Implement affirmative actions and combat gender stereotypes in order to promote the leadership and participation of women in management positions within the coordination and governance mechanisms for climate action at the national and local levels. A.2.2. Develop mechanisms to promote the active participation, empowerment and leadership of women in the design, implementation and monitoring of climate action. A.2.3. Recognize and strengthen the traditional knowledge of women, and promote their leadership in the management of natural assets and ecosystem services. A.2.4. Strengthen the guidelines and create mechanisms to implement the right to free, prior and informed consultation with a gender perspective. A.2.5. A.2.6. A.2.7. Promote the participation of women in the transition towards a sustainable economy by promoting green employment opportunities specifically designed to favour their training and empowerment.

Develop and implement a comprehensive protection program for women environmental defenders with a gender, intersectional and intercultural perspective, in order to eliminate the criminalization of territorial defence and develop civilian community protection systems.

A.6. Ensure that strategies and plans for responding to extreme climate events and reducing risks incorporate an intersectional gender perspective.

Axis b. Human rights approach

The Paris Agreement recognizes that climate change is a problem for all of humanity and that, in adopting measures to address it, Parties should respect, promote and consider their respective obligations relating to human rights. Mexico is a Party to the main international and regional human rights treaties, which establish obligations to respect, protect and guarantee the full exercise of all human rights. In addition, the country has a robust constitutional framework that recognizes the fundamental rights of all people, including the human right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.

This axis promotes actions to ensure that climate action is aligned with Mexico's international and national obligations on the matter. Reference is made to Advisory Opinion No. 32 (OC-32) of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (CIDH) on the climate emergency and human rights, as well as the Advisory Opinion of the International Court of Justice (CIJ) on the obligations of States in relation to climate change. In this way, Mexico reaffirms its commitment to advancing towards a climate policy centred on justice and social well-being.

B.1. Integrate the human rights perspective into the formulation, implementation and monitoring of national climate change policy, ensuring that it does not create new vulnerabilities or exacerbate existing ones.

B.1.1. Establish measures consistent with the due diligence standard in order to address the specific risks that climate change poses to the full exercise of human rights. B.1.2. Promote specific analysis of the various economic, social, cultural and environmental rights in their relationship with the socioeconomic risks arising from the climate emergency, and develop recommendations to incorporate their consideration into the relevant programs, plans and standards. B.1.3. Establish effective regulation to prevent extractive and productive activities from causing or contributing to causing human rights violations and, where applicable, adopt measures aimed at remedying such violations. B.1.4. Ensure that national climate policy strengthens the right of all people to participate in scientific progress and to enjoy its benefits without discrimination.

B.2 Promote environmental democracy, guaranteeing the rights to information, participation and access to environmental justice, as the basis for ensuring the legitimacy and effectiveness of climate action.

B.2.1. Strengthen compliance with rulings on climate change and human rights by improving the mechanisms for enforcing, monitoring and following up on the decisions issued by the Inter-American Human Rights System, the National Human Rights Commission, the state commissions and the Judiciary, as well as the recommendations of the special rapporteurs, ensuring coordination among the different orders of government. B.2.2. Adopt measures to guarantee the right to information –and to combat the disinformation that contributes to generating erroneous perceptions about scientific consensus-, through the adoption of accessible climate change communication strategies based on the best available science. B.2.3. Ensure the right to participation of all sectors of society in the design, implementation and evaluation of national climate policy – including a comprehensive review of multilevel climate governance mechanisms-, and establish mechanisms to empower historically marginalized populations, ensuring that the results, agreements and decisions of participatory processes inform the decisions of the authorities and facilitate accountability. B.2.4. Improve access to administrative, judicial and quasi-judicial dispute resolution mechanisms for addressing possible human rights violations related to the impacts of climate change.

Axis c. Intergenerational equity

The decisions taken by present generations directly affect the lives and rights of future generations. Intergenerational justice means ensuring that present-day actions, policies and conduct do not generate adverse impacts on the capacity of coming generations to exercise their rights, and that the effective participation of children and youth in the definition of climate policy is ensured. This axis seeks to incorporate the principles of justice and sustainability into climate action, considering its short-, medium- and long-term impacts, and drawing on the best available scientific evidence. From this perspective, the equitable distribution of the quality and availability of natural resources, as well as of the efforts needed for their conservation, is promoted between present and future generations.

The NDC 3.0 promotes solidarity between generations, with the purpose of preventing those who contributed least to climate change from being the ones who suffer its worst consequences.

C.1. Establish effective participation mechanisms so that children and youth play a significant role throughout the process of designing, implementing and monitoring national climate policy.

C.1.1. Encourage the participation of youth delegates in the Mexican delegation that takes part in international climate negotiations. C.1.2. Support networks of children's and youth organizations to strengthen their capacities and promote their participation in the implementation of the NDC 3.0. C.1.3. Promote spaces for intergenerational dialogue to strengthen exchange, mutual learning and capacity-building among representatives of different generations. C.1.4. Encourage, recognize and support the positive contribution of children and youth to environmental sustainability and climate justice.

C.2. Adopt the principle of intergenerational equity and respect, protect and guarantee the rights of future generations in climate action.

C.2.1. Adopt concrete tools to incorporate consideration of future generations into present-day decision-making –such as the incorporation of the social price of carbon into the cost-benefit analyses used for decision-making related to investment projects. C.2.2. Respond to foreseeable environmental threats, whose consequences will become apparent in the medium and long term, on the basis of the precautionary principle. C.2.3. Recognize the differentiated impacts of climate change on indigenous children and youth and on those belonging to other priority populations from an intersectionality approach. C.2.4. Ensure the full and effective exercise of children's rights in relation to climate change, including the right to a healthy environment, through the adoption of science-based laws, policies and strategies on environmental health and safety. C.2.5. Strengthen poverty-reduction programmes focused on children and youth in the areas most vulnerable to climate impacts.

Axis D. Just transition towards a sustainable economy

Combating climate change requires an economic and social transformation that will directly or indirectly affect all productive activities and all sectors of society. In that context, the International Labour Organization (ILO) defines a just transition as one that makes it possible to “green the economy in a way that is as fair and inclusive as possible to all those concerned, creating decent work opportunities and leaving no one behind”.

The NDC 3.0 seeks to underpin a just transition that equitably distributes the costs and benefits of climate action, avoids exacerbating existing inequalities and generates new opportunities to trigger shared prosperity. This entails, among other aspects, a profound transformation of the labour force, particularly in the industries and sectors with the highest emission levels, and ensuring that the process of transition towards a low-carbon economy respects, protects and guarantees human rights.

This axis establishes actions to identify the impacts of climate change in the labour sphere and to design a strategy that creates new green employment opportunities, promotes corporate social responsibility and contributes to improving the working conditions of workers and their communities. The just transition framework adopted by the NDC 3.0 has a holistic approach, covering both the mitigation and the adaptation components, with the aim of reducing possible negative impacts and maximizing the socioeconomic benefits of the transition.

D.1. Develop a National Just Transition Plan with the active participation of all key actors in the labour sector.

D.1.1. Ensure labour rights and the participation of workers in the design, implementation and monitoring of climate action policies. D.1.2. Promote the preparation of assessments of the impact of climate change in the labour sphere, making it possible to plan a roadmap towards a just transition of the labour force that guarantees labour rights. D.1.3. Promote the formal incorporation of the Ministry of Labour and Social Welfare into the climate governance bodies. D.1.4. Create spaces for tripartite social dialogue among unions, employers and government that contribute to informing the development of national climate policy. D.1.5. Establish a strategy to generate new green and decent employment opportunities in the sectors that are key to the transition towards a sustainable economy, placing priority populations at the centre. D.1.6. Promote training and labour reconversion in the most polluting sectors and industries, taking into account the skills and labour needs associated with different climate change mitigation and adaptation scenarios. D.1.7. Strengthen Payment for Environmental Services programmes as a central strategy to promote green employment at the community level. D.1.8. Incorporate a care perspective into climate action policy, fostering inter-institutional collaboration to recognize, reduce and redistribute domestic and care work, taking into account the impacts of climate change on the care economy.

D.2. Adopt climate and human rights due diligence policies and standards that promote corporate and business social and environmental responsibility.

D.2.1. Adopt environmental and human rights due diligence standards to incorporate climate criteria into corporate policies, making it possible to identify risks and mitigate potential impacts. D.2.2. Implement measures to strengthen climate-related financial disclosure. D.2.3. Promote the inclusion of climate criteria in public tendering and procurement processes in order to foster socially and environmentally responsible business activity. D.2.4. Recognize and strengthen environmentally sustainable MSMEs, cooperatives and social and solidarity economy models as key actors in the just transition towards a sustainable and inclusive development model.

Mexico is a country that faces deep structural inequalities requiring special attention in the context of the climate crisis, in order to comply with the principle of leaving no one behind. From that perspective, the following priority populations are identified as requiring a specific approach within the NDC, owing to their high vulnerability and their potential to contribute actively to the fight against climate change: women in all their diversity, Indigenous Peoples and Afro-Mexican communities, people in situations of human mobility, persons with disabilities, the LGBTQ+ population, workers, children and youth, older persons and environmental defenders. Mitigation Component

Mexico reaffirms its commitment to the Paris Agreement and to the international community by setting new mitigation targets for 2035, and by committing to reach net zero emissions by the middle of this century.

With the aim of increasing the transparency and traceability of efforts aimed at reducing greenhouse gases and compounds (GCEI), Mexico presents for the first time its 2035 mitigation target in terms of absolute emissions, replacing the previous relative target, formulated in relation to the baseline scenario as of 2014 (business-as-usual). On the basis of the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, an unconditional target is presented, which is estimated to be achievable with the country's own resources, along with a conditional target, subject to the availability of financing, technical assistance and technology transfer from external sources.

In line with the trajectory projected towards net zero emissions by mid-century, Mexico commits to reaching net emissions of between 364 and 404 MtCO2e in 2035 on an unconditional basis, and between 332 and 363 MtCO2e conditional on the mobilization of financing, technology transfer and capacity-building through international cooperation. This commitment is based on the country's capacity to maintain and increase carbon removals in natural sinks, estimated at at least 200 MtCO2 by 2030. It should be noted that the 2035 mitigation target is presented without sectoral allocations, given that the distribution of the effort has to be validated with greater theoretical and political depth in the next administration.

Moreover, the black carbon target is also reformulated in terms of absolute emission ranges for 2035, with an unconditional target of emissions between 35,800 and 39,700 tonnes, and a conditional target between 32,600 and 35,799 tonnes.

Meeting this pathway entails achieving the target of a 35 % reduction in GEI by 2030 relative to the baseline scenario established in the NDC 2.0, which is equivalent to emitting a maximum of 644 MtCO2e in gross emissions in that year. This target has been reflected in the National Development Plan 2025–2030. In accordance with what was agreed by the Intersecretarial Commission on Climate Change, the sectoral distribution of the mitigation effort by 2030 is established according to the criterion of responsibility, allocating the contribution of emissions to be mitigated according to the share of each sector in the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas and Compound Emissions (with preliminary 2024 data):

  • Transport: 23 %
  • Electricity generation: 19 %
  • Industry: 18 %
  • Agriculture and livestock: 17 %
  • Waste: 9 %
  • Oil and gas: 8 %
  • Land use, land-use change and forestry: 3 %
  • Residential and commercial: 3 %

The decarbonization pathways for each sector must be designed together with the actors involved, and considering the technological and economic feasibility of the proposed measures, in order to maximize emission reductions at the lowest cost and with the least possible impact on competitiveness and on the economy.

Through a vision of shared prosperity, Mexico is implementing actions aimed both at meeting its climate targets and at driving the country's economic and social development. Transport sector

In 2024, the transport sector was the country's largest emitter with 23 % of total emissions, attributed mainly to fuel consumption in road transport and to the accelerated growth of the vehicle fleet. It is also one of the sectors with the greatest opportunities to implement cost-effective measures which, in addition to having high mitigation potential, contribute directly to public health, economic development and social well-being, especially at the local level.

Electricity generation sector

In 2024 electricity generation accounted for 19 % of the country's total emissions, being the second sector with the largest contributions, mainly owing to the use of fossil fuels such as gas and fuel oil in power generation plants.

Electricity generation is one of the sectors with the greatest potential to drive decarbonization, since the transition of the electricity mix triggers and facilitates mitigation in other sectors. It also represents an opportunity to move towards fair and equitable energy systems that democratize energy and reduce inequalities such as energy poverty, which mainly affects women and girls, increases the burden of domestic and care work, and limits their access to economic and educational opportunities.

Through the secondary laws and other binding planning instruments derived from the 2025 energy reform, the Government of Mexico seeks to strengthen the public companies of the energy sector in order to guarantee a sufficient, sustainable, reliable and affordable electricity supply for the country's development. For their part, the efforts promoted by the recently published Biofuels and Geothermal Energy laws will be essential to diversify the country's energy mix.

The Electricity Sector Development Plan (PLADESE) 2025–2039, published by the Ministry of Energy, envisages three broad lines of action to meet the commitments established in the NDC, together with the participation of the private sector under defined market rules:

1) The incorporation of clean energy into electricity generation; 2) The replacement of high-carbon-content fuels with gas in high-efficiency plants; and 3) The reduction of technical losses in the electricity grid.

Likewise, PLADESE establishes that the electricity sector will reach its emissions peak in 2027 and that the share of clean sources in electricity generation will be on the order of 38.5 % in 2030 and 43.3 % in 2035. According to the Binding Program for the Installation and Retirement of Power Plants (PVIRCE), close to 70 % of the new installed capacity between 2025 and 2030 will correspond to clean energy, mainly solar photovoltaic and wind.

Within the public investments of Plan México, the first portfolio of one hundred projects for the expansion and modernization of the National Transmission Grid is considered. Likewise, as part of the Strengthening and Expansion Plan 2025–2030, and through an investment of 8,177 million United States dollars, 275 new transmission lines and 524 additional works in electrical substations will be built, benefiting 50 million people in different regions of the country. In addition to investments in the electricity grid, the Government of Mexico is promoting universal energy access programs, which seek to bring electricity to more than 500,000 households by 2030 and to reach coverage of 99 % in the country.

The binding planning established in the secondary laws of the energy sector lays the foundations for this transition to be carried out in an orderly, just and equitable manner, oriented towards meeting climate commitments, and grounded in the principles of energy security and sovereignty.

Waste sector

Controlled sites, uncontrolled sites and open dumps account for most of the sector's emissions, mainly in the form of methane (CH₄), followed by wastewater, and by the treatment and disposal of municipal and industrial discharges.

Mexico is strengthening the implementation of measures oriented towards a circular economy, with the objective of limiting waste generation, reintegrating waste into value chains in the different economic sectors and promoting its integrated management. In this context, Plan México reinforces the shift towards a zero-waste model, proposing, among other measures, to increase by 30 % the use of recycled fibers in the textile and footwear industry; to ensure the recycling or refurbishment of 100 % of household appliances and electronic products; and to replace 20 % of single-use plastics with recyclable or compostable materials.

The current administration's push on this issue is evidenced by the launch of the first project for the construction of an Ecological and Circular Economy Park, the process for updating the National Program for the Prevention and Integrated Management of Waste, and the initiative for the adoption of a General Law on Circular Economy.

All these measures must be implemented under a social and climate justice approach, which promotes equitable participation and the strengthening of capacities of local communities, especially of workers engaged in waste collection and recycling, who directly face the environmental, social and health impacts associated with soil and water contamination from open-dump leachate, and exposure to toxic agents, among other risks. Oil and gas sector

In 2024, the oil and gas sector accounted for 8 % of total national emissions. Emissions in this sector are attributed mainly to the extraction, refining and transport of hydrocarbons, specifically to gas venting and flaring, to leaks from pipelines and facilities, and to the intensive use of energy in refineries and processing centers. While these activities concentrate a significant part of the climate impact of the oil and gas sector, they also offer technological opportunities for reducing emissions, such as energy efficiency processes and the progressive replacement of fossil fuels with cleaner sources.

In 2024 the Sustainability Plan of Petróleos Mexicanos (Pemex) was published in order to contribute to meeting national climate goals, in line with efforts oriented towards energy self-sufficiency. This Plan establishes emission reduction commitments to 2030 and sets out the goal of reaching net zero emissions in scopes 1 and 2 by

2050. Likewise, it considers five pillars with lines of action, initiatives and enabling components in order to achieve concrete goals by 2030, including:

  • Reduce the intensity of scope 1 GEI emissions relative to 2021: 61 % in exploration and production, 40 % in refineries and 60% in gas processing.
  • Achieve a reduction of 30 % in methane emissions relative to 2020.
  • Eliminate routine gas flaring in exploration and production.
  • Reduce sulfur oxide (SOx) emissions in Gas Processing Centers by 90 % relative to 2021.
  • Conserve and restore at least 2,000 hectares of protected natural areas.
  • Double the level of water reuse in refineries.
  • Remediate at least 361 hectares of environmental liabilities.

For its part, the Strategic Plan 2025–2035 foresees increasing the use of electricity from renewable sources —solar, wind and geothermal—. Likewise, it maintains the environmental goals for 2030 regarding the reduction of GEI, methane emissions and sulfur dioxide in gas complexes, remediation of contaminated soils and water reuse.

Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry Sector

The Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (UTCUTS) sector plays a strategic and dual role within the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gases and Compounds (INEGyCEI). On the one hand, it is the country's main carbon sink, contributing significantly to the absorption of GEI through natural capture in forests, tropical forests, mangroves and other ecosystems, estimated in 2024 at around 210 MtCO2e. On the other hand, it accounts for approximately 3 % of national emissions, derived mainly from deforestation and forest degradation, as well as land-use change, particularly the conversion of forest ecosystems to agricultural, livestock or urban uses.

Under the leadership of SEMARNAT, the National Forestry Commission (CONAFOR) and the National Commission of Natural Protected Areas (CONANP) have implemented measures aimed at meeting the commitments established in the NDC, focused on sustainable forest management based on community management, payment for environmental services, and ecosystem protection. These measures incorporate social safeguards that, among other objectives, strengthen the role of women in the sustainable management of ecosystems. Likewise, Mexico has adopted a series of key instruments to recover and protect its natural heritage, among which the National Environmental Restoration Program stands out. Adaptation Component

Adaptation to climate change constitutes a strategic imperative and a necessary condition for guaranteeing the resilience of territories, ecosystems and communities in the face of current and future impacts. As a highly vulnerable country, Mexico understands adaptation as a political, social and cultural process that must be conducted with a territorial vision, a rights-based approach and multisectoral participation.

Within the framework of the process of developing the NDC 3.0, a comprehensive assessment was consolidated on the impacts of climate change in Mexico and the priority adaptation measures needed to address its effects. Mexico already faces significant economic losses derived from climate change, particularly in key sectors such as agriculture, health and water resources. During the 2024–2030 administration, it is estimated that the present value of accumulated losses could be equivalent to 25 % of the 2024 Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (PINCC, 2025), regardless of the climate scenario considered. This inertia of the climate, social and economic systems generates a phenomenon of structural "lock-in" of damages, which compromises the development of the coming decades if adaptation and risk management are not accelerated starting now.

On the other hand, the agricultural sector presents high exposure and sensitivity to climate change. Accumulated losses projected between 2024 and 2100 amount to 1.94 trillion pesos, concentrated in four crops —maize, wheat, sorghum and sugar cane and four states —Veracruz, Sinaloa, Tamaulipas and Jalisco. Food insecurity is intensifying, which projects a reduction of up to 464 kilocalories per person per day by

2100. Historic droughts have generated average losses of 26,752 million pesos per event in the primary sector, particularly affecting Sinaloa, Jalisco, Michoacán, Estado de México and Guanajuato (PINCC, 2025).

Currently, 15 % of the national territory is in a critical state of water availability. By 2050, a reduction of up to 21 % in surface water availability is projected, even under scenarios of global compliance with the NDC. Per capita availability would also decrease by 6 % (PINCC, 2025). On the other hand, the increase in temperature is associated with an increase in mortality from non-external causes in most of the federative entities. The evidence obtained during the process of updating the NDC 3.0 reinforces the urgency of implementing structural, intersectoral and territorially differentiated measures that reduce climate vulnerability and risk, strengthen resilience and ensure social, economic and environmental benefits in the short, medium and long term.

Mexico has made progress in building regulatory and programmatic frameworks that promote adaptation. However, structural challenges persist that limit the effective implementation of adaptive measures, particularly in rural, Indigenous and Afro-descendant communities, where climate risks are intertwined with historical vulnerabilities and social inequalities

This NDC proposes evolving towards a transformative vision that recognizes the country's biocultural diversity, local knowledge and the need to strengthen institutional capacities at all orders of government. This implies moving from fragmented sectoral approaches to comprehensive strategies that link risk management, biodiversity conservation, sustainable production and climate justice. This component will be the foundation for the preparation of Mexico's first National Adaptation Policy, whose preparation process began in 2025, and it will take the measures and lines of action of the NDC to a greater level of detail in order to make them operational, including a monitoring and evaluation framework and a portfolio of projects to advance implementation.

Adaptation, being specific to local contexts, must be just and grounded in a human rights, gender and intersectionality approach, as mandated by the General Law on Climate Change (LGCC). Therefore, this NDC 3.0 is a key opportunity to trigger better living conditions for the population, through addressing structural inequalities and combating poverty, as factors that inhibit participation in climate action.

Likewise, the aim is to consolidate climate financing mechanisms that prioritize adaptation, promote shared responsibility between public and private actors, and ensure transparency in the use of resources. In addition, inclusive climate governance is required that guarantees the effective participation of all social sectors, in particular women, youth and native peoples, in the definition, implementation and evaluation of public policies, building climate resilience that is just, lasting and representative of the country's multiple realities. For this reason, this NDC 3.0 presents six strategic axes that guide the construction of an inclusive and just adaptation policy.

Pillar C. Conservation, restoration and sustainable use of biodiversity and ecosystem services

Mexico is one of the twelve countries considered megadiverse and ranks fourth in the world in species richness. This exceptional biological diversity is combined with great cultural richness, which makes the country a strategic biocultural territory for climate action.

Climate change, together with habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species and pollution, is recognized as one of the five main threats to biodiversity. The worsening of the climate crisis represents a serious threat to ecological processes, since even small-scale changes can generate profound consequences, such as the collapse of coral reefs, the melting of glaciers, the alteration of ocean circulation patterns and changes in the migratory cycles of species, as well as potentially greater transmission of zoonotic diseases and the spread of pathogens, to mention just a few.

This pillar takes up and positions priority issues for the country's development through approaches to the conservation, restoration and sustainable management of ecosystems and their biodiversity, which contribute to green and blue carbon capture. These actions are also linked to disaster risk management, particularly in the country's coastal and island regions. In addition, they strengthen the management and connectivity of Protected Natural Areas and of conservation schemes, under climate change scenarios and with full respect for the collective rights of communities.

The measures contained in this pillar will be strengthened by reinforcing environmental policy instruments and the regulations that facilitate the execution of actions at the territorial level, in collaboration with communities, recognizing the interconnection of ecosystems and natural processes beyond the national sphere. These measures are oriented towards the consolidation of integrated territorial management that combines the conservation and resilience of ecosystems and biodiversity with a well-being and social justice approach.

Pillar D. Integrated water resources management with a climate change approach

Climate change has altered the hydrological cycle at the global level, generating impacts that are manifested both in the scarcity and in the overabundance of the resource, as well as in the degradation of its quality. In Mexico, the water available in most of the territory is under a high degree of pressure, and projections indicate that this situation will tend to intensify due to climate change and population growth.

Agriculture continues to be the main water user, followed by public supply, industrial activities and electricity generation. In parallel, human activities have modified the chemical composition of numerous bodies of water due to the discharge of wastewater without adequate treatment, which generates negative impacts on biodiversity and aggravates water availability deficits in hydrological basins. Added to this are overexploitation and growing water stress.

The effects of climate change will intensify in the areas of the country with accelerated urbanization processes, without setting aside the impacts in rural regions, where water availability and temperature changes could cause the displacement of crop areas and, consequently, affect both the rural and the urban population. In view of this, the integrated water resources management approach is recognized as central to guaranteeing the human right to water, and to promoting productive activities that recognize the value of water as a resource with a social function.

Surface runoff is expected to be reduced by 8% to 12% on average for Mexico by the middle and end of the century, surface water availability is expected to decrease in most of the country's hydrological regions, and the national average is expected to decrease by between 21% and 34% by the years 2050 and 2100, respectively (PINCC, 2025).

Loss and Damage Component

Owing to its bi-oceanic condition, its broad ecosystem diversity and the extent of its territory, Mexico is exposed to multiple risks and impacts arising from climate change. Even with the implementation of adaptation and mitigation measures, adverse effects will continue to affect the economy and livelihoods, health, infrastructure, education and security. The inclusion of a loss and damage component in the NDC 3.0 responds, on the one hand, to the country's structural vulnerability and the need to strengthen institutional and citizen capacities; and, on the other, to the urgency of consolidating mechanisms to address impacts that exceed the limits of adaptation, as well as slow-onset impacts that will intensify in the coming years.

Mexico recognizes that the losses and damages associated with climate change are no longer part of a future scenario, but of a present reality that threatens lives, economies, cultures and ecosystems. The country has already experienced hurricanes that intensify within a few hours —as in the case of Otis in 2023 —, historic droughts that devastate harvests, and coastal communities that have had to be relocated. These realities confirm that losses and damages are no longer residual, but structural, and demand a national and global response.

The General Civil Protection Law (LGPC) establishes that risk identification consists of recognizing and assessing the probable losses or damages to affectable agents and their geographic distribution, through the analysis of hazards and vulnerabilities. In this context, it is a priority to address areas of high exposure to climate risks, including the more than 11,000 kilometers of coastline exposed to cyclones and hurricanes. These conditions add to mass wasting processes in mountainous areas and to the recurrence of flash floods in various basins.

In 2025, water stress affected 64 % of the continental land area, where close to 70 % of the population lives and more than 7 % of GDP is generated. Drought, as a slow-onset phenomenon, severely affects Mexican territory. As a reference, in June 2024, 86 % of Mexico's municipalities were experiencing some degree of drought. In addition, according to the Agrifood and Fisheries Information Service (SIAP), it has been identified that, at the national level, between 2018 and 2023, the total area lost to drought amounted to 2,832,583 hectares, affecting various crops in most states, among them grain maize and beans.

Through the loss and damage component of the NDC 3.0, the Government of Mexico seeks to coordinate the response to and management of emergencies and disasters, humanitarian action, risk transfer, resilient reconstruction, climate mobility and the assessment of economic and non-economic losses. This architecture of the component recognizes that losses and damages are also social, cultural and human, and that community fragmentation, the deterioration of mental health and the loss of cultural heritage can be as devastating as the collapse of physical infrastructure.

It also promotes the use of comprehensive financial, social and cultural mechanisms that address both material replacement and the protection of community values. Among these mechanisms, parametric insurance stands out as one of the most effective financial solutions for addressing immediate losses caused by climate disasters. In Mexico, these schemes have been used to protect crops through the Catastrophic Agricultural Insurance Fund, and have demonstrated their effectiveness in providing rapid liquidity to rural producers after extreme events. The expansion of this model to strategic infrastructure, social housing and the protection of critical ecosystems represents an opportunity to strengthen the financial response and reduce dependence on emergency public funds.

Pillar A. Response to emergencies and disasters associated with climate change and humanitarian action

In the face of the growing frequency and intensity of extreme climate events, as well as the cumulative impacts of slow-onset phenomena, Mexico intends to decisively strengthen its capacities for preparedness, response and recovery in relation to residual losses and damages, with the aim of progressively reducing the impacts caused and building resilience to future emergencies.

According to the National Center for Disaster Prevention (CENAPRED), during 2023 the losses and damages caused by hydrometeorological phenomena represented approximately 0.3 % of GDP. Tropical cyclones caused damages of 85,866,5 million pesos and 54 deaths, while rains and floods generated losses of 1,393,2 million pesos and five deaths. Taken together, climate events accounted for 52.2 % of total disaster-related deaths and 98.1 % of the damages and losses recorded that year.

During climate emergencies, secondary losses, both in physical and mental health and in basic infrastructure, water and sanitation services, food security and quality of life, substantially aggravate the initial impacts, especially in communities in situations of social vulnerability. Likewise, the collection of information after the impact faces limitations due to the lack of interinstitutional coordination and timely communication, which delays the preparation of damage and loss assessments, prolongs the interruption of basic services and hinders recovery processes.

In this context, the NDC 3.0 recognizes that the availability of public, reliable, up-to-date and accessible data on losses and damages is essential for anticipatory decision-making, the planning of civil protection responses, risk assessment, the attribution of responsibilities and the mobilization of national and international financing. For this reason, Mexico will promote the creation of a national database that strengthens predictive models, optimizes the prioritization of interventions, enhances institutional and social learning, and promotes transparency and accountability.

In addition, provision is made for the use and strengthening of the early warning systems already developed under adaptation, as well as the integration of impact monitoring mechanisms in order to identify potential losses, anticipate damages and activate response protocols in a timely manner before an extreme climate event occurs, integrating the humanitarian and anticipatory action approach. Likewise, the need is recognized to provide targeted and accessible information to the groups at greatest risk — in particular girls, boys, older persons, Indigenous Peoples and persons with disabilities— through climate communication strategies that reduce uncertainty, prevent erratic behavior, mobilize coordinated responses and strengthen trust in institutions.

Through this pillar, Mexico's climate policy strengthens multilevel coordination (federation, states and municipalities) during and after climate emergencies. In addition, it seeks to reinforce municipal and state civil protection units. The actions under this pillar will guarantee life-saving assistance to affected people during emergencies and will ensure that temporary shelters are safe and dignified spaces, with a gender approach and differentiated attention.

Improve the capacities of communities in preparedness for and response to emergencies and disasters associated with climate change through the development of plans, and the formation of community risk prevention and reduction committees, with a gender, interculturality and intersectionality approach, as well as training on multi-hazard early warning systems in coordination with different actors. A.1.2. Promote the development of multi-hazard early warning systems, particularly local and community-based ones, taking into account the participation of government, academia and society, and including priority groups.

A.1.3. Strengthen the management of emergencies and disasters associated with climate change with an interinstitutional action approach, detailing the course of action to be followed before, during and after an event. A.1.4. Strengthen interinstitutional mechanisms so that the different sectors compile, systematize and update information on the impacts, losses and damages associated with climate change, in order to assess their economic, social and environmental effects and improve the planning and allocation of resources for adaptation and risk reduction. A.1.5. Promote the collection, systematization and use of information on losses and damages associated with climate change in state and municipal planning processes, in order to strengthen development management, land-use planning and disaster risk reduction.

A.2. Design a climate communication strategy that prepares the population with timely, understandable, accessible and culturally appropriate information for priority groups (including in Indigenous languages), in order to minimize and address the losses and damages arising from extreme climate events and slow-onset phenomena.

A.2.1. Create inter-institutional and cross-sectoral working groups focused on crisis communication, to facilitate access to physical and mental health care, minimize human, material or environmental impacts, and guarantee the right of access to official information.

A.2.2. Implement local dialogue spaces for the prevention of conflict and insecurity in post-disaster crises, adapted to the needs of communities and to local characteristics, in order to improve the response of the competent authorities. A.2.3. A.2.4. Support the dissemination of weather and climate event alerts issued by the competent authorities, through clear and prompt communication with the support of community risk prevention and reduction committees, using channels accessible to the population (community radio, loudspeaker announcements, cellular technology, etc.), in different languages and adapted to the needs, characteristics and settings of communities, drawing on traditional knowledge, with the free, prior and informed consent of its holders, and using understandable language. A.2.5. A.3.1. A.3.2. Provide training to all health and education personnel on the physical and mental health impacts of extreme events and slow-onset phenomena, and provide guidance on official communication channels, care centres and available support. A.3.3. Invest in the reconstruction of infrastructure with a climate-resilient vision and with technologies that guarantee continuity in the provision of basic services to meet the primary needs of the population (health, food, sanitation) in the face of climate-related emergencies. A.3.4. Implement comprehensive care and training strategies in recovery processes stemming from climate change impacts on affected populations, with a gender, human rights and intersectionality perspective.

A.4. Strengthen the humanitarian action approach in the institutions, mechanisms, processes and resources related to the immediate response to loss and damage associated with climate change, taking into account the differentiated needs of the population.

A.4.4. Develop immediate actions to clear and repair essential highways, bridges and roads, prioritizing timely access to humanitarian services.

Pillar B. Risk transfer and retention

This pillar arises from the recognition of the disproportionate and long-term impacts that some communities face from climate change, as well as the need for significantly greater efforts for the care and recovery of people, infrastructure, livelihoods, ecosystems and community cohesion. Its objective is to guarantee immediate assistance with a humanitarian approach, as well as to establish mechanisms for the timely and effective recovery of affected environments, with targeted attention to populations in situations of vulnerability and climate risk.

It is estimated that the cumulative economic losses over this century due to climate change could represent between two and nine times Mexico's GDP in 2024. Moreover, these impacts will have lasting and differentiated effects across the country's various social sectors and regions, increasing inequalities and hindering the achievement of poverty reduction and sustainable development goals. The magnitude and recurrence of climate disasters, together with slow-onset phenomena such as prolonged drought or coastal erosion from sea level rise, may jeopardize the State's financial and operational capacity, which is why a new architecture of financial, institutional and cultural protection is required.

The risk and costs associated with climate change are usually absorbed mainly by the most vulnerable people and communities, so the mobilization of the means of implementation to address loss and damage must also address the social, environmental, cultural and human dimensions, in line with the Sendai Framework, the Paris Agreement and the guidelines of the CMNUCC Fund for responding to Loss and Damage.

In Mexico, insurance has been essential for climate risk management, through the protection of the assets and livelihoods of those living in at-risk areas and the facilitation of faster economic recovery after disasters, which is why this pillar proposes building on the progress made in this area and expanding it for the benefit of all.

B.1 Develop financial security schemes, including public-private insurance for all productive sectors based on climate indices and available official public data, that consider a comprehensive approach to the provision of resources for measures aimed at averting, minimizing and addressing loss and damage associated with climate change.

B.1.1. Set up a working group with the insurance industry, financial institutions, local governments, communities, organizations and productive sectors, to establish applicable damage thresholds or parameters for developing legal frameworks and instruments such as parametric insurance. B.1.2. Design and implement mechanisms for access to life and property insurance that incorporate coverage of risks associated with climate change, targeting households, communities and productive sectors in at-risk areas, in order to protect people, their assets and livelihoods and strengthen social and economic resilience. B.1.3. Expand the use of risk transfer and retention instruments such as parametric insurance and contingency funds, ensuring a reduction in gaps in access to insurance and social protection schemes in the face of climate-related events. B.1.4. Strengthen and expand the coverage of subsidized climate insurance for small agricultural, livestock, fishing and aquaculture production units so that they receive prompt support in the face of climate-related events, as well as generate schemes in which the federal government can participate as a reinsurer of last resort or provide seed capital to help cover the cost of premiums. B.1.5. Create a Loss and Damage Index and develop financing indicators and criteria, within the framework of Mexico's Sustainable Taxonomy. B.1.6. Consolidate the operation of parametric insurance at the regional and/or state level to address the impact of hurricanes on mangrove and coral reef ecosystems, ensuring its prompt application.

B.2. Promote the development of contingency and risk retention plans, to improve resilience in sectors and communities that are vulnerable to or at risk from climate change.

B.2.1. Strengthen financial education among the population to improve understanding of financial products that protect their assets and livelihoods, and encourage the adoption of risk control measures to address climate change and decision-making in exposed, at-risk or vulnerable communities. B.2.2. Develop and promote Business Continuity Plans (PCO) that make it possible to identify critical processes and the continuation of essential public and private sector activities, through the definition of roles and responsibilities in the event of emergencies and disasters. B.2.3. Design a portfolio of primary activities that may be eligible for financing in order to diversify and/or improve the livelihoods of people affected by loss and damage resulting from climate change.

B.3. Develop a platform for the monitoring and evaluation of loss and damage associated with climate change that allows for the systematic calculation of economic impacts, brings together critical information for risk assessment and provides disaggregated data on the differentiated impact of climate change on different populations, sectors and territories.

B.3.1. Develop a loss and damage platform with periodic assessments and evaluations of the economic impacts of climate change and of loss and damage by sector, as well as of the availability of financing to address them. B.3.2. Strengthen the quality of disaggregated information and the methodologies for the monitoring, recording and assessment of loss and damage, making it possible to generate post-disaster assessments and response plans in line with people's needs and realities, taking differentiated impacts into account. B.3.3. Generate climate risk calculators, particularly for small production units and micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (MiPymes), that allow them to access specific financial information and create scenarios to calculate the costs and benefits of insurance against potential and imminent loss and damage. B.3.4. Develop inter-institutional and cross-sectoral coordination mechanisms that promote and facilitate the allocation of resources to address loss and damage, with the participation of institutional investors.

Pillar C. Reconstruction with a risk prevention and resilience approach

The NDC 3.0 seeks to guide rehabilitation and reconstruction processes that reduce existing risks and prevent the emergence of new risks, through territorial planning with a rights-based, inclusive approach and explicit criteria to build back better, build back fast, build back fair and build back potential, ensuring that their implementation is transparent, with verifiable mechanisms for generating information, oversight and follow-up.

Considering the high vulnerability and climate risk of municipalities in Mexico and that, based on climate projections, an increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme events is expected, it is a priority to address impacts on infrastructure not only from a climate change adaptation perspective, but also from one of comprehensive attention to loss and damage in reconstruction processes. The Government of Mexico, through the Ministry of Agrarian, Territorial and Urban Development (SEDATU), has a Territorial Strategy for Loss and Damage. This strategy includes medium- and long-term territorial planning components, as well as housing support in the face of disaster risks and damage caused by natural phenomena, through housing solutions aimed at displaced populations.

C.1. Design territorial rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction strategies in the territories and with the populations that are vulnerable to or at risk from climate change, considering post-disaster and build back better schemes under a human rights approach and with multi-stakeholder participation.

C.1.1. Develop a national strategy for resilient recovery that establishes standards and good practices for building back better in infrastructure and housing in at-risk areas, so that new buildings increase their resistance to climate events and align with the Mexican Official Standards on the matter. C.1.2. Establish inter-institutional and cross-sectoral collaboration mechanisms for rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction focused on eliminating structural gaps for the population affected by loss and damage. C.1.3. Strengthen the technical capacities of authorities and priority populations and communities in planning methodologies for rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction, incorporating disaster risk reduction, NbS, adaptation approaches and local traditional knowledge, with the consent of its holders. C.1.4. Strengthen the national regulatory framework for rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction with guidelines and standards that promote good practices to strengthen the resilience of communities, incorporating disaster risk reduction elements and including the criteria of build back better, build back fast, build back fair and build back potential.

C.2 Develop and/or improve codes and certifications for resilient rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction, considering post-disaster and build back better schemes, for cities with high exposure to extreme events and slow-onset phenomena.

C.2.1. Promote the adoption of public policies for disaster risk reduction by advancing the National Strategy for Comprehensive Disaster Risk Management and encourage the relevant authorities at the state and municipal level to consider various climate change scenarios and favour resilience in post-impact reconstruction and rehabilitation processes. C.2.2. Create a working group on the links between the rehabilitation, recovery and reconstruction of urban infrastructure, considering factors such as urban heat islands, thermal comfort, health, access to water and ecosystem services. C.2.3. Develop guidelines for drawing up local protocols for the identification and reconstruction of affected infrastructure, taking into account immediate needs, technologically viable solutions and safety principles in order to bring into operation drinking water networks, health centres, shelters and temporary housing solutions, roads and communications and transport elements, and educational and workplace centres that are priorities for communities, among others. C.2.4. Stabilize the most affected areas to avert, minimize and address loss and damage, as well as the immediate rehabilitation of damaged schools, hospitals and community shelters to provide temporary containment, integrating criteria of targeted attention to priority groups.

C.3 Maintain close collaboration and communication with the private sector, communities and other actors on the potential effects of climate change on existing infrastructure.

C.3.1. Create working groups with construction and business chambers, as well as with academia, communities and other actors to identify the possible risks of climate change to existing infrastructure, and identify needs for modification in building codes and other regulatory instruments with a preventive approach. C.3.2. Disseminate good practices at the local level regarding anticipatory actions to avert loss and damage to infrastructure as a consequence of extreme and slow-onset climate events.

Pillar D. Human mobility linked to the impacts of climate change

Human mobility associated with climate change is already a documented reality in Mexico. Cases such as that of the community of El Bosque, in Tabasco, reflect that the loss of habitable territories is irreversible in various coastal areas. In addition, the impacts of prolonged droughts are forcing displacement in rural and Indigenous regions. Furthermore, it is estimated that by 2050 more than three million Mexican women and men could be displaced if the rise in global temperature is not brought under control.

Attention to this pillar takes on special relevance in the face of slow-onset phenomena, such as droughts in rural areas highly dependent on agriculture, which make subsistence conditions unviable and force entire families to move to urban areas, often intensifying their vulnerability and climate risk. In addition, the sea level rise trend in the Gulf of Mexico is 44 % higher than the global average trend, which affects the coastal areas of that region, threatening entire communities.

The Government of Mexico recognizes that the impacts of climate change not only destroy infrastructure, but also displace identities, ways of life and collective memories. It follows from this that legal frameworks are required that recognize and protect mobility due to climate change as a reality that demands legal safeguards, the protection of human rights, and active citizen participation, access to targeted services, inclusive and safe shelters, education for those who relocate and cultural respect. D.1. Integrate scenarios and considerations on the impacts of climate change into Mexico's Humanist Model of Human Mobility, strengthening the capacities of all competent authorities to prevent and respond to new migratory flows and dynamics related to extreme climate events and slow-onset phenomena.

D.1.1. Develop an analysis of actors, powers, responsibilities, regulations and coordination mechanisms of the three orders of government for addressing human mobility linked to the impacts of climate change, identifying barriers to response and approaches to solutions.

D.1.2. Establish collaboration mechanisms and/or working groups among the three orders of government, civil society, academia, the private sector, international organizations and affected populations, taking into account the corresponding cultural relevance, especially when they are Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities, in order to develop a comprehensive proposal for incorporating climate criteria into the Humanist Model of Human Mobility, particularly in its pillars of addressing the structural causes of migration through cooperation in communities of origin, of expanding labour mobility pathways and of protecting the human rights and inclusion of people in mobility. D.1.3. Strengthen inter-institutional and cross-sectoral collaboration mechanisms on migration, in particular the Inter-ministerial Commission for Comprehensive Attention to Migration Matters, in order to incorporate the climate dimension into actions related to the humanist management of migration and internal displacement.

D.2. Develop a national planned relocation programme for communities and human settlements in high-risk areas, with differentiated attention to priority populations and guaranteeing the full exercise of human rights in relocation planning, the implementation of measures and post-relocation follow-up.

D.2.1. Establish technical criteria for identifying communities and human settlements at high risk from climate change and develop a national mapping to prioritize the relocation of those that are eligible, considering the assessment of vulnerability and climate risk of the areas, the analysis of the technical and economic feasibility of in situ protection measures, the assessment of the population's social vulnerability and adaptive capacity, and the determination of unacceptable risk thresholds. D.2.2. Design a territorial strategy for planned relocation for localities and communities that are vulnerable to or at risk from the impacts of climate change (including sea level rise), prioritizing safety, human rights, resilience and adaptation to climate change. D.2.3. Develop guidelines for planned relocation due to climate risk with principles of voluntariness, participation and a human rights approach, that take into account the feasibility of resettlement and the livelihoods of the affected population, and appropriate site selection with criteria of: long-term climate safety, access to services, economic opportunities, cultural acceptability and reasonable proximity. D.2.4. Establish Local Relocation Committees with majority community participation, create mechanisms to defend the rights of communities undergoing relocation, and develop differentiated attention protocols with a gender, interculturality and vulnerable group rights approach, integrating the approach of social cohesion and protection of health, housing, employment, education and safety. D.2.5. Develop integration programmes in receiving communities that include vocational training, support for formal employment, access to quality services for all family units and intercultural mediation mechanisms, among others. D.2.6. Establish support mechanisms for the voluntary, safe and dignified return of migrants, where feasible.

D.3. Recognize and address trapped populations in at-risk areas and develop programmes for assisted mobility.

D.3.1. Identify populations in situations of forced immobility (due to extreme poverty, disability, advanced age, cultural attachment, caregiving responsibilities, etc.) and, in collaboration with local authorities and civil society, map their locations and establish contact mechanisms. D.3.2. Develop and implement assisted mobility programmes for these populations that offer comprehensive financial support, personalized accompaniment, support in the search for housing and employment, and post-relocation follow-up, among others. D.3.3. Offer intermediate options such as temporary relocation during high-risk periods, as well as access to early warning systems for those who decide to stay. D.3.4. Establish mechanisms to facilitate access to life and property insurance for residents who remain, on an informed basis, in at-risk areas.

D.4. Improve understanding of the relationship between the impacts of climate change and human mobility through data recording and analysis systems that inform decision-making.

D.4.1. Develop, with the support of specialized national and international organizations, standardized protocols for data capture in temporary shelters, service points and other strategic spaces that make it possible to generate statistical information on cases of human mobility linked to climate change. D.4.2. Incorporate modules on environmental and climate causes into relevant surveys and carry out longitudinal studies in areas of high vulnerability or climate risk in order to document internal displacement and migratory dynamics. D.4.3. Train the personnel who provide services to the population in mobility and who carry out data collection.

D.5. Establish national and cross-border cooperation mechanisms on human mobility due to climate change, in order to jointly and humanely manage migratory flows linked to the impacts of climate change.

D.5.1. Develop humanitarian visa mechanisms and regularization options for people affected by climate impacts. D.5.2. Provide effective legal advice for the care and protection of people in situations of mobility due to extreme events or slow-onset phenomena linked to climate change, and develop coordinated cooperation actions at the regional level. D.5.3. Strengthen access to resources through international financial organizations in order to manage projects focused on human mobility associated with climate change and to address the causes that drive it. D.5.4. Establish safeguards to ensure that forced returns to areas of high climate risk do not occur.

Pillar E. Economic and non-economic losses (tangible and intangible)

Mexico recognizes that, in addition to direct economic impacts, the impacts of climate change have social and cultural consequences of great magnitude, such as the loss of lives, effects on physical and mental health, the fragmentation of community cohesion and the deterioration of tangible and intangible cultural heritage.

In 2023, the country recorded loss and damage of 88,910 million pesos, equivalent to 0.3 % of GDP that year. However, the repercussions of disasters go beyond the economic, since the loss of human lives constitutes a primary element. In that same year, 984 deaths related to disasters were recorded, which represented an increase of 98 % compared to 2022. Something similar occurs in Mexico with prolonged droughts, sea level rise and environmental degradation, since these have also begun to erode identities, collective memories and ways of life that cannot be quantified in monetary terms alone.

This pillar aims to develop actions that help to understand and account for loss and damage, including the development of robust methodologies, in addition to implementing strategies that favour the preservation of cultural expressions and ensure that social memory and community values endure in the face of the climate crisis. It follows that attention to loss and damage should not be limited to material reconstruction, but rather requires comprehensively addressing the economic, social, environmental, cultural and human dimensions of climate change.

E.1. Improve understanding of loss and damage associated with the impacts of climate change, both economic and non-economic (tangible and intangible), taking into account direct and indirect repercussions and cascading events, from both extreme events and slow-onset phenomena.

E.1.1. Generate studies and statistical information on the sites and sectors facing loss and damage from climate change, making it possible to guide planning and decision-making on the matter, as well as to move towards a net zero damage approach. E.1.2. Strengthen institutional capacities for the collection of information related to loss and damage, the assessment of disaster impact and the application of post-disaster recovery needs assessment methodologies. E.1.3. Establish unified methodologies for calculating economic and non-economic losses (tangible and intangible) based on international best practices that consider robust estimation techniques, economic models and quantitative and qualitative analyses, and generate interdisciplinary measurement indicators on these matters, including participatory methodologies and the recognition of incommensurable loss and damage. E.1.4. Identify the technical and capacity needs for incorporating and collecting information on loss and damage (including human mobility associated with climate change) and for this to contribute to the generation of basic national statistics on the matter.

E.2. Develop strategies focused on the protection and recovery of tangible and intangible cultural heritage, with cultural and community resilience criteria.

E.2.1. Create protocols for the protection, recovery and reconstruction of tangible cultural heritage, under the build back better approach in the cultural sphere, with the participation of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican Peoples and of other communities, as well as the integration of local and traditional knowledge. E.2.2. Protect Mexico's intangible cultural heritage in the face of climate change, through the collection of the knowledge, practices and traditions of the populations that hold such heritage, as well as the recovery of biocultural heritage, the reconstruction of the social fabric, relevant legal mechanisms and inclusive responses.

Enabling Environment and Means of Implementation Component

The significant increase in ambition represented by the NDC 3.0 implies an effort of equal magnitude in mobilizing the means of implementation to make it effective. In addition, given the number and complexity of the sectors and topics involved, the NDC requires an enabling environment that facilitates compliance with the commitments it contains, particularly a solid legal and institutional framework that strengthens coordination and collaboration among the various sectors of society, through transparent and participatory governance. Moreover, the effective and iterative implementation of the NDC entails the creation of robust monitoring, reporting and verification mechanisms that make it possible to assess progress and make the adjustments needed to respond to the circumstances faced during its period of validity.

Accordingly, the implementation of the NDC demands strategic planning and public and private climate finance, together with technology transfer and capacity building, all within a framework of the historical responsibilities of developed countries which, in accordance with the mandate of the Paris Agreement, must provide support to developing countries.

With this in mind, the regulatory reforms and the mobilization of finance, technology and technical assistance that Mexico is promoting seek to build an enabling environment with a systemic and integrated vision, one that facilitates the articulation of different instruments, generates intersectoral synergies and mobilizes all key actors to ensure effective and equitable implementation of the NDC 3.0.

Pillar a. Regulatory harmonization and institutional coordination

In Mexico there is a multiplicity of regulations, policies and institutional powers that hinders the effective application of climate policy and coordination among the various authorities responsible for its fulfilment. Since climate change and its consequences are a complex and interrelated phenomenon, it is necessary to link and articulate the various standards, policies and institutions so that, taken together, they generate a clear, coherent and harmonized framework for the effective implementation of the country's climate provisions and commitments.

Given the identification of barriers arising from regulatory and institutional fragmentation, uneven subnational capacities, and the lack of articulation and coordination among the various branches and orders of government, the following measures are proposed to reduce regulatory and institutional gaps.

A. 1. Strengthen the regulatory framework related to climate action at the national level to enable the effective implementation of the NDC 3.0. A.1.1. A.1.3. Update the General Law on Climate Change (LGCC) in line with the structure of the NDC, fostering the harmonization of planning instruments and of the powers and coordination mechanisms of the competent authorities.

Pillar B. Strategic planning and finance

According to the Sustainable Finance Mobilization Strategy of the Ministry of Finance and Public Credit, it is estimated that to meet the country's sustainable development goals it will be necessary to mobilize annually, towards 2030, approximately 1.7 trillion pesos, an amount equivalent to 5.4 % of the GDP of

2023. Faced with this enormous mobilization challenge, it is essential to diversify the sources of resources and ensure that the financial architecture responds comprehensively to the principle of sustainability.

Compliance with this NDC depends on the mobilization of financial resources, domestic and international, in a sustained, predictable, sufficient and strategic manner, geared towards its lines of action, and responding to principles of accessibility, transparency, inclusion and climate justice. This section is based on the planning, regulation and management mechanisms and instruments that the Mexican Government has been implementing to date in the area of climate finance.

Mobilizing resources of this magnitude requires a regulatory framework and management that provide guidance, structure, certainty and incentives (Pillar A), as well as a set of finance-enabling instruments that provide, in the most cost-effective manner, the resources needed to develop the lines of action and programs of the NDC components (Pillar B). This must be accompanied by planning, monitoring and evaluation that make it possible to identify whether financial gaps are closing and, where appropriate, to make the necessary adjustments (Pillar C), all within a framework of transparency, traceability and climate justice. Nevertheless, despite progress in the management and regulation of instruments that trigger climate action, Mexico needs to significantly expand the mobilization of public and private, domestic and international financial resources that create the conditions necessary for the implementation of the NDC. To this end, it is necessary to continue promoting and generating public policy instruments that facilitate a financial model capable of generating structural change towards a low-carbon economy.

Regulatory and management instruments

It is essential to have a regulatory and management framework for climate finance that facilitates and incentivizes, under the best conditions, the investment needed in all NDC components. This pillar seeks to identify barriers and best practices for investment planning, as well as to recognize the critical interconnection between regulatory frameworks, institutional powers and financial flows for effective implementation.

B.1. Promote mechanisms for aligning financial, budgetary and investment resources with climate objectives in expenditure budgets.

B.1.1. Promote the creation of instruments to incorporate climate change criteria into the planning, monitoring and evaluation of budget programs. B.1.2. Promote the updating and use of the Methodology for the Identification and Quantification of Resources for Mitigation and Adaptation. B.1.3. Promote the analysis of climate risks in public investment. B.1.4. Promote the allocation of resources by the different orders of government to contribute to the execution of infrastructure geared towards the NDC commitments.

B.2. Strengthen Mexico's Sustainable Taxonomy and promote its use to identify activities that contribute to sustainable development and to drive their financing by public and private financial institutions.

B.3. Carry out an assessment of green fiscal policy instruments and their possible improvement in order to drive the development of projects linked to the measures and lines of action of the NDC.

B.4. Strengthen a policy to promote insurance against the impacts of climate change that incorporates climate risk criteria.

B.4.1. Promote the implementation of a financial risk management strategy to ensure that sufficient, timely and effective financial instruments and resources are available to respond immediately to extreme weather events, address the emergency, protect the population, restore public services, contribute to the recovery of productive sectors and rebuild with a long-term vision.

Finance-enabling instruments

Increasing and accelerating finance, from both public and private sources, is crucial so that the measures and lines of action of the NDC are financed with the most appropriate and innovative sources and mechanisms.

B.5. Implement the first operational phase of the Emissions Trading System.

B.6. Develop and implement the National Offsets Program to drive and regulate the generation of environmental projects that make it possible to generate offset credits with high environmental integrity.

B.7. Facilitate access to financing from national and international companies and organizations, in order to accelerate the availability of resources from public and private sources.

B.7.1. Promote innovative financing programs that mobilize public and private resources for decarbonization, climate resilience, applied research and sustainable development, among others, prioritizing the participation of local actors and the domestic financial sector. Pillar C. Technology development and transfer

The Paris Agreement underscores the importance of fully realizing technology development and transfer in order to improve resilience to climate change and reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Mexico has a legal and institutional framework that promotes inclusive science and innovation, particularly with the entry into force of the General Law on Humanities, Sciences, Technologies and Innovation (LGHCTI) in 2023 and the creation of the Ministry of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation (SECIHTI) as the federal agency governing this area.

Following Mexico's ratification of the Paris Agreement and its entry into force in 2016, the necessary legal adjustments were made to incorporate the provisions of the Paris Agreement into the national legal framework. Thus, the General Climate Change Law (LGCC) was amended in 2018 to establish the concept of "Nationally Determined Contributions" (NDC) as the "set of objectives and targets, assumed by Mexico, within the framework of the Paris Agreement, on climate change mitigation and adaptation to meet the long-term objectives of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change", in accordance with Article 3, section X, of the LGCC. The LGCC establishes the National Climate Change System (SINACC) as the main multi-actor and multilevel coordination mechanism on climate change. It is made up of the Inter-ministerial Commission on Climate Change (CICC), which coordinates the action of the various Ministries of State; the National Institute of Ecology and Climate Change (INECC), which provides technical and scientific support; the Congress of the Union, which provides regulatory and budgetary backing; the state governments and municipal authorities, responsible for implementing their powers in accordance with local realities; and the Climate Change Council (C3), an advisory body that contributes the perspective of academia, civil society and the private sector.

In line with the above, Mexico reaffirms its commitment before the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (CMNUCC) by presenting the update of its NDC 3.0. Within this same legal framework, the Ministry of the Environment and Natural Resources (SEMARNAT) fulfils its mandate to prepare NDC 3.0 with the support of INECC, the approval of the CICC and the opinion of the C3. Consequently, and in accordance with section IV of article 47 of the LGCC, which indicates that the CICC must approve NDC 3.0, 2 ordinary sessions were held of the 5 Working Groups that make up that Commission -Mitigation Working Group (GT); Adaptation GT and Loss and Damage subgroup; GT on Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation (REDD); Financing GT; and International Negotiations GT to present the proposal of targets, measures and lines of action of NDC 3.0. The plenary of the CICC approved NDC 3.0. It was subsequently sent to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (SER) which, in its capacity as National Focal Point before the CMNUCC, sent it to the Secretariat. Mexico presented NDC 3.0 at COP30.

The process of updating Mexico's NDC 3.0 was broad, participatory and deliberative. It was led by SEMARNAT and INECC. Seven sectoral dialogues on mitigation, adaptation, and loss and damage were held, in which more than 200 people from 52 government agencies and entities participated. 30 meetings were held with APF agencies and 8 specific consultations for adaptation and mitigation. In addition, 19 participatory workshops were held with federal government institutions, subnational governments, international organizations, academia, civil society, the private sector and traditionally underrepresented groups. These workshops were guided by the vision of strategic foresight and participatory governance as science-based approaches for the anticipatory construction of public climate policy. Two online surveys were also carried out: the first, in collaboration with UNICEF and aimed at children and youth; the second, prepared by SEMARNAT and aimed at society in general.

Lastly, it is noteworthy that NDC 3.0 is underpinned by multiple modelling exercises led by INECC, with the collaboration of the Danish Energy Agency (ADE), the Mexico Climate Initiative (ICM), and the Climate Change Research Programme (PINCC) of the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), among other members of the Mexican modelling community. Various scenarios were developed to identify the optimal sectoral decarbonization pathways, and to map climate risks in the national territory.

e) Sector-, category- or activity-specific assumptions, methodologies and approaches consistent with IPCC guidance, as appropriate, including, where applicable: i. The approach used to address emissions and subsequent removals from natural disturbances on managed lands;

i) To estimate CO2 emissions and removals derived from natural disturbances and anthropogenic activities on managed lands, the Losses and Gains methodology is used through the approach of Activity Data (DA) by Emission Factors (FE), where the specific FE take into account carbon variations on the basis of sources and sinks in accordance with the IPCC 2006 Guidelines.

With the gains and losses approach, emissions are defined as the net balance of the increases and reductions of a carbon store and are estimated as the product of the areas that cause emissions and removals, characterized as activity data, and the responses of the carbon stocks for those activities, characterized as emission factors (IPCC, 2006)

The estimation of specific emission factors through the difference in carbon contents, measured between two points in time (stock-difference method), is considered. The estimation of FE to assess some mitigation measures is complemented with modelling.

This approach includes the assessment of five essential pools: above-ground biomass, below-ground biomass, litter, dead wood and soil organic matter in forest lands.

The detailed breakdown of the procedures for estimating changes in carbon stocks, taking into account changes in areas and management conditions, as well as the statistical and technical tools included in the methodology, will be presented in the National Inventory Report (NID) and in subsequent BTRs.

This approach guarantees consistency with international best practices, ensuring that anthropogenic carbon changes are quantified in a robust and transparent manner, and that the reported emissions and removals faithfully reflect the dynamic processes that occur in the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry sector in Mexico.

ii. The approach used to account for emissions and removals resulting from harvested wood products;

ii) The estimation of emissions and removals corresponding to Harvested Wood Products uses the annual change in total stocks approach, in addition to the production approach, in accordance with the provisions of the Modalities, procedures and Guidelines of the Paris Agreement. The quantification of changes in carbon stocks as a result of timber production follows the methodological recommendations of the 2006 IPCC Guidelines, by tracking the inflows and outflows of the carbon pool contained in products in use, based on historical indices.

The products considered in this category include solid wood (covering sawnwood, panels and roundwood), as well as paper-derived products (paper and cardboard). The specific methodological aspects for the estimation of changes in carbon stocks are detailed in Mexico's Report of the National Inventory of Greenhouse Gas and Compound Emissions (NID).

iii. The approach used to address the effects of the age structure of forests;

iii) To address the effects of age structure in forests, the information generated by the National Forest and Soils Inventory (INFyS), which has a systematic and stratified sampling design at the national level, will be assessed. The INFyS establishes a network of clusters or sampling units distributed equidistantly throughout the territory, where detailed dendrometric information on forest vegetation is collected, including variables that make it possible to characterize the temporal and spatial structure of forests. Based on INFyS information, stratified sampling is carried out by vegetation type and ecological region. In addition, complementary inputs will be assessed, such as those derived from available remote sensors or their integration with INFyS data.

On the basis of this integration, the feasibility of analysing the age structure of forests and estimating their growth rates and carbon dynamics will be explored.

The specific details of the methodological procedures, analysis processes and data updates related to age structure will be presented in the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR) in order to strengthen the estimation and monitoring of carbon stocks in the forestry sector.

Glossary

Adaptation. Measures and adjustments in human or natural systems, in response to projected or actual climate stimuli, or to their effects, which may moderate harm, or take advantage of their beneficial aspects.

Blue foods. Fish, shellfish, aquatic plants and algae, caught or farmed in freshwater and marine environments to promote healthy, sustainable, inclusive and climate-resilient diets.

Biodiversity. Variability of living organisms from any source, including, among others, terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems, as well as the ecological complexes of which they form part; it comprises diversity within each species, between species and of ecosystems.

Bioenergy fuels. Fuels obtained from biomass derived from organic matter from agricultural, livestock, forestry, aquaculture and algae culture activities, fishery, domestic, commercial and industrial residues, from microorganisms and enzymes, and from their derivatives, through sustainable technological processes that comply with the specifications and quality standards established by the competent authority, in accordance with the Law on the Promotion and Development of Bioenergy Fuels.

Carbon dioxide (CO2). A gas that exists naturally and also as a by-product of the combustion of fossil fuels from carbon deposits of fossil origin, such as oil, gas or coal, as well as from the burning of biomass, or from land-use changes and other industrial processes. It is the anthropogenic greenhouse gas that most affects the Earth's radiative balance.

Climate change. Variation of the climate attributed directly or indirectly to human activity that alters the composition of the global atmosphere and adds to the natural climate variability observed over comparable periods.

Adaptive capacity / adaptive capacity of ecosystems. Set of capacities, resources and institutions of a country or region that would make it possible to implement effective adaptation measures. It is the ability of ecosystems to adjust to climate change —including climate variability and its extremes— with the aim of moderating potential damage, taking advantage of opportunities, and coping with its consequences.

Natural capital. Set of ecosystems and the organisms they contain (plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms), which through their natural processes in the ecosystem generate environmental goods and services that are indispensable for survival and social well-being.

Greenhouse compounds. Greenhouse gases (GEI), their precursors and particles that absorb and emit infrared radiation in the atmosphere.

Ecological connectivity. Free movement of species and flow of ecological processes — physical, chemical and biological— that sustain life on Earth. It includes processes associated with trophic relationships, natural disturbances and hydroecological flows, and can be manifested from local scales (for example, streams or coral reefs) to regional or continental scales (for example, chains of islands, mountains or large river systems).

Deforestation. Loss of forest vegetation, whether from induced or natural causes, which gives rise to any other non-forest condition.

Degradation. Reduction of the carbon content in natural vegetation, ecosystems or soils, caused by human intervention, in comparison with the state they would have without such intervention.

Sustainable development. Development process that requires concentrated efforts to build an inclusive and resilient future for people and the planet, in which the needs of the present can be met without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. To achieve this objective it is essential to harmonize three basic elements: economic growth, social inclusion and environmental protection.

Urban development. Process of planning and regulation for the creation, conservation, improvement and growth of population centres.

Disaster. Damage caused by the occurrence of one or more severe or extreme disturbing agents, whether concatenated or not, of natural or anthropogenic origin, which, when occurring in a given time and area, exceed the response capacity of the affected community.

Ecosystem. Set of species in a given area that interact with each other and with their abiotic environment through processes such as predation, parasitism, competition and symbiosis. The species of the ecosystem, including bacteria, fungi, plants and animals, depend on one another and their relationships with the environment allow the flow of matter and energy and the recirculation of nutrients.

Intergenerational equity. Principle of international environmental law that seeks to distribute the quality and availability of natural resources and of the efforts for their conservation between present and future generations.

Ecotechnologies. Instruments and techniques developed to make efficient use of natural and material resources, allowing the production of goods and services, as well as the sustainable use of diverse natural and material resources for daily life.

Greenhouse effect. Phenomenon whereby greenhouse gases (GEI) effectively absorb the infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface and by the atmosphere itself. This radiation is emitted in all directions, especially towards the Earth's surface, which causes heat to be retained in the surface-troposphere system.

Emissions. Release of greenhouse gases, their precursors and aerosols into the atmosphere, including, where applicable, greenhouse compounds, in a given area and period of time.

Green employment. Decent and sustainable jobs that contribute to preserving and restoring the environment, both in traditional sectors and in new emerging sectors, such as renewable energy.

Exposure. Presence of people, livelihoods, services and infrastructure, as well as natural resources, economic, social or cultural activities in places that may be adversely affected by phenomena or hazards.

Climate finance. Financial resources from sources that are national and external to the country (of public or private origin) intended to facilitate and implement the implementation of the national climate change policy, as well as the actions that contribute to reducing emissions of greenhouse gases and compounds, transitioning towards low-carbon development, conserving and increasing carbon sinks, reducing vulnerability and maintaining and increasing the resilience of human and ecological systems to the impacts and negative externalities of climate change, through adaptation measures, as well as the development of policies, programmes and projects in this area.

Greenhouse gases (GEI). Gaseous components of the atmosphere, natural or anthropogenic, that absorb and emit infrared radiation.

Gender. Category of analysis in the social sciences, which refers to a classification of people, on the basis of sexual difference, in order to assign characteristics, roles, expectations, spaces, hierarchies, permissions and prohibitions to women and men within society. This distinction is a social and cultural construction that restricts people's responsibilities and the development of their full capacity. The use of gender as a justification for binary, masculine and heteronormative supremacy reproduces conditions of injustice and inequality; it is important to recognize that, since gender is a social construct, it can be modified in favour of equality among people, regardless of their gender identity or biological sex.

Comprehensive risk management. Set of actions aimed at the identification, analysis, assessment, control and reduction of risks, considering them by their multifactorial origin and in a permanent process of construction, which involves the three levels of government, as well as the sectors of society, which facilitates the carrying out of actions aimed at the creation and implementation of public policies, strategies and procedures integrated into the achievement of sustainable development patterns that combat the structural causes of disasters and strengthen society's capacities for resilience or resistance. It involves the stages of identification of risks and/or their process of formation, forecasting, prevention, mitigation, preparedness, relief, recovery and reconstruction.

Gender equality. Condition in which all people, without distinction of gender,, have access with the same possibilities and opportunities to the use, control and benefit of the goods, services and resources of society, and to decision-making in social, economic, political, cultural and family life.

Strategic infrastructure. Set of spaces, buildings, constructions, equipment and other assets intended for the functioning, maintenance and operation of the activities considered strategic by the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States, indispensable for the provision of public goods and services and whose destruction or disablement is a threat against national security and would cause harm to the population, their property or environment.

Interculturality and cultural appropriateness. Interrelated concepts that promote coexistence and respect among cultures. Interculturality is understood as the process of dialogue, exchange and circular learning among different cultures, without asymmetries of power, under principles of mutual respect, equality and recognition of the other, of the capacities and rights of people or communities, which lead to the revaluation and positive reaffirmation of the identity of each people. For its part, cultural appropriateness refers to the adaptation, alignment and coherence of government services and actions with linguistic and cultural characteristics, with the worldview and conceptions of development and well-being of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican Peoples, as well as with their geographic, environmental and socioeconomic characteristics, among others. In this sense, cultural appropriateness is closely linked to interculturality.

LGBTIQ +. Acronym that groups together lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, intersex and queer people, as well as other diverse gender identities and expressions and sexual orientations.

Forest management. Process comprising the set of actions and procedures whose purpose is the ordering, cultivation, protection, conservation, restoration and use of the resources and environmental services of a forest ecosystem, subject to the consideration of ecological principles, respecting the functional integrality and interdependence of resources and without compromising the productive capacity of the ecosystems and resources existing therein.

Mitigation. Set of policies, measures and actions intended to reduce or limit emissions of greenhouse gases and compounds from sources, as well as to conserve, strengthen or increase the sinks that absorb them.

Electric mobility. Land transport systems based on light and heavy vehicles with electric traction systems or hybrid systems that obtain energy from an electricity supply source and are used to transport people or material goods. Also called "electromobility" in some regulatory and policy instruments. Territorial planning. Public policy oriented towards the occupation and rational use of the territory as the spatial basis for socioeconomic development strategies and environmental preservation.

Sexual orientation. Capacity of each person to feel emotional, affective, erotic and/or sexual attraction towards people of a gender different from their own, of their same gender, or of more than one gender or gender identity, as well as the capacity to maintain intimate and sexual relationships with these people.

Effective participation. Condition whereby the ideas, opinions and proposals of the population are substantively integrated into the design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of public policies.

Biocultural heritage. Set of local ecological knowledge and practices, the associated biological wealth (ecosystems, species and genetic diversity), landscape features and cultural landscapes, as well as the heritage, memory and living practices of managed or built environments.

Hazard. Process, phenomenon or human activity that may cause loss of life, injury or other health impacts, property damage, social and economic disruption or environmental degradation. It is characterized by its location, intensity, frequency or duration.

Loss and damage. Negative effects of climate change that occur despite mitigation and adaptation efforts. The concept is often classified into two categories: economic loss and damage, which refers to negative impacts whose costs are quantifiable, such as damage to infrastructure or reduced crop yields; and non-economic loss and damage, which refers to negative impacts that are not easily traded in markets and tend to be more difficult to quantify in monetary terms, such as the loss of culture, ecosystem services and displacement. These tend to be more irreparable and irreversible.

Environmental defenders. Individuals or groups who, in a personal or professional capacity, and peacefully, strive to protect and promote human rights related to the environment, in particular water, air, land, flora and fauna. On many occasions, defenders are also members and/or leaders of indigenous and Afro-descendant communities, or may be members of human rights movements or broad social networks. Their work focuses on demanding respect for the rights to land and natural resources of communities affected by development projects, business activities and climate measures that do not take into account their rights, needs and perspectives.

Preparedness. Set of activities and measures taken in advance to ensure an effective response to the impact of a disturbing phenomenon in the short, medium and long term.

Preservation. Set of policies and measures to maintain the conditions that foster the evolution and continuity of natural ecosystems and habitats, as well as to conserve viable populations of species in their natural environments and the components of biodiversity.

Prevention. Set of actions and mechanisms implemented in advance of the occurrence of disturbing agents, with the purpose of knowing the hazards or risks, identifying, eliminating or reducing them; as well as of avoiding or mitigating their destructive impact on people, property, infrastructure, and the environment. These actions also seek to anticipate the social processes that contribute to the generation of risk.

Priority populations. In accordance with the Strategic Plan on Gender, Human Rights and Climate Change, priority populations are considered to be "women in all their diversity, children, adolescents and youth; indigenous and Afro-Mexican population; people in situations of human mobility; defenders of human rights in environmental matters; LGBTIQ+ population; older adults; and persons with disabilities".

Green fiscal policy. Set of tax, budgetary and financial instruments that promote sustainable economic activities and technological innovation; strengthen the conservation and sustainable use of biodiversity and ecosystems; and disincentivize polluting practices and the inefficient use of natural resources.

Reforestation. Induced establishment of forest vegetation on forest lands.

Resilience. Capacity of natural or social systems to recover from or withstand the effects arising from climate change.

Restoration. Process aimed at assisting the recovery of an ecosystem that has been degraded, damaged or destroyed. It includes the set of activities intended for the recovery and re-establishment of the conditions that foster the evolution and continuity of natural processes.

Disaster risk. Probability that severe alterations to the normal functioning of a society will occur due to climatic events that interact with conditions of social vulnerability.

Risk, probable damage or losses to an affectable agent. Result of the interaction between its vulnerability and the presence of a disturbing agent.

Food security. Situation in which all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient food in good condition and nutritious, meeting the dietary needs and food preferences that allow an active and healthy life.

Structural and functional safety. Condition that refers both to the physical stability of the building or infrastructure and to its operational and functional capacity to guarantee the continuity of essential services.

National security. Set of actions intended in an immediate and direct manner to maintain the integrity, stability and permanence of the Mexican State, leading to: I. The protection of the Mexican nation against the threats and risks it faces; II. The preservation of national sovereignty and independence and the defence of the territory; III. The maintenance of the constitutional order and the strengthening of the democratic institutions of government; IV. The maintenance of the unity of the constituent parts of the Federation indicated in article 43 of the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States; V. The legitimate defence of the Mexican State with respect to other States or subjects of international law, and VI. The preservation of democracy, founded on the economic, social and political development of the country and its inhabitants.

Drought. Extreme hydrometeorological phenomenon that occurs as a result of a prolonged disturbance of the hydrological cycle, causing a significant water deficit. It is characterized by local water resources being insufficient for carrying out the established or customary activities of the place, being a natural phenomenon caused by a transitory anomaly, prolonged over time, with precipitation values below the normal value in a region. Droughts are interpreted and classified as meteorological, hydrological and agricultural.

Energy system. Set of components related to the production, conversion, delivery and use of energy. In an energy system, the production of electricity is one of the components of that system, with the use of energy and its integration into the activities of society being of comparable importance, as well as its relationship with the economy and the environment; likewise the technologies and end-use equipment for energy and the energy efficiency of the system.

Nature-based solutions. Actions to protect, sustainably manage and restore natural and modified ecosystems that address social challenges effectively and adaptively, simultaneously benefiting people and nature.

Energy transition. Planned and progressive process for the country's energy mix, which envisages the rational and sustainable change of the current energy, industrial, technological and economic system based on the transformation of non-renewable energy resources, towards another energy system based on the progressive use of electricity from renewable and clean energies.

Sustainable tourism. Tourism activity that complies with the following guidelines: I. Make optimal use of the natural resources suitable for tourism development, helping to conserve them in accordance with the applicable laws; II. Respect the sociocultural authenticity of host communities, conserving their cultural attractions, their traditional and architectural values, and III. Ensure the development of viable economic activities that deliver socioeconomic benefits, including employment opportunities and the generation of income and social services for host communities, that contribute to improving living conditions.

Vulnerability. Level at which a system is susceptible to, or unable to withstand, the adverse effects of climate change, including climate variability and extreme events. Vulnerability is a function of the character, magnitude and rate of the climate variation to which a system is exposed, its sensitivity and its adaptive capacity.

Annex

Gaps affecting priority populations in the context of combating climate change in Mexico

The climate crisis affects different groups in society in differentiated ways, aggravating social, economic and gender inequalities and inequalities in access to resources. This situation especially affects populations that have historically been excluded and marginalized because of their sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, ethnicity, migration status, social class, disability or age, as well as those who defend territories and rights.

Recognizing these gaps constitutes an opportunity for the climate policy derived from the NDC 3.0 to become a vehicle for advancing towards social justice, substantive equality and sustainable development.

Below is an overall analysis of the gaps in access to rights faced by some of the populations in the greatest situation of vulnerability to the impacts of climate change in Mexico:

Population

Description of the gaps

Women in all their diversity

The climate crisis has a differentiated impact on women, adolescents and girls in all their diversity, since it interacts with and exacerbates existing inequalities, which erodes their capacity for resilience in the face of present and future crises. In the patriarchal system, the sexual division of labour, social norms, gender roles and stereotypes perpetuate inequalities and systemic discrimination. In particular, Indigenous and Afro-descendant women living in rural contexts disproportionately experience the negative impacts of climate change because of the decline in essential resources such as water, energy sources and food and the increase in their prices, as well as the lack of security of land tenure and their limited participation in the decision-making that affects their lives and those of their families and communities. In addition, gender inequalities are especially aggravated by the impact of climate change on unpaid domestic and care work, which falls disproportionately on women and girls. The alteration of the climate system reduces the capacity to provide care, exponentially increases the demand for this work within families and communities and limits access to the inputs and basic infrastructure that sustain care systems. The difference in roles, responsibilities and needs directly influences the resources available to women, adolescents and girls and the way in which they can confront the impacts of climate change. It is also important to highlight the connection between the climate crisis and the increase in violence against women, since disasters and the tensions arising from environmental degradation tend to sharpen conditions of vulnerability and limit the possibilities for protection and access to justice. Nevertheless, women in all their diversity must be recognized as fundamental agents of change, since their knowledge, wisdom and experience are essential to driving the social transformations that are required and to articulating solutions to climate change.

Indigenous Peoples and Afro-Mexican communities

Indigenous Peoples and Afro-Mexican communities play a fundamental role in the conservation of biodiversity, since a large part of it is found in their territories. Women, in particular, contribute actively through traditional practices such as polyculture, which favour soil regeneration and agrodiversity. Their ancestral knowledge and their bond with the natural environment are key to ecological restoration projects. However, they face exclusion from decision-making processes and a lack of recognition as key actors, which limits their access to resources and therefore reduces their capacity to respond to climate change, aggravating their vulnerability. Despite their biocultural wealth, these communities live in conditions of poverty, marginalization and discrimination. Climate change also threatens to cause non-economic loss and damage, such as the disappearance of languages and ancestral agroforestry practices and the weakening of their forms of social and cultural organization, which puts at risk their food security, their self-determination and their livelihoods. The 16 municipalities with more than 40 % Afro-Mexican population are located in the states of Guerrero and Oaxaca, mostly in the Costa Chica region, except for Valerio Trujano, which is in the Cañada Oaxaqueña region. Coastal areas are particularly vulnerable to flooding and Extreme Hydrometeorological Events (FHE); in addition, they are affected by saline intrusion, heat waves, sea level rise and coastal erosion, which are slow onset phenomena.

Children, adolescents and youth

The impacts of climate change affect children, adolescents and youth disproportionately, especially those living in conditions of vulnerability. According to the IPCC, the increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme events makes recovery more difficult for the poorest families and deepens existing inequalities. This pushes children and youth into contexts of greater poverty, forced mobility, family violence and social tension. According to UNICEF's Children's Climate Risk Index, Mexico ranks 54 out of 200 countries where children face high risks from phenomena such as floods, cyclones and water scarcity. Furthermore, children are especially vulnerable to climate change because of their lower physical and physiological capacity to cope with extreme phenomena such as heat waves, floods or droughts. It is estimated that 88 % of the increase in diseases associated with climate change will affect girls and boys under five years of age. Limited access to basic services such as water, health or education reduces their adaptive capacity and aggravates their exposure to climate impacts. In 2023, heat waves forced classes to be suspended in 18 states of the country, affecting the right to education of more than 13 million children. These events show how the climate crisis directly violates their rights, limits their development opportunities and deepens the inequalities that will mark coming generations.

LGBTIQ+ population

These communities experience various forms of violence and exclusion because of homophobia, transphobia, lesbophobia, biphobia, racism and sexism. The binary system imposed by the heteropatriarchal structure, which recognizes only two biologically determined genders, imposes gender roles and dismisses sexual diversity, which has accentuated violence against LGBTIQ+ people in various spaces. In 2022, according to the National Survey on Discrimination (ENADIS), of the population aged 18 and over belonging to sexual and gender diversity, 37.3 % reported having experienced at least one situation of discrimination. Data disaggregated by sex (assigned at birth) reveals a higher percentage of women who declared having suffered discrimination, compared with men. This situation of social vulnerability is interrelated with their adaptive capacity in the face of the impacts of climate change throughout the entire risk cycle: poor air quality and greater exposure to pollution, restricted access to green spaces (including discrimination and surveillance in parks), disproportionate impacts of disasters with worse outcomes due to discriminatory practices in infrastructure and emergency services, food insecurity and land and water pollution with negative health effects; in addition, there is limited access to green financing, heightened risks for environmental defenders and fragmented political participation, which restricts their effective influence. For example, in the face of extreme hydrometeorological phenomena, the lack of legal recognition of families headed by two fathers or two mothers, as well as discrimination and violence, limits access to the support offered by the State. In addition, the LGBTIQ+ population is exposed to situations of violence, rejection or discrimination in the shelters set up to respond to climate emergencies. In situations of human mobility caused by extreme climate events, temporary shelter spaces do not usually take into account the specific needs of the LGBTIQ+ population, which can exacerbate situations of discrimination and violence and their vulnerability to the impacts. These dynamics are cut across by intersections of identity (trans, Indigenous/Two-Spirit, racialized, disabled and young people) and territorial conditions (e.g. island territories and urban areas with heat islands), which deepens vulnerabilities and hinders recovery after extreme events.

People in contexts of human mobility induced by climate change

Climate change aggravates existing vulnerabilities and limits the exercise of human rights, especially affecting communities living in poverty, displaced and marginalized, which have contributed minimally to GEI emissions. This context forces many people to move from their place of origin, both internally and internationally, in search of decent living conditions. The World Bank estimates that between 9.4 and 17.1 million people in Latin America will be internally displaced for climate reasons by 2050. For its part, the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre (IDMC) recorded that, in the last decade, a total of 24.5 million people in the Americas were displaced within their countries because of disasters and sudden events, mainly due to storms and floods.

Persons with disabilities

Persons with disabilities have to face a series of social barriers in their daily lives, among which greater poverty, a higher unemployment rate and difficult access to education stand out. In addition, according to the UN, their unemployment rate is twice that of the rest of the population. Disasters caused by climate change and slow onset phenomena can seriously affect persons with disabilities by limiting their access to basic rights such as food and nutrition, drinking water and sanitation, health and access to medicines, education and training, adequate housing and decent work. They also face disproportionate risk: it is estimated that they are between two and four times more likely to die in disasters and emergencies than persons without disabilities. Moreover, conditions such as rain, ice or strong winds can discourage people with reduced mobility from travelling, since they influence both their walking speed and the very possibility of using public space. Given the holistic approach to disability, it is essential to consider accessibility criteria that guarantee that they can participate in all aspects of their lives independently and to provide psychological, material and community support in the face of the impacts of climate change.

Older persons

Economic vulnerability and health conditions related to chronic degenerative diseases, dependence on medicines and the propensity to develop a disability, in addition to the deterioration of the body due to natural ageing, make older persons more vulnerable to the negative effects of climate change, such as FHE and vector-borne diseases, particularly older women. Older persons experience greater vulnerability in situations of disaster, heat waves, poor air quality and other climate events. Their chronic health problems or particular needs may delay evacuation in situations of climate risk or threat, and in some cases there is resistance to moving to shelters, which increases their exposure. Older women play a fundamental role in the preservation and transmission of traditional knowledge that is essential for climate adaptation. In local communities, they conserve knowledge about medicinal plants, ancestral methods of food preservation and agricultural practices adjusted to local conditions. However, there is concern about the lack of continuity in the intergenerational transmission of this knowledge, threatened by territorial transformations, economic pressures and the migration of young people. This situation represents a significant loss of cultural tools that are indispensable for community resilience.

Environmental and human rights defenders

People and communities that defend the environment and human rights are frequently victims of homicide, intimidation and attacks because of their work in protecting ecosystems and their struggle for the recognition of rights. Community members, civil society organizations, women, agrarian populations and Indigenous Peoples in particular tend to be stigmatized and criminalized for carrying out this work, which is fundamental for the conservation of nature. In the last 10 years at least 189 lethal attacks against environmental defenders have been documented in Mexico The context of illegal logging, the construction of development megaprojects, the operation of industrial projects, as well as the activity of organized crime in certain areas of the country places people and communities that defend the environment in a particular state of vulnerability.

Workers

Workers face differentiated impacts from climate change due to water stress, the loss of biodiversity, and impacts on health and on human mobility. All of these conditions mean that workers, especially those belonging to priority populations and who are most represented in poverty and inequality indices, are the ones most affected by environmental degradation. Likewise, the transformation of polluting industrial sectors may entail risks related to job losses if measures are not taken to retrain and reconvert the labour force and to provide new green employment opportunities for workers. In this context, gender gaps in employment may also be exacerbated in sectors that are key to the transformation towards sustainable economies and that are traditionally dominated by men and where women tend to be underrepresented.
