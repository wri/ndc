Preface

The Islamic Republic of Mauritania, a Sahelo-Saharan country particularly exposed to the effects of climate change, firmly reaffirms its resolute commitment to the global fight against this phenomenon. Through the submission of this third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0), our country confirms its determination to accelerate the ecological transition, to consolidate sustainable, resilient and low-carbon economic development, and to contribute actively to the collective effort to preserve our planet.

Aware of the vulnerability of its populations and the fragility of its ecosystems, Mauritania places the sustainable management of its natural resources and the protection of the environment at the heart of its national priorities. This orientation is fully in line with the principles of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Paris Agreement. NDC3.0 thus marks a major step in the continuity of the efforts already undertaken, while integrating achievements, lessons learned, as well as new challenges and opportunities. It is based on a participatory and inclusive approach, involving all stakeholders: public institutions, local authorities, civil society, the private sector, women, young people, and technical and financial partners.

In terms of mitigation, Mauritania commits to reducing its net greenhouse gas emissions by 75,8 % by 2035 compared to the baseline scenario. This ambitious target includes an unconditional effort of 8,1 %, mobilising national resources, and a conditional reduction of 67,7 %, dependent on the support of international cooperation. To achieve this, our country intends to make full use of its immense renewable energy potential, notably solar, wind and green hydrogen, pillars of a low-carbon economy that holds opportunities for future generations.

In view of the increased vulnerability of its ecosystems and populations, the adaptation component of NDC3.0 now includes 85 measures aimed at strengthening resilience in key sectors such as water management, agriculture, food security, fisheries, health, housing, education and ecosystem conservation. Particular attention is paid to strengthening national capacities, to integrating cross-cutting priorities — notably gender, youth, employment and human mobility — as well as to promoting inclusive, equitable and sustainable development.

The realisation of these ambitions will depend on access to adequate, predictable and accessible financing, estimated at 18 088,33 million US dollars, of which 15 642,85 million are conditional on the mobilisation of external resources. Beyond financing, Mauritania calls for increased support for the transfer of clean technologies, for institutional and technical capacity building, and for the establishment of a robust Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system, guaranteeing the transparency and effectiveness of its climate action.

The Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development expresses its deep gratitude to all technical and financial partners, to national stakeholders, and to Mauritanian youth, whose mobilisation and commitment were essential to the preparation of this new national climate ambition.

Together, united by the same vision, we will build a resilient, low-carbon and prosperous Mauritania, faithful to its international commitments and resolutely turned towards a sustainable future.

Overall ambition of Mauritania’s third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC3.0)

To make Mauritania a model of climate resilience by 2035, by achieving an ambitious 75,86% reduction in its GHG emissions and ensuring the full adaptation of its ecosystems, its population, its territories and its production sectors to climate hazards.

KEY MESSAGES OF NDC3.0 2025-2035

Mauritania’s total GHG emissions represent 0,015% of global emissions. The largest contribution to these total emissions comes from Land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF), livestock and energy. These three sectors account for 99% of emissions.

The “business as usual” baseline scenario, calculated on the basis of SCAPP growth data, shows that direct greenhouse gas emissions, expressed in terms of Global Warming Potential (GWP/5th IPCC report), range overall from 8497,01 Gg Eq CO2 in 2015 to 11 951,646 in 2022, i.e. an increase of 37,4% (Source NIR/BTR1).

Mauritania belongs to one of the regions of the world most vulnerable to climate change, whose effects are already affecting all sectors of its economy, its ecosystems and its populations, in particular women and children.

Mauritania is fully committed to the implementation of the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement in order to contribute to global efforts to reduce overall GHG emissions by making available to the global community all the mitigation potential that the country has. This potential consists of the enormous reserve of clean energy production, wind and solar.

Thus, Mauritania’s NDC3.0 provides for an economy-wide net reduction in GHG emissions of 8,1% (i.e. 1 872,77 Gg CO2eq) in 2035 compared to emission trends up to 2022. This unconditional target will be achieved with the country’s own means supported by international assistance comparable to that received up to 2025. With additional support, the country could ensure a net reduction of around 75,8% (i.e. 17 433,84 Gg Eq CO2eq) in 2035, i.e. a conditional scenario of around 67,7% (i.e. 15 561,07 Gg CO2eq).

Mauritania’s mitigation efforts are estimated at 12 103,62 MUSD, of which 13% (1529,42 MUSD) is unconditional and 87% (10574,19 MUSD) conditional on the mobilisation of external resources.

In view of its extreme vulnerability, Mauritania has broadened its adaptation ambition to cover the following areas: protection and conservation of ecosystems including wetlands, sustainable rangeland management, biodiversity conservation, fisheries and aquaculture, housing and urban planning, agriculture and food security, health, water, coastal management, prevention of extreme climate events, infrastructure and education.

Consideration of human mobility will be strengthened through integrated actions that will take into account the interactions between climate change, population displacement and human security. These actions will aim to anticipate and reduce the risks associated with climate migration, to protect vulnerable communities, and to strengthen the resilience of displaced populations and host territories, while promoting sustainable and inclusive solutions for human mobility.

Particular attention is paid to genetic improvement (breeds and seeds). This broadening is based on the Green Climate Fund readiness programme (Readiness) and on the results of the first studies carried out as part of the process of developing the country’s National Adaptation Programme (NAP).

The financing needs for the implementation of NDC3.0 are estimated at 18 088,33 million USD, of which 2 445,48 million USD is unconditional financing, ensuring the implementation of priority actions independently of external contributions. The remaining 15 642,85 million USD is conditional and will depend on the mobilisation of international resources, partnerships and climate finance mechanisms. This financing will make it possible to support adaptation and mitigation measures, strengthen the resilience of populations and ecosystems, and promote inclusive and sustainable low-carbon development.

In addition to the conditional financing required to secure the country’s mitigation ambitions and implement its adaptation actions, the implementation of Mauritania’s 2025-2035 NDC requires support in terms of capacity building for all development actors, integration of cross-cutting aspects (gender, youth, employment, education and human mobility) and the operationalisation of a Measurement, Reporting and Verification system to ensure the monitoring and evaluation of climate action.

By harmonising its development process with that of the SCAPP and building on the country’s sectoral strategies and programmes, NDC3.0 defines the framework of the country’s climate policy up to 2030. It offers a framework for consultation and dialogue for all stakeholders to define transformative, integrated, inclusive, clean and sustainable programmes.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Mauritania, one of the Sahelian countries most vulnerable to the repeated droughts since 1968, faces major environmental challenges. Desertification, exacerbated by climate change and human impact, seriously affects an already fragile environment, and the key sectors of the national economy suffer heavy consequences. By ratifying the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), as well as the Paris Agreement, the country has firmly committed to limiting its greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and to adopting adaptation strategies compatible with its sustainable development objectives. After submitting its first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) in 2015 and its update in 2021, Mauritania is continuing its efforts with the development of NDC3.0 in 2025, within the framework of the National Strategy for Accelerated Growth and Shared Prosperity (SCAPP). This updating process aims to integrate climate resilience into all of the country’s development sectors, while ensuring consistency with the priorities defined in the SCAPP and following the five-year NDC update cycle promoted by the Paris Agreement.

Thus, NDC3.0 presents not only the country’s overall climate ambitions, but also specific mitigation and adaptation objectives, as well as the framework and implementation mechanisms to ensure the effectiveness of these actions.

Mitigation ambition of NDC3.0

Through its NDC3.0, Mauritania commits to a reduction of 75,8% (i.e. 17 433,84 Gg CO2eq) in 2035 compared to the reference level (BAU), including an unconditional reduction of 8,1 % (i.e. 1 873 Gg CO2eq) and a conditional reduction of 67,7% (i.e. 15 561 Gg CO2eq).

The mitigation ambition covers the five emission sectors (Energy, Agriculture, LULUCF, IPPU and Waste). The national effort to increase the share of renewables in the energy mix will focus mainly on exploiting the high renewable energy potential, aiming to install more than 13 GW by 2035. Another fundamental pillar for achieving this target is the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector, which will be strengthened by ambitious forestry projects (such as the Great Green Wall and assisted forest regeneration), as well as by agroecology initiatives and improved livestock practices.

In total, 63 mitigation measures are included in NDC3.0 (refer to Annex 2), comprising 39 unconditional actions and 24 conditional actions.

Capacity-building needs and implementation support

In addition to the financial support expressed as conditional support for mitigation measures and adaptation actions, the 2025-2035 NDC defines needs in terms of capacity building, technology transfer and training, notably for the education of young people.

In terms of capacity building, the establishment of an operational measurement, verification and reporting (MRV) system is a priority action in the process of implementing the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement. The establishment of this system is mandatory for the country In addition, and although not binding for the country, the development of an action plan for the implementation of the NDC is a prerequisite for MRV.

Mauritania has already made substantial efforts in this direction. The establishment in 2013 and the institutionalisation of the national network of sectoral climate focal points (PFS) (supported by task forces) and of civil society and private sector Focal Points constituted a first step. In September 2020, the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development (MEDD) consolidated its institutional arrangements by establishing the Directorate of Climate and Green Economy (DCEV) to coordinate the entire national climate change programme. This institutional arrangement is reinforced by a network of independent experts from academia who have contributed to the preparation of all national communications, GHG inventories and biennial update reports, etc. required as part of the reports submitted to the UNFCCC.

Notwithstanding all these achievements, significant progress is needed to promote the collection and processing of the data needed to report transparently and clearly on the implementation of the country’s climate policies, measures and strategies. These needs were identified during the preparation of the project identification document that was submitted to the CBIT (Capacity Building Initiative for Transparency) programme of GEF-7 for a total amount of 1263650 $US.

This project will make it possible to define the responsibilities of the NDC actors, in particular the way in which ministries other than the MEDD will participate in the implementation of the NDC and in the operation of the MRV system.

The other capacity-building priorities for the implementation of the NDC concern the continuation of readiness efforts (Readiness) of sectoral departments, the private sector and civil society. They also concern the integration of cross-cutting dimensions into climate programmes and projects. Thus, the integration of gender, youth, human rights and human mobility dimensions is planned in all projects and programmes developed within the framework of the NDC, by reserving for it at least a 10% share of the budget of each programme or project (Estimate based on the assessment of the Gender/youth/employment/education expert group and of the expert working group drawing on the Doha work programme as well as Article 12 of the Paris Agreement). Similarly, the aspects of job creation and improvement of educational curricula to address the challenges of climate change are considered among the priorities for support to the implementation of the NDC.

Technology needs assessments were carried out in 2017. They will need to be updated in light of the new mitigation and adaptation options adopted in NDC3.0.

I. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

A Non-Annex 1 Party to the UNFCCC, Mauritania belongs to the zone of the African Sahel most affected by recurrent droughts since 1968. The resulting desertification is all the more severe as the effect of the climate, combined with human action, has direct consequences on the population and ecosystems. The country’s vulnerability to climate change affects all the vital sectors of the national economy.

By ratifying the UNFCCC, Mauritania resolutely committed itself to the global process of combating global warming by limiting GHG emissions and establishing adaptation strategies compatible with its sustainable development policy. It reaffirmed this commitment by ratifying the Paris Agreement and had submitted its first Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) in 2015. Although the country’s emissions constitute barely 0,015% of global emissions, Mauritania commits through its NDC to participate fully in the effort of the international community, by making available, on a conditional basis, a mitigation potential of approximately 33,56 million tonnes Eq-CO2, i.e. 22,3% compared to the emissions projected for the same year, according to the baseline scenario (business as usual), over the period 2025-2035.

The WMO report1 states that if the first round of NDCs were not fully implemented this would lead to warming of 2,9 to 3,4 degrees C over the century. Increased mitigation ambition is therefore essential to achieve the goal of the Paris Agreement to limit warming to well below 2 degrees C, i.e. 1,5 degrees C. In this regard, Article 4 of the Paris Agreement, as well as decisions 1/CP.19, 1/CP.20 and 1/CP.21 of the UNFCCC, establish a five-year NDC update cycle that must represent a progression beyond the previous nationally determined contribution and reflect its highest possible ambition.

At the global level, updating the NDC is an opportunity for the international community to improve adaptation planning and strengthen countries' commitment and transparency regarding the mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions in order to achieve the goals set by the Paris Agreement and limit the rise in global temperature to +1.5 C° by 2050, compared with the pre-industrial era.

Thus, the 2025-2035 NDC reflects the efforts made by Mauritania to contribute to the progression over time of global efforts while recognising its need for multifaceted support (capacity building, access to technologies and financing) for their effective implementation.

Indeed, Mauritania aims to undertake a more significant greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions mitigation contribution than that of its previous and first NDC. Likewise, the country intends to strengthen its climate change adaptation efforts to build the resilience of its populations, ecosystems and infrastructure most vulnerable to climate hazards. This is in accordance with its responsibility and in full harmony with the objectives of its SCAPP, which constitute Mauritania's strategic development vision for the period 2015-2030 and serves as the reference framework for all development actions undertaken by the State, public and socio-professional bodies as well as Technical and Financial Partners (PTF) over the period 2015-2030. It incorporates the 2030 Agenda and the targets classified as priorities for the country among the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) as well as the African Union's Agenda 2063.

At the national level, updating NDC 2.0 is an opportunity for Mauritania to strengthen the integration of climate change into the country's development policies and strategies by drawing lessons from the implementation of its two previous NDCs.

While the SCAPP constitutes Mauritania's strategic development vision for the period 2015-2030 and serves as the reference framework for all development actions undertaken by the State, public and socio-professional bodies as well as technical and financial partners (PTF), the NDC serves as the framework for defining the country's climate policy and as the instrument for its implementation. Like the SCAPP, NDC3.0 incorporates the United Nations 2030 Agenda and the targets classified as priorities for the country among the sustainable development goals (SDGs) as well as the orientations of the African Union's Agenda 2063. It derives the legitimacy of the mitigation ambitions it advocates and the adaptation actions it defines from the sectoral and thematic strategies from which they stem. It serves as an instrument of coordination between sectors and as a basis for technical and financial partners (PTF) to plan the integration of climate issues into their strategic framework for intervention in the country and into the programmes and projects they support there. In addition to national efforts, the NDC3.0 process also aims to strengthen the crucial role of decentralised local authorities (CTD) in the implementation of the NDC.

1.2 NDC update process: stakeholder engagement

During the preparation of the initial NDC, the country was preparing a transition in its strategic vision, moving from the Strategic Framework for the Fight against Poverty CSLP 2001-2015 to the new Strategy for Accelerated Growth and Shared Prosperity SCAP (2015-2020).

For the NDC update, the results of the review of the first five-year period of SCAP implementation led to a change in priorities. In this context, the AFOLU sector, as the country's leading source of emissions, had been classified as a priority in the country's ambition, with an overestimation of its potential. The five-year review of the SCAPP highlighted this weakness. Indeed, the energy sector, which was considered the second driver of the country's mitigation ambition, has experienced a boom and its potential, particularly in renewable energy, covers the national commitment in the country's ambition or even more. With the new vision and in line with sectoral strategies, Mauritania is counting on making the most of its potential and its own resources while respecting the United Nations sustainable development goals.

The NDC2.0 update process was launched in July 2025. This process was participatory, inclusive and transparent, with the active participation of all of Mauritania's sectoral ministries as well as representatives of the private sector and civil society, brought together in the ad hoc steering committee (CoPil). This committee, chaired by the Secretary General of the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development, ensures the coordination and monitoring of all stages of the update. It oversees the mobilisation and involvement of stakeholders, the technical validation of deliverables, as well as the review and validation of the technical proposals and policy orientations of the NDC, ensuring their coherence, their feasibility and a level of ambition suited to the goals of the Paris Agreement. The Committee is supported by a technical sub-committee which ensures operational coordination and day-to-day monitoring of the experts' work, guaranteeing the quality of data and deliverables before their submission to the main committee. This organisational framework enabled genuine multisectoral engagement and broad ownership of the NDC 2.0 update process in Mauritania.

The integration of the coordinators of key processes such as the NAP, the BTR, the National Communication, and the CBIT within the Technical Sub-Committee is a major strategic opportunity. This approach ensures coherence between the NDC's mitigation and adaptation ambition, the tracking of climate finance, and the establishment of a robust national transparency system, in line with the Enhanced Transparency Framework of the Paris Agreement. The overlap in data needs and capacity building between these different national reports is thus managed synergistically, avoiding duplication of effort, ensuring the quality of information, and optimising the effectiveness of the planning and financing of climate actions in Mauritania. The memorandum relating to the establishment of this committee is available in annex 5.

The NDC2.0 update process also benefited from the direct support of several agencies of the United Nations System (SNU) in Mauritania, notably the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and the International Organization for Migration (IOM).

UNDP financed Mauritania's NDC 2.0 update process and contributed to the mobilisation of the national firm EKS sderconsult as well as an international consultant to conduct this process. As lead agency, UNDP also encouraged the mobilisation of all United Nations System partners so that they would participate actively in this update.

IOM supported the NDC 2.0 update process by mobilising an international consultant embedded within the MEDD, in order to provide direct technical assistance for the development of NDC3.0. Subsequently, this approach aims to strengthen national capacities in international negotiations on the occasion of COP30, by facilitating the mobilisation of resources and the establishment of strategic partnerships essential to the implementation of NDC3.0 priorities. Furthermore, thanks to this assistance, IOM contributed to a better integration of the human mobility dimension in the country's new NDC, a significant improvement compared with previous versions.

UNEP and the NDC Partnership, which are supporting Mauritania in the parallel process of assessing the implementation of NDC2.0, also participated actively in updating NDC3.0, thus ensuring synergy between the two processes.

UNICEF and UNHCR also participated actively in the update process, each in its specific area of intervention. UNICEF contributed to the gender and youth dimension, notably through the mobilisation and conduct of targeted consultations with young people. For its part, UNHCR, together with IOM, enriched the discussions on the issues of taking refugees into account as a vulnerable group in the adaptation priorities defined in the area of human mobility.

The RCC Lomé also provided its support and advice throughout the process, in order to ensure rigorous follow-up of the guidance of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) in the context of the NDC update.

The main phases and flagship activities carried out during the update process are as follows:

  • 21 - 30 July: setting up of the UNDP consultant teams and start of data collection from the sectors and preparation of the official launch workshop for the NDC2.0 update
  • 1st - 26 August 2025: Data collection from sectors, civil society and the private sector and deferred official launch of the NDC2.0 update process by the MEDD and the UNDP RR;
  • 1-7 September 2025: Conduct of a 2nd series of sectoral consultations to determine the state of progress of the mitigation actions and adaptation objectives included in NDC 2.0 and the main changes to be made to this NDC 2.0;
  • 8 - 15 September 2025: drafting and readjustment process with the sectors, with the DCEV/MEDD and UNDP;
  • 17 September 2025: Sending of the draft report of the updated NDC2.0 to the National Network of Climate Change Focal Points for review and validation;
  • 21 September to 5 October 2025: Technical validation workshop of NDC3.0 and National Steering Committee (CoPil);
  • 08 October 2025: Submission of the consolidated and revised version of the NDC3.0 document and finalisation of the document with a view to its submission to the UNFCCC secretariat.

1.3 Institutional arrangements for the implementation of national climate policy

Mauritania has consolidated its institutional arrangements for climate action by relying on a multi-level governance framework. At the top, the National Council for Environment and Sustainable Development (CNEDD), supported by its regional branches, the Regional Committees for Environment and Sustainable Development (CREDD), constitutes the high-level strategic body. The CNEDD plays a key role as a consultative and decision-making body, responsible for promoting sustainable development and validating major national orientations, thus ensuring political and cross-sectoral integration. The CREDDs, for their part, ensure the ownership and decentralisation of climate action at the regional and local level.

At the operational level, and to consolidate the UNFCCC transparency requirements, the Directorate of Climate and Green Economy (DCEV) was established in September 2020 within the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development (MEDD) in order to coordinate the entire national climate change programme. The DCEV steers this mechanism by relying on a robust Task Force made up of Sectoral Focal Points (PFS) designated by the ministries, civil society and private sector institutions, forming a network of 38 members. For the update of NDC 2.0 in 2025, the MEDD also set up a Steering Committee chaired by its Secretary General, ensuring the supervision and cross-sectoral validation of the work. This Committee relies on sectoral working groups (sectoral task forces) within key ministries, which have benefited from capacity building for active participation and effective steering of climate action, led by the Sectoral Focal Point at the ministerial level and the DCEV at the national level.

II. NATIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES

2.1 Environmental context

Mauritania is a coastal country in north-west Africa, located between northern latitudes 15 to 27 degrees and western longitudes 5 and 17 degrees, with a total area of 1 030 700 km2.

Since 2015, the country has had 15 regions or Wilayas. These 15 wilayas are in turn subdivided into Moughataa (i.e. departments) and the latter into 218 communes.

The country is divided into four agro-ecological zones and production systems.

  • The Sahara in the north, covering a large part of the territory, is the mining zone in its northern part and pastoral in the far north; it is arid and suitable for date palm cultivation in the oases at the foot of the escarpments in its southern part
  • The South-East, of the Sahelian agropastoral type, is characterised along the Malian border by plains rich in herbaceous pastures and large pastoral reserves where an ocean of sand begins, dotted with dunes, interrupted in places by a series of escarpments oriented towards the south-west; these dunes generally increase in size and mobility from east to west and this is where extensive and transhumant livestock farming prevails. It is in this same zone that rainfed crops are developed together with crops behind dams and in lowlands during the rainy season;
  • The Senegal River valley zone in the far south is sedentary and agrarian; this is where the natural and improved flood-recession production systems, or water-controlled or irrigated systems, are found; alluvial lands along the banks of the Senegal River, these plains are rich in clay and buffered by a belt of natural vegetation, including patches of tropical forest, and contain the majority (1 360 km2) of the country's irrigated land for commercial or family agriculture.

3.2 Lessons learned

The analysis of the implementation of NDC 2.0 makes it possible to draw several essential lessons to guide the update of NDC3.0:

1. Strengthening coordination bodies and institutional capacities

  • The revitalisation of environmental coordination bodies is necessary to ensure effective governance of climate and the green economy.
  • Strengthening the skills of the Directorate of Climate and Green Economy, the structure responsible for the secretariat of the CTEDD and national focal point of the UNFCCC, is crucial to ensure effective coordination and monitoring of climate actions at all levels.

2. Establishment of an operational monitoring and evaluation mechanism

  • NDC 2.0 highlighted the need to institutionalise a monitoring and evaluation system equipped with adequate human and material resources.
  • Such a mechanism will make it possible to regularly measure the progress of climate actions, identify obstacles and adjust interventions for greater effectiveness.

3. Natural resource inventories and environmental monitoring

  • Carrying out comprehensive inventories of natural resources (soils, water, forests, rangelands…) in priority areas is essential for objective planning based on reliable data.
  • This work must be complemented by a modernised environmental monitoring system using new technologies such as remote sensing, geographic information systems (GIS) and digitalisation platforms.
  • This monitoring will make it possible to assess changes in the environmental situation and to guide adaptation and mitigation actions effectively.

4. Capacity building, technology transfer and awareness-raising

  • NDC 2.0 showed that the mobilisation of sectoral departments depends heavily on capacity building, the transfer of appropriate technologies and awareness-raising.
  • Strengthening these aspects is the best strategy for systematically integrating the fight against the impacts of climate change into sectoral planning and for ensuring local ownership of climate actions.

In summary, these lessons underline the need for strengthened governance, effective monitoring systems, reliable environmental data and increased capacities for all actors, in order to ensure that NDC3.0 rests on solid, operational foundations oriented towards concrete results.

V. ADAPTATION COMPONENT OF NDC3.0

5.1 Vulnerability to climate change

Mauritania’s population, ecosystems and territories are all affected by climate change. The impacts of climate change particularly affect vital sectors of the national economy such as water resources, crop production and livestock farming, the coastal economy, natural ecosystems, health and the living conditions of the population.

The main climatic phenomena affecting Mauritanian ecosystems are rising temperatures, drought, sandstorms, floods, sea-level rise and strong winds.

We summarise below the vulnerabilities of these different territories and sectors.

5.1.1. Vulnerability of territories, ecosystems and biodiversity.

Vulnerability of the Mauritanian Sahara

The Saharan zone is the driest region and covers most of the country.

Its current vulnerability would be amplified by climate change, which exposes it to problems of wind erosion and shifting sands and sometimes to floods caused by torrents that can occur in wadi beds during extreme rainfall events.

Vulnerability of the Sahelian zone

The Sahelian zone constitutes the transition between the arid zone and the Senegal River zone. It is richer in biological resources than the arid zone.

The impacts of climate change on the Sahelian zone result in significant land degradation and erosion. The main challenges to be addressed in this zone are:

  • Protecting rangelands against fires;
  • Agricultural development that takes soil protection into account;
  • Protecting socio-economic infrastructure against sand encroachment;
  • Measures against deforestation for firewood and charcoal; and
  • Protecting wildlife against illegal hunting and poaching.
Lakes and Marshes Biome

Mauritania’s lakes and marshes, particularly those near coastal areas and estuaries, are vulnerable to changes in rainfall patterns and to sea-level rise, which lead to flooding or periods of drought.

The marshes and lakes are suffering a loss of biodiversity, particularly for aquatic species and migratory birds that depend on these ecosystems.

Communities using these areas for fishing, agriculture and the collection of non-timber forest products suffer the consequences of resource losses and must adapt to the reduction in the quantity and quality of water and in the availability of habitats.

Desert Biome

The Mauritanian desert is characterised by extreme temperatures and low rainfall. Global warming exacerbates these conditions, increasing the frequency and intensity of heatwaves and of prolonged drought periods and environmental degradation, leading to land desertification. The increased frequency of sandstorms and violent winds could accelerate erosion and the loss of fertile soil.

Nomadic and pastoral communities that depend on vegetation and water points for livestock and crops suffer severe impacts, including livestock losses, declining agricultural productivity and forced migration

The Senegal River valley has a Sahelian climate, with the particularity of hosting the country's only permanent watercourse: the Senegal River and its tributaries. It has agricultural potential thanks to the availability of water and the fertile soils of the Chemama.

This zone is highly vulnerable given the impact of climate change, and in particular of rising temperatures, on crops and food

The main challenges are:

  • The protection and regeneration of forests;
  • The protection of agriculture in the floodplains against erosion and stray livestock.
River Biome (Senegal River)

The Senegal River, the main source of water for a large part of the country, is particularly vulnerable to changes in rainfall patterns. Prolonged droughts or floods can affect the river's flow.

River ecosystems and the species that depend on them (fish, birds) are threatened by changes in water quality and quantity.

Farmers who depend on irrigation from the Senegal River for their crops face water shortages, land salinisation and declining agricultural yields. Communities using these areas for fishing, agriculture and the collection of non-timber forest products suffer resource losses and must adapt to reduced water quality and habitat availability.

According to the exposure index, the most significant hazards are: rising temperature, drought, rainfall variability, sea level rise and changes in wind patterns

Forest Biome (Sahelian forests and bush)

Mauritania's Sahelian forests are subject to intensifying drought, which compromises their regeneration. Deforestation and prolonged droughts harm the plant and animal species of these ecosystems. Some tree and animal species may disappear because of extreme climatic conditions.

Rural communities that depend on harvesting wood, livestock fodder and non-timber forest products see their way of life disrupted by the reduction in forest cover, soil degradation and loss of resources

Vulnerability of Marine Ecosystems, the Coastline and Protected Areas

Coastal ecosystems are particularly sensitive to pollution, climate change and the unsustainable exploitation of resources. The increase in fishing and oil exploration activities heightens these threats.

Marine and Coastal Biome (Coastal zone and maritime areas)

Mauritania's coastal areas, notably around the Parc National du Banc d'Arguin, are vulnerable to sea level rise, leading to coastal erosion and the salinisation of agricultural land and natural habitats.

Storms and marine heatwaves alter the conditions of marine ecosystems. Rising sea water temperatures affect marine biodiversity, notably seagrass beds, fish shoals, and sensitive ecosystems such as mangroves. Water acidification threatens shellfish, corals and marine organisms. Coral reefs, mangroves and seagrass beds, which are essential for biodiversity and fisheries, are vulnerable to rising ocean temperatures are at risk of disappearing.

Fishing-dependent communities suffer declines in fish stocks, degradation of coastal infrastructure (ports, housing) and the loss of agricultural land due to salinisation. These impacts can lead to significant economic losses.

Estuaries and Wetlands Biome (Parc National de Diawling, Banc d'Arguin, etc.)

Estuaries and wetlands, already sensitive to variations in water level, are particularly vulnerable to sea level rise and saltwater intrusion. Changes in rainfall patterns and increased evaporation affect river flows, which alters water levels in estuaries.

Changing salinity and temperature conditions affect the reproduction of many aquatic species, including migratory fish and birds.

Wetlands and estuaries, which host a diverse fauna (migratory birds, fish, etc.), suffer biodiversity losses due to sea level rise, salinisation and habitat loss.

The populations living in these areas, notably fishers and farmers, suffer from habitat loss and declining agricultural and fisheries yields. Displacements due to flooding or land salinisation are also frequent.

5.1.2. Vulnerability of Agriculture to CC (Agriculture, livestock and forests)

Agricultural production, based on family practices, remains dependent on the climate, which is characterised by high variability. It is considered the sector most vulnerable to the adverse effects of climate change, notably through:

  • Rising temperature;
  • The increased frequency of drought years and;
  • The increased frequency and intensity of exceptional events.

The currently pronounced degradation of natural ecosystems will be aggravated by climate change, leading to a widespread reduction in available natural resources (deforestation, declining soil fertility and changes in hydrological cycles).

The impact of climate change on forests, in addition to anthropogenic pressures (land clearing, fuelwood harvesting, grazing pressure, etc.), takes the form of

  • reduced, or even absent, regeneration,
  • the destruction of wildlife habitats and the spread of diseases, as well as fires
  • the reduction of pastoral areas
  • the reduction of forest cover.

The impacts on society and on farming communities take the form of

  • Rural exodus of populations to other areas more favourable to economic activities,
  • Temporary reductions in food crops, notably sorghum and millet

5.1.3. Vulnerability of water resources to climate change

The impacts of climate change on the water sector, accentuated by several socio-economic, institutional, legislative and political factors, are essentially:

  • Significant water losses in agriculture and in drinking and industrial water distribution networks,
  • Overexploitation of groundwater resources,
  • Pollution of water resources, and
  • Loss of dam capacity through siltation.

Furthermore, one of the consequences of CC on watercourses (the river and its branches, ponds, Tamourt, etc.) is the proliferation of floating plants (water lettuce, hyacinth, typha, etc.), due in particular to the reduced flow velocity of watercourses, the change in their temperature, and the deterioration of water quality.

Ensure water security: Improve access to drinking water for all Mauritanians in order to foster socio-economic development and reduce the risk of waterborne diseases through investments in the construction and management of climate-resilient water infrastructure, notably in the areas of collection, storage, supply, sanitation and irrigation.

5.1.4. Vulnerability of the housing, urban planning and land-use planning sector

In Mauritania, most climate change risks are concentrated in informal settlements. Many towns have sites built on sensitive land such as eroded slopes, low-lying land close to unprotected wadi and river banks, and ocean shores. In addition, these dwellings have structures that do not comply with building codes.

This trend towards sedentarisation has resulted in the fragmentation and multiplication of human settlements. Human settlements located in coastal and low-lying areas, for example, are exposed to the threat of rising sea or river levels and flooding. Each site will experience particular impacts depending on the climate changes actually experienced (higher temperatures or increased rainfall, for example), which vary from one place to another.

5.1.5. Vulnerability of the fisheries sector

The Mauritanian coastal zone is home to the majority of the country's population and has abundant fishery resources, diverse landscapes and natural sites that enable it to offer a very attractive environment.

The Mauritanian coastline, more than 700 km long, is highly vulnerable to climate hazards, notably rising temperature and sea level. The risks to coastal areas are essentially: submersion of low-lying coasts, coastal erosion and salinisation of estuaries and coastal aquifers.

Fisheries being a sector vulnerable to warming, climate hazards and sea level rise. Rising temperature is the cause, among other factors, of changes in the intensity of upwelling, the rise of cold waters responsible for the biological and trophic richness of our waters. It also often causes harmful algal blooms posing risks to human health (toxicity) and marine fauna, with disastrous economic consequences.

Sea level rise has already manifested itself through the increased frequency and strength of swells and their negative impacts on coasts (coastal erosion), fishers' trips at sea, accidents at sea, vegetation and other biotopes in the coastal fringe, and finally, on infrastructure in flood-prone areas.

Furthermore, fisheries are also vulnerable to drought (frequency and length of dry seasons). This vulnerability is attributed to the degradation of coastal and littoral marine vegetation as well as the silting of inland freshwater courses (including the River).

Increased evapotranspiration often leads to the drying up of ponds and non-permanent watercourses and to a decrease in the volume of permanent ones

The visible impacts of climate change on marine, coastal and continental ecosystems are manifested by:

  • A slight deterioration in the state of fishery resources;
  • An increase in the frequency and strength of tides and floods following the general phenomenon of ocean level rise;
  • A change in biodiversity;
  • A southward migration of pelagic fish species with tropical affinity and a northward migration of those with temperate affinities; and
  • A frequency and length of drought seasons on inland fisheries.

5.1.6. Vulnerability of human health

The impacts of climate change on human health, mainly due to rising temperatures, changes in rainfall patterns and extreme events, are aggravated by poverty, the large extent of precarious urban neighbourhoods and the abundance of rudimentary housing . Health vulnerabilities have specific features depending on the country's climate zones (northern zone, coastal zone, zone along the Senegal River, and continental zone in the south-east).

The main impacts are the spread of vector-borne diseases (malaria), waterborne or foodborne diseases linked to extreme weather events (for example diarrhoea and cholera), respiratory diseases, tuberculosis, malnutrition, mental disorders, cardiovascular diseases and deaths linked to these health problems, and the emergence of new vector-borne diseases such as dengue. In addition, extreme climate events have affected health system infrastructure and reduced access to care. Projections under RCP6.0 indicate that mortality linked particularly to heat will rise to about 7 deaths per year per 100.000 inhabitants by 2080. Under RCP2.6, heat-related mortality would rise to 4 deaths per 100.000 people per year by 2080.

Indirectly, climate change affects health in Mauritania through a negative impact on the socio-environmental determinants of health (for example: drinking water, sanitation…etc.).

The projection of the health sector to 2030 shows

  • High maternal, neonatal and infant and child mortality;
  • A high prevalence of communicable diseases and emerging diseases with epidemic potential (meningitis, measles, haemorrhagic fevers, , etc.).

5.1.7. Climate change-human mobility-security

Climate change accentuates the existing socio-economic vulnerabilities of the most exposed populations in Mauritania and is already influencing human mobility dynamics. Historically, mobility – whether pastoral, linked to disasters or conflicts, or driven by the search for economic opportunities – has been an essential adaptation strategy for the survival of communities. For it to contribute fully to the objectives of the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), this mobility must take place in dignified and inclusive conditions.

Mauritania has chosen to integrate human mobility in a cross-cutting manner in its NDC3.0, considering it as a lever for its climate ambitions and its poverty reduction and sustainable development objectives. In the face of major challenges – desertification, recurrent droughts, floods, coastal erosion and heatwaves – climate action must include concrete measures:

  • Strengthen the preparedness and anticipation capacity of frontline communities through early warning systems and participatory data collection mechanisms to guide decisions.
  • Develop nature-based solutions that reconcile the rights of mobile populations and host communities, while regenerating ecosystems.
  • Promote vocational and Community training programmes focused on promoting innovative adaptation mechanisms to diversify livelihoods.

Finally, to fill the data gap on the links between climate, mobility, conflict and security, IOM and its national partners are committed to supporting knowledge production and the integration of these issues into international processes, notably those relating to loss and damage.

Box: Data collection by IOM for human mobility and climate In order to inform policies and strengthen community resilience, IOM is implementing three complementary tools in Mauritania:

  • CVA (Climate Vulnerability Assessment): participatory assessment of climate vulnerabilities in at-risk areas, involving local communities.
  • SAP (Early Warning System): a community-based mechanism for reporting risks of disasters, conflicts and unusual movements.

5.2 Adaptation vision and objectives of the NDC3.0

The general adaptation objective of the NDC3. is to "promote a climate-resilient Mauritania, having strengthened the adaptive capacities of its ecosystems, its population, its economy and its territories in order to ensure inclusive and sustainable territorial and socio-economic development".

Specifically, adaptation aims to

  • Reduce the impacts and decrease the loss and damage caused by climate change, including extreme climate events.
  • Strengthen resilience so as to enable populations, infrastructure and ecosystems to better cope with climate shocks.
  • Integrate the long-term vision and put in place sustainable solutions that are adapted to future climate conditions.

To achieve this objective, the adaptation component of the updated NDC is based on an approach designed as a structuring framework to support Mauritanian actors and their technical and financial partners, both public and private.

In order to strengthen its resilience in all its components, namely food, water, ecological, social, economic, territorial and health resilience, and also resilience to natural disasters, the adaptation component of the NDC is based on an integrated and cross-sectoral approach.

Implementation of the updated NDC is based on a prioritised action plan, with vertical actions that respond to specific sectoral issues and cross-cutting actions to better address cross-sectoral challenges. To this end, the sectoral actions cover the most vulnerable sectors, namely: water resources, agriculture, ecosystems, livestock, fisheries, the coastline and health, taking into account four cross-cutting areas of intervention, namely: gender, education and employment and human mobility.

Mauritania's nationally determined contribution on adaptation involves four main issues: making adaptation smarter, faster, more systemic, and stepping up international action on climate change adaptation.

Adaptation measures are based on solid data and reliable risk assessment tools.

Climate change will have repercussions on the whole of society, so adaptation measures must also be systemic. The new NDC3.0 will thus apply to all sectors of society and the economy (horizontal component), as well as to all levels of governance (vertical component).

5.4 Integration of climate change-related Loss and Damage (L&D)

Mauritania is among the countries most vulnerable to climate change, owing to its arid climate, advanced desertification and the heavy dependence of its economy on agriculture, livestock and fisheries (IMF, 2023a3 ; IMF, 2023b4). According to the World Bank5, hydrometeorological risks — prolonged droughts, flash floods, coastal erosion — have increased over recent decades, affecting agricultural production, infrastructure and public health. The IMF6 estimates that climate shocks cause an average loss of 1 to 2 % of GDP per year, underlining the need for climate-sensitive fiscal policies.

The national climate profile prepared by the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK)7 and the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ) shows that weather extremes are already causing recurrent economic losses, with a projected increase in urban flooding in Nouakchott and in pasture losses in the south of the country8. The same IMF report notes that these climate disasters reduce medium-term growth and increase public financing needs for repairing infrastructure and reviving productive sectors9.

In the Sahel, Mauritania shares regional dynamics of economic losses (agriculture, livestock) and non-economic damage (displacement, biodiversity loss, traditional knowledge), according to the report10 on loss and damage in Africa and SPARC's Bearing the Burden study11. These cumulative effects aggravate poverty, inequality and socio-economic fragility, particularly in pastoral and coastal areas.

The effects of climate change in Mauritania are already resulting in tangible loss and damage in several vital sectors of the economy and of social well-being. These impacts, which affect livelihoods, infrastructure and community cohesion alike, illustrate the growing extent of the country's vulnerability to climate hazards.

  • Agriculture and pastoralism - Repeated droughts have caused a significant reduction in productivity and massive livestock losses12.
  • Infrastructure and loss of livelihoods and human lives - Roads and hydraulic structures are frequently damaged by floods and torrents, generating high repair costs13 in both rural and urban areas. Beyond the damage caused to infrastructure, flood events in recent years have caused loss of human life, which unfortunately is not documented or centralised.
  • Coastal zones - Nouakchott and the coastal areas face erosion, saline intrusion and sea-level rise, threatening infrastructure and housing14.
  • Health and social cohesion - Increased exposure to heat waves, water-borne diseases and the degradation of natural resources aggravates the vulnerability of populations, causing internal migration to urban or coastal areas in search of livelihoods. These repeated displacements lead to the disintegration of rural communities and the loss of traditional knowledge and local cultural practices closely linked to the territories and to pastoral ways of life (ACPC, 201915 and SPARC, 202216).

Furthermore, the degradation of natural resources, desertification and increasing water scarcity exacerbate tensions over access to land and pastures, leading to population displacement within the country and across borders. These human mobility dynamics, often forced, interact with existing socio-economic fragilities and can heighten the risk of local conflicts, thereby threatening the stability and security of the most vulnerable communities.

This NDC3.0 aims to integrate the management of loss and damage as a cross-cutting pillar of the contribution to adaptation and climate resilience.

The country also aims to promote initiatives that support the integration of loss and damage (L&D) into national climate action, notably.

Integration of the “Loss & Damage” (L&D) mechanism into the national climate governance framework

Create a joint collaboration unit between the DCEV of the MEDD and the Directorate General of Civil Protection (DGPC) of the Ministry of the Interior and Decentralisation (MIDEC), responsible for coordinating initiatives and- monitoring loss and damage and for mobilising resources from international mechanisms, notably the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD), the GCF and the Adaptation Fund).

Integration of Loss & Damage (L&D) into the national monitoring system

Establish a national Loss and Damage Observatory to collect, map and monitor sectoral losses ; strengthen early warning and risk analysis systems.

Capacity building for the integration of loss and damage at national, sectoral and local levels

Strengthen the skills of national, sectoral and local institutions and actors to systematically integrate climate loss and damage into plans and policies. It includes training, the sharing of good practices and the development of assessment and monitoring tools. The objective is to improve planning, coordination and decision-making in order to reduce impacts and increase the resilience of communities.

VI. CROSS-CUTTING PRIORITIES

6.1 Synergy with the various strategies and the SDGs

The preparation of Mauritania's NDC3.0 is part of an approach of strong integration with the entire national framework for sustainable development planning. Drawing on the country's sectoral strategies and programmes, the NDC3.0 defines a climate policy framework consistent with national sustainable development priorities to 2030, aiming at transformative, integrated, inclusive and low-carbon development programmes.

Alignment with Sustainable Development and the SDGs

The mitigation and adaptation actions of the NDC3.0 are designed to be an essential lever in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), as they contribute directly to the sustainable management of resources and to improving socio-economic resilience. The emphasis placed on integrating cross-cutting aspects such as gender, youth, employment and education ensures that climate action benefits all development actors, in full accordance with the spirit of the SDGs.

Close Link with the SCAPP

The synergy is particularly marked with the National Strategy for Accelerated Growth and Shared Prosperity (SCAPP 2016-2030), which constitutes the country's development compass. Harmonising the NDC3.0 preparation process with that of the SCAPP ensures that climate efforts support national growth and prosperity priorities.

This strategic link is made concrete by the fact that the NDC baseline scenario (BAU) is calculated on the basis of the SCAPP growth data. By adopting this approach, the NDC3.0 positions climate ambition (notably the clean, wind and solar energy potential) as a driver of the country's economic and sustainable transition, integrated at the heart of the SCAPP objectives.

Complementarity with the NAP and the PNPRC

The broadening of Mauritania's adaptation ambition in the NDC3.0 is based on a robust vulnerability analysis, drawing on the first results of the studies carried out as part of the process of preparing the National Adaptation Programme (NAP).

This analytical basis enables the NDC to integrate key actions that demonstrate close complementarity with risk and disaster management. The adaptation axes explicitly include the prevention of extreme climate events, thereby establishing a direct operational synergy with the objectives of a National Risk and Disaster Prevention Plan (PNPRC). The integration of these dimensions ensures a comprehensive approach, which combines long-term adaptation (NAP) and immediate shock management (PNPRC), strengthening national resilience to climate hazards.

The co-benefits of the recommended mitigation measures and of the identified adaptation actions, as well as their synergy with the implementation of the SDGs, are identified in the last column of Annex 3.

6.2 Integration of gender and youth

In 2015, Mauritania adopted the National Strategy for Gender Institutionalisation (SNIG) to promote equity between men and women and to create conditions favourable to their fulfilment at the political, economic and social levels. The objective of the SNIG is to ensure the integration of gender into all development sectors of the country in order to guarantee the advancement of women and gender equity.

The country also adopted a new youth strategy 2020-2030, which aims to create conditions favourable to the fulfilment of a youth rooted in national civic values and to its integration into all sectors of social life so that it becomes a genuine actor in sustainable development. The strategy is part of the national decentralisation approach and is geared towards optimising impacts with greater targeting of the most vulnerable.

The NDC intends to strengthen the implementation of the SNIG and the SNJ by ensuring that the gender and youth dimension is taken into account in all the adaptation actions it recommends. In particular, the dimensions of Strengthening the resilience of rural women to the effects of climate change and of Developing community-based approaches to climate change adaptation must form part of any adaptation action undertaken, with a minimum of 10% of the budget allocated to them.

6.3. Education, employment and just transition

The labour market in Mauritania suffers from a reverse structural transformation in which the workforce has shifted towards low-productivity sectors. The sectoral composition of employment has remained relatively unchanged in recent years. According to data from the 2012 and 2017 National Survey on Employment and the Informal Sector in Mauritania (ENESI), the total labour force was approximately 735 000 people in both years. In 2017, 30,4% of the labour force worked in the primary sector, 18.1% in the manufacturing sector, including extractive industries and construction and public works (BTP), and 51.4% in services.13

In view of social and employment vulnerability, the just transition of jobs is taken into account in the NDC3.0.

6.4. Climate change-Human mobility-Security

Climate change in Mauritania exacerbates existing vulnerabilities and profoundly transforms the dynamics of human mobility, which represents a key cross-cutting factor in adaptation and resilience strategies. Indeed, human mobility in Mauritania, influenced by climate change, encompasses pastoral mobility, displacement linked to disasters and conflicts, as well as internal and international migration. These dynamics expose migrants, displaced persons and transit and host communities to increased risks of exploitation, violence and climate vulnerability, particularly in degraded and high-risk areas.

Indeed, while mobility, notably pastoral mobility, has always been an essential adaptation practice for the survival of communities in the face of changing environmental conditions, the increasing scarcity of water resources and pastures, due to desertification and recurrent droughts, is disrupting this traditional mobility. These disruptions force herders to travel longer distances, thereby multiplying the risks of intercommunity conflicts, while affecting agricultural and pastoral activities.

In view of this reality, Mauritania recognises human mobility as a cross-cutting dimension integrated into all the sectoral actions of the NDC3.0, aiming for systematic integration into key sectors such as agriculture, livestock and infrastructure. The envisaged measures target modernisation, nature-based solutions coupled with ambitious vocational and community training programmes, early warning systems and the collection of data related to human mobility (notably based on community perceptions and with the aim of informing UNFCCC processes, including the loss and damage process).

In addition to the concrete measures that would be taken under the NDC3.0, it is imperative (i) to integrate provisions to support displaced persons, refugees and temporarily resident populations working in the sectors of agriculture, water, land-use planning and urban development (ii) to train the ministries of environment, planning, migration and social affairs on the links between climate, human mobility, conflicts and security and to establish joint planning units or working groups (iii) to involve refugees and host communities in the co-design of adaptation and mitigation measures to ensure ownership, social cohesion and the relevance of interventions (iv) to establish institutional coordination and financing mechanisms targeting the climate resilience of displaced and host populations.

Through this cross-cutting approach, Mauritania seeks to protect its vulnerable populations while leveraging climate-sensitive human mobility as a strategic lever for sustainable development, fully integrating climate adaptation into multisectoral planning.

Currently, IOM Mauritania is implementing several strategic climate adaptation and resilience initiatives that are aligned with the objectives of the NDC3.0 and on which the country should capitalise. These are, namely:

  • Climate Change and Migration Data Programme (CCMD), a regional programme in which Mauritania participates and which aims to promote evidence-based migration management and reduce the risks of irregular migration and displacement in the context of climate change.
  • IDF Project, which aims to support the Mauritanian authorities in developing evidence-based policies to address the nexus between human mobility, climate change and conflicts in Mauritania.
  • CERF Project, which aims to provide a humanitarian response and adaptation of communities to the effects of climate change in the Guidimakha region.
  • PBF cross-border project, which supports cross-border investment around water management, climate security and peacebuilding in the border areas of Mauritania, Mali and Senegal.

7.1.4 Financing scheme

Mauritania hopes to adopt a resource mobilisation strategy aimed at financing its climate ambitions through both domestic and external resources.

The operationalisation and transformation of the Fonds d’Intervention de l’Environnement (FIE) into a key instrument for financing climate action in Mauritania will play a central role in mobilising and allocating financial resources to implement policies and measures to combat climate change, in particular by supporting local resilience, adaptation and mitigation initiatives.

The FIE will thus serve to channel domestic and external financing in a coordinated manner, fostering better integration of climate actions into the country’s sustainable development. The objective is also to improve the financial governance of resources earmarked for the environment, by ensuring transparency, efficiency and monitoring of the projects financed in the territories. This transformation aims to strengthen national financial capacity to meet the growing needs for adaptation to climate impacts and emission reductions, in particular for vulnerable populations and key ecosystems. The transformation also envisages providing more support to climate resilience projects led by young people and women and to all resource mobilisation initiatives for local authorities, such as the initiatives supported by the new Regional Climate Change Adaptation Fund (FRACC) 18 set up by the Nouakchott region, a model for localising climate finance at regional level in Mauritania.

Indeed, the decentralisation of climate finance requires increased support for fiscal decentralisation, facilitating municipalities’ access to resources dedicated to climate actions. To this end, it is also important to support regions and municipalities in developing and submitting climate-decentralisation pilot projects to the Ministère Délégué and the MEDD. Furthermore, innovative local financing mechanisms should be explored, such as municipal ecological taxes, territorial resilience funds and local crowdfunding, in order to strengthen the autonomy of local authorities in the implementation of climate actions.

The country also aims to develop a multi-year budgetary framework dedicated to climate, specifically integrated into multi-year budget programming, in particular to meet the unconditional financing needs related to mitigation and adaptation. This programming will ensure the sustainability of public debt while aligning it with national financial capacities.

The country aims to strengthen resource mobilisation from traditional climate action finance partners, notably international financing mechanisms such as the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Adaptation Fund, and the Global Environment Facility (GEF), but also to explore opportunities to mobilise new support through bilateral and multilateral cooperation and also the mobilisation of new decentralised climate financing mechanisms.

Moreover, the mobilisation of climate finance in Mauritania remains limited, particularly with regard to private sector participation. To remedy this situation, the capacities of the national private sector will be strengthened so that it can design investment projects eligible for international climate finance. Furthermore, the national financial system is characterised by a still weak contribution from the banking sector, particularly in rural areas. The tax system, for its part, does not currently have effective mechanisms based on the generation of tax resources dedicated to climate actions.

At the international level, Mauritania intends to make use of the voluntary cooperation mechanisms provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, in particular through carbon market instruments, in order to achieve its mitigation objectives. This will be accompanied by active preparation for the testing and application of pilot carbon pricing schemes, while targeting priority sectors such as renewable energy, energy efficiency, control of industrial emissions, and sustainable waste and forest management.

The recommendations aimed at strengthening the national financial system relate first of all to increased mobilisation of national resources, incentives to encourage the transition of the private sector, support for microfinance and the development of suitable financial products. Capacity building, improved access to credit via guarantee funds, as well as support for fiscal decentralisation and the testing of pilot projects in partnership with government institutions and technical and financial partners, complete these recommendations. Finally, the exploration of innovative local financing mechanisms, such as municipal ecological taxes, territorial resilience funds and crowdfunding, is also encouraged.

The Climate Investment Planning and Mobilisation Framework (CIPMF), which will be developed at a later stage, will present in detail the full strategy for mobilising the additional resources needed to implement the mitigation and adaptation actions of the NDC3.0.

7.2 Institutional and individual capacity-building needs

The capacity-building needs for the implementation of the NDC3.0 are spread across several areas, at both institutional and individual levels, in order to ensure an effective transition towards a greener economy that is more resilient to climate challenges.

These needs include the following:

Capacity building of key actors:

  • Training of local and national actors: It is crucial to offer training to civil servants, municipalities, NGOs and the private sector on essential topics such as climate change mainstreaming, climate project development and climate finance mobilisation. It will also be necessary to strengthen the capacities of regions and local communities in nature-based solutions (NbS) and sustainable natural resource management, and to make reliable climate and environmental data accessible to local actors, in order to support better decision-making in planning and adaptation to climate change. These actions will help strengthen the resilience of communities and promote sustainable management practices at the local level.
  • Training of women and young people: Targeted training should be offered on opportunities linked to the blue and green economy, with particular emphasis on the economic empowerment of women and young people in rural and urban areas.
  • Strengthening technical skills: It is necessary to develop specialised skills in technical fields such as green engineering, agroecology, renewable energy and carbon assessment.
  • Development of community skills: Skills need to be developed at the local level to strengthen ownership of climate projects and strategies.
  • Creation of a centre of excellence: The creation of a centre of excellence will serve as an observatory and an independent scientific advisory tool, thereby strengthening climate research and innovation.

Networking of climate databases:

  • Sharing information: Network the existing databases in the field of the environment and climate change in order to make them accessible and useful for local and national actors.

Institutional capacity building and governance:

  • The country has structures such as the Directorate of Climate and Green Economy within the MEDD, which plays a key role in coordinating climate actions at the national level. Climate focal points have been designated in each sectoral department to ensure an integrated and multi-sectoral approach.
  • Clarification of roles: The operationalisation of the MRV system will make it possible to clarify the responsibilities of actors, in particular ministerial departments other than the MEDD and other state and non-state actors, in the implementation of the NDC3.0.

Specific actions to be strengthened:

  • Involvement of women and young people: The systematic integration of women’s and youth organisations at all levels of development projects and programmes is essential:
  • Sending guidelines to sectoral departments to integrate gender and youth concerns into their strategies.
  • Institutionalisation of the consideration of young people and women with a minimum percentage of financing (10 %) for these groups in development projects.
  • Active involvement of young people and women in the identification, implementation and monitoring and evaluation of projects.
  • Participation of young people in the implementation of the NDC3.0: It is crucial to integrate young people into the committees monitoring the implementation of the NDC. Support structures must be set up to identify and analyse the obstacles encountered by young people.
  • Strengthening awareness-raising, communication and accountability of all stakeholders through the development of awareness programmes; training of journalists on climate information; the creation of a National Network of the environmental press and integrating climate issues into government messages; the mobilisation of cultural and media spaces (cultural centres, libraries, festivals, regional radio stations) in the service of climate awareness; the creation of a parliamentary Network “Culture and Environment” to strengthen legislation and oversight in the climate field; the organisation of information sessions for parliamentarians, elected officials and journalists on the contents and stakes of the NDC3.0.
  • Integration of climate change at all levels: It is essential to systematically integrate climate change issues into the Municipal Development Plans (PDC) and Regional Development Plans (PDR). This requires improved collaboration between all stakeholders at all levels, as well as strengthened climate governance, by clarifying the roles and responsibilities of ministries, local authorities and specialised institutions. In parallel, it is crucial to align sectoral investment plans with climate priorities, particularly targeting the areas and groups most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change.

Structuring programmes and projects:

  • strengthening national and local capacities in the development, design and management of structuring climate projects covering all mitigation and adaptation sectors. This strengthening should enable better identification, formulation and implementation of integrated projects, while maximising socio-economic development co-benefits, such as the creation of green jobs, poverty reduction, improved food security and the promotion of social and gender equity.
  • creation of synergies with structuring projects such as the Innovative Transformative Programme (PTI 2026-2030), for the Great Green Wall, bringing together several key projects aimed at restoring land and wetlands, protecting infrastructure against sand encroachment, securing pastoral routes, and sustainably managing natural resources and biodiversity. It also supports initiatives in renewable energy, resilient infrastructure and the carbon market, in order to strengthen climate resilience, improve food security, create green jobs and reduce poverty sustainably.

7.3 Technology transfer needs

Technology transfer plays an essential role in the implementation of Mauritania’s NDC3.0, in particular to accelerate the transition towards sustainable practices that are resilient to climate challenges. The development of new technologies and their transfer, at both institutional and local levels, not only make it possible to support climate change adaptation and mitigation, but also to improve the capacities of local actors to manage the impacts of climate change. The technology transfer needs identified in several specific areas are as follows:

1. Strengthening environmental information systems:

  • Environmental Information System (SIE): The SIE must be strengthened in order to improve its functionalities, in particular those relating to Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and the display of changes in environmental indicators across the national territory. It is recommended that these systems be strengthened to make them an effective tool for managing the environment and natural resources.
  • Climate information systems: The country needs to strengthen climate information systems, in particular for meteorological data and environmental monitoring. These systems are essential for better planning and effective management of climate impacts.

2. Introduction of clean and resilient technologies:

  • Efficient irrigation technologies: The introduction of suitable technologies for efficient irrigation in the agricultural and water sectors is a priority, in order to better manage water resources, particularly during periods of drought.
  • Technologies for sustainable construction: The promotion and adoption of sustainable construction technologies, adapted to Mauritania’s climatic conditions, are necessary to reduce the carbon footprint of the building sector.

3. Development of renewable energy:

  • Renewable energy: The development of technologies in the renewable energy sector is crucial, in particular:
  • Wind (onshore and offshore);
  • Photovoltaics;
  • Concentrated solar power (CSP);
  • Biogas;
  • Green hydrogen.

These technologies will help diversify the country’s energy mix and reduce its dependence on fossil fuels, while meeting sustainable development needs.

4. Technologies for waste management:

  • Waste recovery: It is necessary to develop techniques and practices for the management and recovery of waste, including the conversion of organic waste into biogas, and the promotion of recycling in urban and rural areas.

5. Research and innovation for agricultural adaptation and biodiversity:

  • Adaptation practices for the agricultural sector: Mauritania must participate actively in international research on agricultural practices for adaptation to climate change, in particular for natural ecosystems and biodiversity conservation.
  • Reduction of CH4 emissions from livestock: It is imperative to support research on methods aimed at reducing methane (CH4) emissions from the livestock sector, one of the major contributors to greenhouse gases.

6. Climate-adapted infrastructure:

  • Resilient infrastructure: The development of infrastructure resilient to the impacts of climate change is an urgent need, in particular for the construction of dykes, dams and resilient roads.

7. Strengthening monitoring and evaluation systems:

  • Monitoring and evaluation systems (MRV, M&E): It is necessary to strengthen monitoring and evaluation systems to measure and track progress in the implementation of the NDC3.0.
  • National indicator framework: The establishment of a national framework of adaptation and mitigation indicators is indispensable to assess the impacts of the actions carried out and to adjust public policies according to the results observed.

7.4 Partnership development needs for implementation

7.4.1. Cooperation under Article 6

The Islamic Republic of Mauritania recognises the importance of the cooperation mechanisms provided for by Article 6 of the Paris Agreement as an essential lever for mobilising additional financing for its climate actions. These mechanisms constitute a strategic opportunity to strengthen the implementation of mitigation and adaptation projects, in particular by strengthening the ecosystem of the national carbon market.

With this in mind, Mauritania has undertaken a set of actions aimed at structuring and developing this market, in particular via the Ministry of Energy, within the framework of its green hydrogen programme. This programme is supported by the development of a long-term low-carbon development strategy currently being prepared, awareness-raising and training campaigns for stakeholders on the cooperative approaches provided for by Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, as well as by the establishment of an institutional framework dedicated to the governance of activities related to Article 6 (eligibility criteria, authorisation procedures and modalities for the transfer of ITMOs/carbon credits).

Mauritania also seeks to boost the participation of public and private actors in this market through capacity building, thereby consolidating their engagement in ambitious projects relating to the development of renewable energy (wind, solar, hydropower), green hydrogen, energy efficiency, integrated waste management, sustainable transport, agroforestry, ecosystem restoration (mangroves, wetlands, seagrass beds), and other green infrastructure.

These efforts are part of a transparent and responsible approach, respectful of international requirements, guaranteeing the traceability of transfers and avoiding any double counting, in order to maximise the environmental and socio-economic impact of projects compatible with national sustainable development ambitions.

7.4.2. Cooperation for sustainable development

To ensure the ambitious and effective implementation of its NDC3.0, Mauritania makes cooperation for sustainable development a central pillar of its mobilisation strategy, aimed at meeting critical needs for financing, capacity building and technology transfer. The country is actively committed to strengthening South-South and North-South cooperation, fostering the exchange of experience with neighbouring Sahelian countries and developed nations, while maximising synergies with key regional initiatives such as CILSS, ECOWAS and IGAD.

Through these efforts, Mauritania aims to play a key role in climate security in the Sahel, by tackling head-on the complex climate change-human mobility-security nexus in Mauritania. In this critical area, a strategic partnership will be specifically developed with the International Organization for Migration (IOM). This partnership aims to create synergies, to steer IOM’s ongoing climate projects in Mauritania and to design new joint initiatives that directly support the adaptation and resilience options in the field of human mobility presented in the NDC3.0. Beyond IOM, the effectiveness of the implementation of the NDC3.0 will rely on close collaboration with the other specialised agencies of the United Nations System (UNS) that have supported the NDC process, including UNDP, UNEP, UNICEF, WHO, FAO and UNHCR, ensuring a multisectoral and integrated approach to climate action.

In order to structure this engagement, Mauritania plans to update the sectoral roadmaps for NDC implementation and to promote, in collaboration with sectoral actors, strategic partnership plans for each key sector/area of action. The NDC Partnership remains a key partner for institutional and technical support in this area and its collaboration with Mauritania will be strengthened to this end.

A major strategic focus lies in establishing partnerships with the private sector to stimulate green investment. At the same time, the country insists on the active involvement of civil society and local communities in the implementation and monitoring of the NDC. The development of scientific and technological partnerships is also prioritised to ensure access to climate innovations and resilient solutions.

New partnerships will also be mobilised to strengthen the governance framework for climate action in Mauritania, support the establishment of a country platform on climate action financing, and develop and implement Mauritania’s Climate Investment Planning and Mobilisation Framework (CIPMF). Support will also be mobilised for the preparation of a portfolio of climate projects and the mobilisation of the international climate financing mechanisms established under the UNFCCC, notably the Green Climate Fund (GCF), the Adaptation Fund and the Global Environment Facility (GEF).

VIII. Governance and monitoring and evaluation frameworks

8.1. Governance framework

Mauritania is committed to strengthening its national climate action governance framework with a view to the effective implementation of the NDC3.0. This framework will draw on ongoing processes and capitalise on the experience of the Ad hoc Steering Committee for the update of the NDC2.0 (CoPil) established by the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development (MEDD).

The Directorate of Climate and Green Economy (DCEV) will continue to play the role of focal point and pivot of technical coordination. Having already served as Secretariat of the CoPil and chaired the Technical Sub-Committee (SCT), the DCEV has demonstrated its capacity to manage the operational interface, the work of national and international experts, the facilitation of data collection, and the ex-ante review of technical deliverables.

For the implementation of the NDC3.0, the DCEV will consolidate this function by becoming the permanent executive coordination unit (UCP-CDN), responsible for managing the Measurement, Review and Verification (MRV) system for the implementation of the NDC2.0, for monitoring synergies between the NDC process and the enabling reports (NAP, BTR, National Communication, ADCOM), and will work with the ministries in charge of the budget and finance to ensure the integration of climate objectives into the national budget cycle and the mobilisation of domestic resources for the implementation of the unconditional commitments of the NDC3.0.

The structure of Sectoral Focal Points (PFS) and non-state actors established by the CoPil will be made permanent to form the main body for dialogue and implementation of the NDC3.0. This ad hoc CoPil has already succeeded in bringing together a comprehensive membership, including representatives of almost all ministerial departments, local authorities, the Employers' Federation (Patronat), and key civil society organisations. This multisectoral, multi-stakeholder platform, whose mission was to ensure stakeholder involvement and to validate technical proposals and policy directions, must evolve into a National Platform for Monitoring NDC Implementation.

The Conseil National Environnement Développement Durable (CNEDD), as the central body, will play a strategic and political role in overall governance, ensuring the coordination of environmental policies, their consistency with national sustainable development orientations, and the steering of climate action mechanisms.

The Comités régionaux environnement Développement Durable (CREDD), at regional level, will be institutionalised as key bodies for local implementation, monitoring and evaluation of activities related to the NDC3.0, facilitating the decentralisation of efforts and the mobilisation of local stakeholders.

8.2 Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Framework for the implementation of the NDC3.0

Monitoring and evaluation of the NDC3.0 measures would be established so as to strengthen the transparency of climate action in Mauritania and facilitate the continuous review of the country's contribution to the global goal of climate change mitigation established in the Paris Agreement, and also to report on the status of achievement of the Global Goals on Adaptation (GGA) as set out in paragraph 9 of decision 2/CMA.5 adopted in 2023 in the United Arab Emirates, as well as the decisions and guidance of ongoing work on finalising the GGA indicators.

8.2.1 Monitoring of selected quantitative progress indicators

For more detail: see chapter 1: National context Geographical position Mauritania is a coastal country in north-west Africa, located between northern latitudes 15 to 27 degrees and western longitudes 5 and 17 degrees, with a total area of 1 030 700 km2.

Since 2015 the country has had 15 provinces or Wilayat. These 15 wilayas are in turn subdivided into Moughataat4 (i.e. departments) and the latter into 218 communes.

Physically, Mauritania is characterised by the flatness of its relief, with low altitudes, often below 500 m with the exception of the Kédia d‟Idjil which peaks at 915 m. The landscapes are characterised by the monotony of tabular plateaus and immense stony or sandy expanses. A predominantly desert country, Mauritania has large pastoral areas and only 0,5 % arable land, with a population estimated at 4,2 Million in 2020, of which the urban proportion is greater than the rural one (52,8 %), compared with 48,3 % in 2013.

Mauritania is entirely Saharan in its northern part and Sahelian in its southern part, and is characterised by a generally hot and dry climate marked by relatively mild winters (with average minimum temperatures of 19 to 23°C) and very short rainy seasons (about three months). In the dry season, temperatures exceed the 40°C threshold in almost all regions of the country (with the exception of Dakhlet Nouadhibou).

Climate Saharan in the north and Sahelian in the south, generally hot and dry, mild along the Atlantic Ocean, with four months of rainy season (from June to September) Average annual rainfall: between 500 mm in the South and less than 50 mm in the North of the country.

Climate hazards, delays in structural reforms and regional insecurity add to the risks related to the pandemic.

SUBJECT: ESTABLISHMENT OF THE AD HOC STEERING COMMITTEE FOR THE UPDATE OF THE NDC2.0

An Ad Hoc Steering Committee (Copil) is hereby established, in charge of supervising the process of updating the NDC2.0.

1. Missions of the Steering Committee:

The steering committee ensures the coordination and monitoring of all stages of the update of the NDC2.0. It ensures the mobilization and involvement of stakeholders, as well as the technical validation of deliverables.

As such, the Steering Committee:

  • mobilizes and involves the various designated representatives of the ministerial Departments, the private sector and civil society;
  • examines and validates the technical proposals and policy orientations of the NDC, ensuring their consistency and feasibility;
  • ensures that the NDC reflects an appropriate level of ambition to contribute to the reduction of global emissions in line with the goals of the Paris Agreement.
