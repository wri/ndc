Foreword

Climate change remains one of Namibia’s most pressing development challenges. As one of the driest countries in sub-Saharan Africa, Namibia is increasingly exposed to drought, rising temperatures, variable rainfall, floods and wildfires. These impacts affect water and food security, biodiversity, infrastructure, health and livelihoods, particularly among communities and groups with limited capacity to respond.

It is therefore my honour, on behalf of the Government of the Republic of Namibia, to present Namibia’s Third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) under the Paris Agreement. This contribution builds on the progress and lessons of our previous NDCs and reflects our continued commitment to climate action that supports sustainable and inclusive national development.

Namibia contributes less than 0.03% of global greenhouse gas emissions and therefore remains a net carbon sink. Our climate response must therefore both reduce emissions from a growing economy and protect and strengthen the natural systems that continue to remove carbon from the atmosphere, while building the resilience of our people and productive sectors.

NDC 3.0 strengthens mitigation ambition across energy, agriculture, forestry and land use, industrial processes and waste, while placing adaptation at the centre of the national response.

Priority areas include water and food security, climate-resilient agriculture, biodiversity and ecosystems, fisheries, health, infrastructure, coastal areas and urban settlements. It also places greater emphasis on gender equality and social inclusion.

Our ambition must be matched by implementation. Namibia has committed domestic resources to climate action, but the scale of investment required exceeds what can be mobilised nationally. Approximately 88% of the estimated financing required for NDC 3.0 is conditional on international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity-building support. Strong partnerships with development partners, climate funds, financial institutions and the private sector will therefore be essential.

The preparation of NDC 3.0 has been a collaborative national process involving Government institutions, development partners, the private sector, academia, civil society and other stakeholders. I extend my sincere appreciation to all who contributed their knowledge, time and resources to this process.

As we move from commitment to implementation, our focus must remain on delivery, accountability and partnerships. Climate action must strengthen Namibia’s resilience, protect our natural heritage, create opportunities for our people and support a sustainable economy for present and future generations.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

BACKGROUND Namibia’s Third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) sets out the country’s climate commitments for the period 2026–2035, with 2030 serving as an interim milestone and 2035 as the principal target year. It builds on Namibia’s previous NDCs, lessons from implementation of NDC 2.0, the latest national greenhouse gas inventory and the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake under the Paris Agreement.

Namibia remains one of the world’s most arid and climate-vulnerable countries. Increasing temperatures, prolonged droughts, erratic rainfall, floods and wildfires threaten water security, agriculture, biodiversity, public health, infrastructure and livelihoods. These impacts are particularly significant for communities whose livelihoods depend directly on land, water and other natural resources, and can deepen existing inequalities affecting women, youth, children, older persons, persons with disabilities and poorer households.

At the same time, Namibia makes a very small contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions and remains a net carbon sink, largely because of removals from its land and forest resources. NDC 3.0 therefore follows a dual approach: reducing greenhouse gas emissions while protecting and enhancing the country’s carbon sink, alongside strengthening Namibia’s resilience to the impacts of climate change.

MITIGATION CONTRIBUTION Namibia’s mitigation programme covers the Energy; Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU); Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU); and Waste sectors. Key priorities include expanding renewable energy and energy efficiency; cleaner transport and fuels; sustainable livestock and agricultural production; reducing wildfires; restoring forests and degraded land; increasing soil carbon; reducing clinker use in cement production; improving waste management, recycling and resource recovery; and reducing methane emissions.

By 2035, the measures identified in NDC 3.0 are estimated to deliver approximately 7.158 MtCO₂e in mitigation under the unconditional scenario and 15.608 MtCO₂e under the conditional scenario. The larger conditional contribution depends on access to international climate finance, technology and capacity support. The Energy Sector represents a major component of this transition, including renewable electricity generation, grid modernisation, energy efficiency and cleaner transport. AFOLU remains equally important because Namibia must not only reduce emissions but also maintain the ecosystems and landscapes responsible for its substantial carbon removals.

ADAPTATION AND RESILIENCE Adaptation is central to NDC 3.0. Namibia has identified nine adaptation priority areas comprising 46 measures and 110 actions: Agriculture and Food Security; Water Resources; Terrestrial Biodiversity and Ecosystems; Marine Biodiversity, Fisheries and Aquaculture; Health and Sanitation; Cross-cutting Issues; Infrastructure; Coastal Zones; and Urban Settlements.

These priorities seek to strengthen food and water security, climate-resilient agriculture, water infrastructure and alternative water sources, healthy ecosystems and community-based natural resource management, sustainable fisheries and aquaculture, climate-resilient health systems and settlements, disaster preparedness and resilient infrastructure. Water security receives particular attention through improved water-resource planning and management, riparian restoration, desalination, water infrastructure, rainwater harvesting and water recycling.

NDC 3.0 also recognises that adaptation cannot eliminate all climate impacts. Improved assessment of loss and damage will therefore support disaster risk management, social protection, public finance and future access to climate finance.

AN INCLUSIVE CLIMATE RESPONSE Gender equality and social inclusion are integrated across mitigation and adaptation. NDC 3.0 strengthens the use of disaggregated indicators and seeks greater participation and access to climate-resilient livelihoods, productive resources and decision-making for groups that may be disproportionately affected by climate change. These commitments will be further operationalised through the NDC Implementation Strategy and Action Plan and the adaptation Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning system.

FINANCING AND IMPLEMENTATION Delivering NDC 3.0 will require substantial investment. The current estimated implementation requirement is approximately USD 14.07 billion, comprising about USD 6.42 billion for mitigation and USD 7.65 billion for adaptation. Approximately USD 1.66 billion (12%) is classified as unconditional, while approximately USD 12.42 billion (88%) is conditional on international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity-building support.

Implementation will be coordinated by the Ministry of Environment, Forestry and Tourism in collaboration with sector ministries, the National Planning Commission, the Environmental Investment Fund, regional and local authorities, the private sector, civil society, communities and development partners. An updated NDC Implementation Strategy and Action Plan (NDC-ISAP) will translate the commitments into implementation arrangements, while strengthened measurement, reporting, verification and adaptation monitoring systems will track progress and support transparency.

NDC 3.0 ultimately seeks to ensure that Namibia’s response to climate change supports, rather than constrains, its broader development aspirations. It provides a pathway towards a climate-resilient, low-carbon and inclusive economy, while safeguarding the natural resources and ecosystems on which Namibia’s people and economy depend.

1. INTRODUCTION

Namibia, as a Party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement, hereby communicates its third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0). This contribution represents a progression from Namibia's First NDC (2015) and its NDC 2.0 (submitted in 2021 and updated in 2023), reflecting the country's commitment to the highest possible ambition in light of its common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, and its particular national circumstances as one of the most arid and climate-vulnerable countries in sub-Saharan Africa.

Namibia is, and is projected to remain, a net carbon sink, owing to extensive removals in the Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) sector. The country's NDC 3.0 therefore pursues a dual objective: reducing gross greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions across all sectors while protecting and enhancing the national sink. NDC 3.0 covers all IPCC-defined sectors, Energy, Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU), AFOLU, and Waste, and all greenhouse gases reported in the national inventory (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and HFCs), with a target end-year of 2035.

This NDC 3.0 has been informed by the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake (GST) concluded at COP28 (December 2023), and is grounded in a comprehensive national evidence base, including the NDC 2.0 Stocktake Report and Revision Roadmap (2026), Namibia's first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1) and Fifth National Communication (NC5) submitted in 2024, the National Inventory Document (NID), and the Long-Term Low Emission Development Strategy (LT -LEDS) Vision document. Its preparation has been guided by the NDC Partnership's NDC 3.0 Navigator and the UNDP Quality Assurance Checklist for NDCs 3.0 across the five dimensions of country ownership and inclusivity, ambition, just transition and sustainable development, clarity and transparency, and feasibility.

The NDC is aligned with Namibia's Vision 2030, the National Development Plan (NDP6), and the National Climate Change Policy (2011) and its Strategy and Action Plan, and relevant sectoral policies and legal frameworks.1

1.1. Salient Features of Namibia

  • Namibia covers approximately 825,000 km² and has a population of just over 3.02 million, growing at about 3.0% annually. More than 71% of the population is under 35 years of age, with an almost even urban-rural distribution (49.5/50.5%) (NSA, 2024). Rapid urbanisation is increasing demand for climate-resilient infrastructure, services and disaster risk management.
  • Namibia's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) stood at N$269.8 billion (approximately USD 16.4 billion) in 2025 (NSA, 2026), with real GDP growth of 2.6% in 2026 (BoN, 2026). Sustaining economic growth will increasingly depend on climate-resilient infrastructure, sustainable natural resource management and investment in low-carbon industries.
  • Namibia is one of the world's driest countries, receiving less than 25 mm to over 600 mm of rainfall annually. Water scarcity remains a major constraint to socio-economic development and increases vulnerability to climate change.
  • Namibia's economy remains highly dependent on climate-sensitive natural resources, making it vulnerable to droughts, floods and commodity price fluctuations. NDP6 prioritises economic diversification, value addition and climate-resilient growth.
  • Manufacturing, mining and mineral beneficiation remain central to Namibia's economic transformation agenda. The expansion of value addition, green industrialisation and low-carbon manufacturing presents significant opportunities to decouple industrial growth from greenhouse gas emissions while strengthening economic resilience.
  • Climate change disproportionately affects women and vulnerable groups due to existing socio-economic inequalities. Gender-responsive planning therefore remains central to Namibia's climate change response. Namibia remains one of the world's most unequal countries, with high levels of inequality increasing vulnerability to climate-related risks and reducing adaptive capacity.
  • Bush encroachment affects an estimated 45 million hectares (about 60% of Namibia), reducing rangeland productivity and biodiversity while creating opportunities for sustainable biomass utilisation, ecosystem restoration and climate change mitigation.
  • Namibia remains a net sink of greenhouse gases and is expected to remain so through 2035. Despite contributing less than 0.001% of global greenhouse gas emissions, Namibia is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. - Agriculture supports the livelihoods of more than 70% of the population and directly employs about 21% of the workforce, making it one of the sectors most vulnerable to climate change.
  • Livestock production remains the largest contributor to Namibia's agricultural sector, accounting for approximately 59% of total agricultural output. The sector supports rural livelihoods, food security and export earnings but is highly vulnerable to climate variability, reinforcing the need for climate-resilient rangeland management and sustainable livestock production systems.
  • By the end of 2024, Namibia had 86 registered communal conservancies and 47 community forests, collectively managing more than 185,800 km² (approximately 22% of the country's land area). These community-managed landscapes play a critical role in biodiversity conservation, sustainable natural resource management, ecosystem-based adaptation, and carbon sequestration.
  • Namibia possesses globally competitive renewable energy resources, including high solar irradiation, favourable wind regimes, sustainable biomass resources and significant green hydrogen potential, providing opportunities for green industrialisation and a low-carbon and climate-resilient economy.
  • Namibia has an installed electricity generation capacity of approximately 730– 750 MW, with peak electricity demand exceeding 700 MW and a significant proportion of electricity still imported from the Southern African Power Pool (SAPP). Rapid expansion of renewable energy generation, battery storage and transmission infrastructure is therefore central to achieving energy security and the country's long-term decarbonisation objectives.
  • Namibia hosts globally significant biodiversity and approximately 40% of its terrestrial area is under some form of conservation, providing important ecosystem services that support climate adaptation and carbon sequestration.

2. NDC REVISION PROCESS

2.1. Overview Namibia's NDC 3.0 has been developed through a structured, inclusive and nationally owned process led by the Ministry of Environment, Forestry and Tourism (MEFT) as the national climate change focal point, in coordination with the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), and sectoral line ministries.

The revision was underpinned by dedicated technical workstreams:

i) the NDC 2.0 stocktake assessing implementation progress, gaps and barriers across mitigation and adaptation; (ii) an update of the GHG inventory and Business-as-Usual (BAU) scenario; (iii) revision of measures, actions and Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV)-ready indicators; and (iv) costing and conditionality analysis.

Gender Equality and Social Inclusion (GESI) considerations were integrated and would be further elaborated through the forthcoming National Climate Change Gender Action Plan (NCCGAP). The revised measures will be operationalised through an NDC Implementation Strategy and Action Plan (NDC-ISAP) designating lead ministries, reporting responsibilities and coordinating bodies.

2.2. Lessons Learnt from NDC 2.0

The NDC 2.0 stocktake highlighted several lessons that have directly informed the

  • MRV and indicator design: NDC 2.0 indicators were largely input-based (e.g. installed capacity in MW, units installed) rather than output or outcome-based. NDC 3.0 reformulates indicators towards measurable outcomes (e.g. GWh generated, tCO₂e avoided, litres of fuel displaced), consistent with the UNFCCC Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF).
  • Inventory data consistency and transparency: The NID is consistent and compliant with the MPGs (paragraphs 17 to 58) and a tabular summary of GHG emissions and removals by sector are included in the CRTs, labelling of GWPs and inclusion of standardised sectoral emissions and removals. The NID aligns with the mandatory requirement under the Modalities, Procedures and Guidelines for the Enhanced Transparency Framework (Article 13 of the Paris Agreement), Section E, paragraph 91.
  • Measure-design coherence: Several NDC 2.0 measures aggregated fundamentally different technologies (for example, solar, wind, biomass, hydropower and the Anixas II heavy fuel oil peaking plant under a single renewable-substitution measure). NDC 3.0 separates mitigation actions from enabling or reliability infrastructure to avoid overstating mitigation progress.
  • Realistic, phased ambition: Targets for hydrogen mobility (road, rail and marine) were found to exceed near-term policy, infrastructure and market readiness. NDC 3.0 recalibrates these towards phased, pilot-to-scale pathways while retaining long-term aspirational targets on a conditional basis.
  • Conditionality and finance tracking: NDC 2.0 lacked systematic tracking of conditional versus unconditional actions and of international support received. NDC 3.0 designates the conditionality of each action and provides for tracking of finance, technology and capacity support.
  • GESI and institutional clarity: Gender-disaggregated data and clearly assigned institutional ownership were largely absent. NDC 3.0 embeds GESI-disaggregated indicators (where applicable) and assigns lead and coordinating institutions for each measure.

3. MITIGATION

3.1. Overview Namibia's mitigation contribution is framed by its status as a net carbon sink. The mitigation strategy therefore both reduces gross emissions across the Energy, IPPU, AFOLU and Waste sectors and enhances national removals. NDC 3.0 mitigation actions respond directly to the calls of the first Global Stocktake, including tripling renewable energy capacity, doubling the rate of energy-efficiency improvement, accelerating the transition away from fossil fuels in the energy system, and reducing methane and transport emissions, and are aligned with Namibia's LT-LEDS vision.

Mitigation actions are classified as unconditional (financed from domestic resources) or conditional (dependent on international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building). The mitigation timeframe runs from 2027 to 2035, with 2035 as the target year. This builds on the NDC 2.0 baseline under which Namibia targeted a reduction of projected national emissions of 7.669 Mt CO₂e together with an increase in removals of 4.233 Mt CO₂e, for a total mitigation potential of 11.902 Mt CO₂e and an enhancement of the net sink from the BAU level of −90.713 Mt CO₂e to −102.615 Mt CO₂e by 2030.

3.2. National Emission Profile

Namibia is a net sink of greenhouse gases. Total emissions do not show a clear increasing or decreasing trend over the time series but stayed rather stable at slightly above 20,000 kt CO2e. However, a slight increase of 11% is observed when considering the national emissions of 2022 compared to those of 1990. Removals increased from 89,977 kt CO2e in 1990 to 122,411 kt CO2e in 2022, resulting in an increase of 32,434 kt CO2e (45%) in net removals also for the same period.2

The most recent official emissions data are reported in BTR1 and NC5 (2024) under the Enhanced Transparency Framework, with the National Inventory Document (NID) providing category-level detail. The inventory covers CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and HFCs.

For NDC 3.0, the Business-as-Usual scenario and sectoral emission profile are being updated using 2022 as the most recent inventory year derived from the NID, extending projections to 2035.

3.4. Energy Sector

Namibia's Energy Sector mitigation addresses electricity generation, energy efficiency, transport, household and institutional energy access, agriculture, and marine decarbonisation. The sector comprises 15 measures, delivered through 57 specific actions, and represents the country's principal contribution to national and global emission reduction efforts over the period to 2030 and beyond. These measures target the expansion of renewable electricity generation, grid modernisation, energy efficiency, low-carbon transport, clean household energy, renewable energy in agriculture, and maritime decarbonisation. Together, these measures promote a diversified, secure and low-carbon energy system while supporting Namibia's long-term energy transition. Refer to Annex II.

3.4.1 Renewable Electricity Generation

Renewable electricity generation, comprising utility-scale solar photovoltaic, hydropower, wind and biomass, together with battery storage and grid modernisation, forms the anchor of the sector's mitigation efforts. This capacity is financed predominantly through domestic public revenue, utility balance sheets and existing concessional credit lines, and delivers the majority of the sector's quantified emission reductions. Strategic long-term generation options, including scaled green hydrogen deployment supporting renewable electricity generation and grid stability, extend this ambition into the 2030s, subject to securing climate finance.

3.4.2 Transport Decarbonisation

The transport sub-sector is addressed through a phased and technically sequenced set of measures spanning road, rail, aviation and maritime transport and is elaborated as follows:

  • Road Transportation: Namibia will target 15% of new light-vehicle imports to be electric or hybrid by 2030, supported by expanded charging infrastructure
  • Railways: Heavy-duty freight decarbonisation and rail conversion to green hydrogen proceed through structured pilot phases ahead of any fleet-scale commitment, guided by the outcomes of the Walvis Bay–Windhoek hydrogen-diesel locomotive pilot.
  • Civil Aviation: A dedicated Sustainable Aviation Fuel and synthetic e-fuels measure brings domestic civil aviation into the national GHG inventory, targeting a 50% sustainable fuel blending rate for domestic aviation by 2030.
  • Water-borne Navigation: Marine decarbonisation proceeds through the Phased Marine Decarbonisation Programme, comprising institutional frameworks for green bunkering, demonstration of commercially viable low-carbon marine fuels (including biofuels, hydrogen and ammonia) and fuel-efficiency technologies, and reduction of NamPort's own operational emissions through on-site solar PV and shore-side power (cold ironing) at Walvis Bay and Lüderitz, ahead of any large-scale green hydrogen bunkering and export investment.

3.4.3 Nuclear Energy

Nuclear energy is included as a long-term strategic option, following Cabinet's approval of Namibia's Nuclear Industry Strategy and the establishment of the Nuclear Energy Programme Implementation Organisation (NEPIO). This pathway extends beyond the current NDC's mitigation horizon and is at the institutional and regulatory planning stage, reflecting Namibia's ambition to diversify its long-term low-carbon baseload generation.

3.4.4 Oil and Gas

Namibia's upstream oil and gas sector continues to expand on the strength of major offshore discoveries in the Orange Basin. While upstream production falls outside the scope of this NDC's mitigation accounting, its growth underscores the strategic importance of the Energy Sector's parallel decarbonisation measures, particularly transport fuel diversification, electric and hydrogen vehicle uptake, and sustainable aviation fuel development, in moderating the sector's associated emissions and economic trajectory.

3.4.5 Emerging Green Hydrogen and Green Industrial Developments

Namibia's green hydrogen sector is progressing from early demonstration towards commercial-scale development. Privately led projects are testing applications across green ammonia, fertiliser, green iron, clean transport and renewable baseload power. While not all are presented as specific NDC actions, these developments support Namibia's broader mitigation and green-industrialisation ambitions:

  • Hyphen Hydrogen Energy: Flagship large-scale green hydrogen and ammonia project near Lüderitz, targeting about 2 million tonnes of green ammonia annually. Project preparation is advancing, supported by a USD 10 million AfDB/SEFA facility for FEED and related studies.
  • Zhero Molecules: Proposed large-scale green ammonia development near Walvis Bay. SDG Namibia One committed up to USD 5.15 million in development funding in 2025, supporting feasibility and preparation towards a targeted investment decision.
  • Dâures Green Hydrogen Village: Pilot project progressing towards commercial green ammonia and fertiliser production. Phase 2 proposes 60 MW solar, 10 MW wind and 40 MW electrolysis, targeting commercial operations around 2030.
  • HyIron Oshivela: Operational green-iron demonstration facility using renewable energy and green hydrogen for iron production. The initial phase targets about 15,000 tonnes of direct reduced iron annually, demonstrating opportunities for low-carbon industrialisation and mineral beneficiation.
  • Cleanergy Solutions Namibia: Operational green hydrogen production and refuelling facility near Walvis Bay, demonstrating hydrogen applications in heavy transport, industrial equipment and off-grid power, alongside local skills development.
  • Renewstable Swakopmund: Proposed renewable power facility combining solar generation, hydrogen storage and fuel cells to provide dispatchable renewable electricity. Project implementation is subject to securing a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with NamPower.

These developments demonstrate how Namibia's renewable resources could support industrial decarbonisation, clean fuels, mineral beneficiation, transport and energy security.

The country's development trajectory is reflected in the Green Industrialisation Blueprint and the Sixth National Development Plan (NDP6), which emphasizes economic transformation, employment creation, youth empowerment, environmental sustainability, and value addition. It is therefore critical that Namibia develops a skilled workforce required to support green industrialisation. Failure to do so could result in increased dependence on imported skills, missed employment opportunities for Namibians, reduced local content participation, and diminished economic benefits from green investments.

4. ADAPTATION

4.1. Overview

Adaptation is a central component of Namibia’s NDC 3.0 because the country’s predominantly arid climate, dependence on climate-sensitive natural resources and persistent socio-economic inequalities increase its vulnerability to climate change. Climate-related shocks can deepen existing inequalities, undermine livelihoods and limit the ability of households to meet essential needs, particularly among women, children, youth, older persons, persons with disabilities and poorer communities.

Namibia’s adaptation contribution therefore focuses on reducing vulnerability, strengthening the resilience of communities, ecosystems, infrastructure and productive sectors, and safeguarding national development gains. The adaptation measures and actions presented in this NDC promote inclusive, gender-responsive and locally appropriate interventions that are aligned with national development priorities, sectoral policies and Namibia’s emerging National Adaptation Plan.

4.2. Climate change risks and adaptation priorities

Namibia faces multiple and interlinked climate risks, including increasingly erratic rainfall, prolonged droughts, floods, wildfires and rising temperatures. These risks have direct implications for water availability, agricultural productivity, biodiversi ty, public health, infrastructure and livelihood security.

Drought and rainfall variability are particularly significant because many rural households depend on rain-fed agriculture, livestock production and natural resources for their livelihoods. Floods and wildfires further compound these pressures by damaging infrastructure, disrupting services and degrading ecosystems. Women, youth, children, elderly people, persons with disabilities, and poorer households often have lower adaptive capacity and are therefore more exposed to climate-related shocks.

Namibia recognises that adaptation can substantially reduce climate risks but cannot eliminate them entirely. Residual climate risks may result in economic and non-economic loss and damage, including loss of livelihoods and productive assets, damage to infrastructure, livestock and crop losses, ecosystem degradation, displacement and impacts on human health and well-being.

Strengthening the documentation and assessment of such losses, including their distribution across vulnerable groups and geographic areas, will therefore complement adaptation planning and support disaster risk management, social protection, public finance and access to climate finance.

In response to these risks, Namibia has identified nine (9) adaptation priority areas to reduce vulnerability and strengthen resilience across key sectors and communities. The nine (9) adaptation priority areas comprise 46 measures and 110 actions. These measures and actions align with Namibia’s long-term national and sectoral development strategies, including Vision 2030 and NDP6, as well as relevant sectoral policies, strategies and implementation frameworks. Namibia is also developing its National Adaptation Plan, which will further guide adaptation planning and implementation within the context of the NDC.

Namibia is also developing its National Adaptation Plan (NAP), which will provide the detailed framework for prioritising, costing, implementing, monitoring, evaluating and learning from adaptation interventions. The NAP and its Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) framework will support implementation and tracking of the adaptation priorities under the NDC, while enabling periodic review of progress, emerging climate risks and adaptation needs.

4.2.1. Agriculture and Food Security

Agriculture and food security are critical adaptation priority areas for Namibia because of their central role in sustaining livelihoods, supporting rural economies and enhancing national food resilience. About 70% of Namibia’s population depends on agriculture for their livelihoods, particularly through the livestock and crop value chains. However, Namibia remains a net importer of food commodities, which increases the country’s exposure to food and nutrition insecurity, especially under conditions of climate variability, drought and external market shocks.

Strengthening the agriculture sector is therefore essential for reducing vulnerability and improving resilience. This requires improved access to basic agricultural in puts, including seeds, fertilisers, machinery, irrigation systems, animal feed and veterinary medicines, to increase production, enhance competitiveness and sustain agricultural value chains.

The following are the measures under this priority area:

4.2.2. Water Resources

Namibia is a water-scarce country, and the limited availability of good-quality water remains a major constraint to socio-economic development. Existing water scarcity challenges are expected to intensify under climate change, as rising temperatures, prolonged droughts and variable rainfall further reduce water availability and increase pressure on already stressed water resources. For this reason, water resources management has been identified as a critical adaptation priority area. The following are the measures under this priority area:

  • Develop and Update the IWRM Plan - Develop a National Water Master Plan - Establishment and restoration of riparian buffer - Desalination (sea water and saline groundwater) - Water infrastructure development, maintenance and rehabilitation (including rainwater harvesting) - Water recycling infrastructure

4.2.3. Terrestrial Biodiversity and Ecosystems

Biodiversity and ecosystems are critical adaptation priority areas for Namibia because of their importance to community livelihoods, ecological stability and the national economy.

Namibia’s biodiversity includes the variety of living organisms found across terrestrial, marine and desert ecosystems, as well as the ecological systems of which they form part. Biodiversity management is recognised in Namibia’s Constitution and reinforced by NDP6, which prioritises ecosystem protection, biodiversity conservation and the sustainable use of natural resources.

However, Namibia’s ecosystems are fragile and increasingly vulnerable to climate change, particularly rising temperatures and reduced rainfall. These changes threaten ecosystem services that communities depend on and that contribute significantly to the economy. The following are the measures under this priority area:

Sustainable land management - Management of state protected areas - Environmental management - Fire management plans - Green spaces and urban corridors - Adaptation management of fragile natural habitats (Karoo, Wetlands and - Desert) Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) - Land use planning - Designation of flood areas/high-risk areas and development - free zones - Promotion of landscape-based tourism - Integrating Ecosystem-based Adaptation in Community-Based Natural Re- - sources Management (CBNRM) programme

4.2.4. Marine Biodiversity, Fisheries and Aquaculture

Marine biodiversity, fisheries and aquaculture are important adaptation priority areas for Namibia because of their contribution to employment, export earnings, food security and economic development. 4.2.5. Health and Sanitation

Health is a key adaptation priority for Namibia, as climate change is expected to increase public health risks. Namibia’s population grew from 2.1 million in 2011 to 3.1 million in 2023, with young people making up a large share of the population. NDP6 also notes that the urban and rural population distribution now stands at about 50 percent each, reflecting increasing rural-urban migration.

These trends place pressure on health services, water, sanitation, housing and nutrition. Climate change may further intensify these challenges through heat stress, water scarcity, malnutrition, floods, and increased risks of vector-borne and water-borne diseases.

4.3. Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) for Adaptation

The implementation of Namibia’s adaptation contribution will be supported by a functional Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) system housed within MEFT, as the ministry responsible for coordinating climate change matters.

The system will track progress in implementing adaptation measures and actions, assess their effectiveness in reducing vulnerability and strengthening resilience, and identify implementation gaps, emerging risks and priorities. It will monitor agreed output, outcome and, where feasible, impact indicators, as well as the financial, technological, technical and capacity-building support received and required. Data should be disaggregated, where relevant, by sex, age, disability, geographic location and other vulnerability characteristics.

The adaptation MEL system will be coordinated by MEFT in collaboration with the National Planning Commission, line ministries, regional and local authorities and other implementing institutions.

Implementing entities will be responsible for collecting, verifying and submitting adaptation-related data, including information generated by communities, civil society organisations, research institutions and the private sector. MEFT will consolidate and analyse the information to assess national progress and support reporting on the implementation of the adaptation component of the NDC.

The Namibia Statistics Agency may support the standardisation, management, analysis and archiving of relevant data, where appropriate.

Learning will be integrated throughout the implementation cycle to ensure that monitoring and evaluation findings inform adaptation planning, policy review, resource allocation and implementation. Periodic reviews will assess the relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, inclusiveness and sustainability of adaptation interventions and support the revision of measures, actions, indicators and targets where necessary. This approach will enable Namibia to respond to changing climate risks, incorporate new evidence and lessons, strengthen institutional coordination and progressively improve adaptation outcomes.

5. EQUITY, FAIRNESS AND AMBITION

Namibia considers its NDC 3.0 to be equitable, fair and ambitious. It contributes directly to the ultimate objective of the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement and reflects Namibia's common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities as one of the world's most arid and climate-vulnerable, yet lowest-emitting, countries.

Fairness rests in Namibia's continued voluntary commitment to reduce emissions and enhance removals despite having historically been, and being projected to remain, a net carbon sink through 2035. NDC 3.0 extends this fairness further by broadening the scope of gases and emission categories covered relative to NDC 2.0, and by responding directly to the first Global Stocktake's call for tripled renewable energy capacity, a doubled rate of energy-efficiency improvement, accelerated transition away from fossil fuels, and reduced methane and transport emissions.

Namibia targets to reduce emissions from all sectors under unconditional mitigation measures amounting to 7.15 MtCO2 e by 2035. Under conditional measures, the country targets to reduce emissions by 15.61 MtCO₂e by 2035. The conditional target increased the ambition by 7.94 MtCO₂e compared with the target in the NDC 2 of 7.669 MtCO₂

Equity is operationalised through the differentiation of unconditional actions, financed from domestic resources, and conditional actions requiring international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building, consistent with Article 9 of the Paris Agreement. NDC 3.0 is fully aligned with Vision 2030 and NDP6, under which equity and inclusive economic growth are embedded as core national development pillars alongside environmental sustainability, and is further anchored in Namibia's ongoing National Adaptation Plan process, which guides equitable adaptation planning.

6. NDC TRACKING, MEASUREMENT, REPORTING AND VERIFICATION

The successful implementation of NDC 3.0 rests on a functional Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system enabling Namibia to track and report on mitigation and adaptation progress, including financial, technological and capacity-building support received and needed, in compliance with the Enhanced Transparency Framework under Article 13 of the Paris Agreement and its Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) cycle. The system comprises four components: tracking emissions, mitigation, support and needs, and adaptation, building on Namibia's existing Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) system coordinated by the National Planning Commission.

The NDC 2.0 stocktake identified several MRV weaknesses that have directly shaped the design of NDC 3.0. Indicators, previously largely input-based (e.g. installed capacity, units installed), have been reformulated towards measurable outcomes (e.g. GWh generated, tCO₂e avoided, litres of fuel displaced), consistent with ETF requirements. NDC 3.0 also addresses inconsistencies identified between the BTR and the National Inventory Document, and prioritises reconciliation of sectoral figures, consistent labelling of Global Warming Potentials, and inclusion of a standardised sectoral emissionsand-removals summary table in future inventory submissions.

Systematic tracking of conditional versus unconditional actions and international support received has been introduced, alongside clearly assigned lead and coordinating institutions for each measure. The MRV system will capture gender-disaggregated data and information on environmental, social and economic co-benefits in line with the Sustainable Development Goals and will be tested and refined through the preparation of Namibia's second Biennial Transparency Report (BTR2).

6.1. Institutional arrangement for NDC Tracking

Namibia’s institutional arrangements are designed to support inter-sectoral collaboration in tracking NDC progress. Key institutions, such as the Ministry of Environment, Forestry, and Tourism (MEFT), lead coordination efforts, supported by sectoral ministries for energy, agriculture, forestry, and waste management. However, limited financial and technical capacity poses challenges to fully institutionalising tracking processes. External support, including international partnerships and capacity-building initiatives, plays a crucial role in bridging these gaps.

The NDC tracking framework integrates Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) across mitigation, adaptation and support received and needed, building on existing national and sectoral monitoring and evaluation systems. Relevant institutions will collect, verify and report data against agreed NDC indicators, while MEFT will coordinate the consolidation of information for national tracking and reporting under the UNFCCC Enhanced Transparency Framework.

The system will progressively strengthen the consistency, quality and use of data to support transparent assessment of progress towards Namibia’s NDC commitments.

For adaptation, the broader NDC tracking framework will be complemented by the Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) system outlined in Section 4.3, which will integrate climate risk and vulnerability information and assess progress in reducing vulnerability and strengthening resilience. In collaboration with the National Planning Commission and other relevant institutions, monitoring and evaluation findings will inform national planning, policy review, resource allocation and the periodic adjustment of adaptation measures, indicators and targets. Where relevant, GESI-disaggregated data will be captured to support inclusive and equitable adaptation action.

7. GENDER EQUITY AND SOCIAL INCLUSION

Climate change in Namibia has differentiated impacts, with women, youth, children, persons with disabilities, indigenous minorities and other vulnerable groups disproportionately affected due to existing socio-economic inequalities, unequal access to resources and assets, and limited participation in decision-making. These vulnerabilities are compounded by dependence on climate-sensitive livelihoods and natural resources, and by gendered responsibilities such as water and fuelwood collection. Namibia’s gender and social inclusion response is guided by the National Gender Policy (2010–2020), the Third National Gender Equality and Equity Policy (2025–2035), and the National Social Protection Policy (2021–2030). These frameworks are complemented by the forthcoming National Climate Change Gender Action Plan (NCCGAP), which will provide sector-specific guidance for gender-responsive climate action.

Building on lessons from NDC 2.0, NDC 3.0 integrates Gender Equality and Social Inclusion (GESI) considerations across mitigation and adaptation measure design. This includes GESI-disaggregated indicators; strengthened participation and access to climate-resilient livelihoods, productive resources and decision-making; gender-responsive delivery of selected mitigation measures; and adaptation measures addressing protection, GBV risks and shock-responsive social protection for climate-vulnerable populations. GESI integration will be further operationalised through the NDC Implementation Strategy and Action Plan (NDC-ISAP) and adaptation Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL) system

8. MEANS OF IMPLEMENTATION

8.1. Implementation overview

Namibia's ability to deliver NDC 3.0 depends on a coordinated, whole-of-government approach to financing, technology transfer, institutional coordination and capacity building, consistent with Articles 9, 10 and 11 of the Paris Agreement. Mitigation measures costed to date carry a total estimated investment requirement of approximately USD 6.42 billion to 2030, comprising USD 753 million (12%) in unconditional actions financed through domestic resources, private sector investment and already-secured financing, and USD 5.67 billion (88%) in conditional actions requiring international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building.

Adaptation costing across the nine (9) priority areas carries a total estimated investment requirement of approximately USD 7.65 billion to 2030, comprising approximately USD 0.90 billion (12%) in unconditional actions deliverable through existing government budgets, line-ministry capacity and domestic resources, and USD 6.75 billion (88%) in conditional actions requiring international climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building. Combined, Namibia's total estimated NDC 3.0 implementation cost (mitigation and adaptation) is approximately USD 14.07 billion to 2030/2035, comprising approximately USD 1.66 billion (12%) unconditional and USD 12.42 billion (88%) conditional investment requirements.

Implementation is coordinated by MEFT as the national climate change focal point, working with sectoral line ministries, the National Planning Commission and the Environmental Investment Fund of Namibia (EIF), Namibia's Green Climate Fund-accredited National Implementing Entity, together with development partners. Delivery will be operationalised through an updated NDC Implementation Strategy and Action Plan (NDC-ISAP), which designates lead and coordinating institutions, tracks conditionality and support received, and embeds GESI-responsive delivery mechanisms, consistent with the MRV arrangements described in Section 6. Priority cross-cutting needs include expanding direct-access climate finance, strengthening MRV and technical capacity, and deepening public-private collaboration to mobilise and de-risk investment at scale.
