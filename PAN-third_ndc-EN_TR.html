Declaration: Panama's Pact with Nature (Nature Pledge)

The planet is agonising. The world is in crisis. We see it in the forests being felled and burned, in the lands that dry out and crack and become unproductive, in the seas that overheat and lose their biodiversity. We see it in communities displaced by floods, in crops lost to droughts, in fishermen returning with empty nets and in girls and boys who inherit a future marked by uncertainty. Enough! We cannot remain trapped in a broken system where institutions work in isolation and solutions are diluted while nature bleeds. The Republic of Panama, fulfilling its constitutional mandate to guarantee a healthy and sustainable environment, declares its decision to act with urgency and coherence, uniting science, public policy and territory.

Panama's Pact with Nature (Nature Pledge), announced by President José Raúl Mulino at the 2025 United Nations General Assembly, is our manifesto of survival and dignity: a unified framework that integrates, in coherence with the multilateral system and national realities, our goals in climate change, biodiversity, combating desertification, plastics, marine and terrestrial conservation into a single country plan with clarity and transparency. This approach responds to the call of the first Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement and the Sustainable Development Goals, overcoming fragmentation and prioritising integrated and multi-sectoral solutions.

Our goals are concrete and comprehensive. By 2027, we will drive the realisation, revitalisation and full consolidation of the country's national parks and protected areas system, strengthening its management under the highest international standards. From now until 2035, we commit to restoring 100,000 hectares of ecosystems; achieving net zero deforestation; reducing our emissions by 11%; and decreasing plastic pollution by 50%. Likewise, by that same year, 40% of our land territory and 60% of our seas will be officially protected, consolidating Panama as a global leader in conservation and sustainability. These goals, taken together, are the core of a common vision that protects the life, economy and identity of Panama, with verifiable indicators and public monitoring mechanisms developed in the technical annexes. To achieve these objectives, we will establish the Panama Natural Fund, announced by the Government of Panama at the 2025 United Nations General Assembly, to guarantee permanent, long-term financing for nature and biodiversity conservation.

But conservation is not enough: we also need to regenerate and restore — we will recover degraded lands, sustainably manage soils and watersheds, and protect water as a common good. Our ocean is the backbone of our identity and economy: we will strengthen marine protection and combat plastic pollution, ensuring healthy marine, coastal and island ecosystems and sustainable fisheries. We will halt the loss of species and habitats, and ensure that ancestral knowledge and the benefits of nature are cared for, conserved and shared in a fair and equitable manner. Because we understand that there is no climate justice without ecological justice, nor ecological justice without social justice: both depend on conserving nature today and guaranteeing its care and protection forever.

Adaptation is an inseparable part of this vision. With the Regional Climate and Nature Observatories, we will bring science, innovation, conservation and traditional knowledge to every territory. These centres will not be limited to climate matters: they will be the comprehensive platform where all the Nature Pledge commitments are realised, from restoration to biodiversity protection, water management, combating pollution and community resilience. There, on the ground, the synergy we proclaim today will be lived: a space where nature, society and the economy work together in the same direction. Through the observatories, we will work at the local level to include children, adolescents and youth in designing solutions that will impact their future.

As a country of oceanic vocation, Panama takes on additional commitments for the protection of the ocean in all its dimensions. We will lead the global movement for the creation of the first High Seas Marine Protected Area, covering 10 million square kilometres, to safeguard the high seas ocean boundaries of the Tropical Eastern Pacific Marine Corridor, vital for migratory species such as humpback whales.

We support the global Antarctic Avengers campaign, which seeks to halt destructive industrial fishing in Antarctica, including krill fishing — the indispensable food for whales that migrate from those frozen seas to our tropical sanctuaries in Panama. We will lead the Silent Ocean Coalition, aimed at reducing underwater acoustic pollution and its impacts on migratory marine species. We will maintain a firm position of opposition to deep-sea mining in all spaces, promoting a global moratorium based on the precautionary principle. We will also lead the Coalition of Pioneer Countries of the Treaty on Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction (BBNJ), promoting its firm entry into force and its equitable implementation.

We recognise that losses and damages resulting from the global environmental crisis are already unavoidable in many cases. Floods, droughts, heat waves and forced displacement are not future threats — they are present realities, with direct effects on human health and quality of life. We commit to strengthening the national monitoring and evaluation system for losses and damages, ensuring science-based decisions and rapid responses across all territories. And when no alternative remains, we will apply a Planned Relocation Protocol, dignified and fair, that ensures the rights and livelihoods of communities that must rebuild their future on safe ground, as Panama was already compelled to do in Guna Yala in the community of Gardi Sugdub. This system will be coordinated with the National Climate Transparency Platform (PNTC) and sectoral information systems.

We are aware that none of this will be possible without permanent financing. That is why we affirm that the Panama Natural Fund is the cornerstone of this commitment: a tool designed to protect conservation and resilience, ensuring stable, permanent, predictable and long-term financing. We will prioritise international cooperation and new, additional, predictable and accessible environmental financing, including innovative instruments that do not increase the country's public debt, but rather the contrary: those that contribute to reducing debt and bringing its flows back to the country. Likewise, we recognise the key role of the private sector and public-private partnerships in mobilising investment, technology and green employment. We will promote entrepreneurship and private investment proactively and enthusiastically, prioritising free enterprise and sustainability.

Within this framework, we also take on the fundamental transformation that environmental justice demands. We bet on a just transition that puts an end to dependence on fossil fuels and ensures clean and accessible energy for all. We commit to resilient agri-food systems capable of guaranteeing food sovereignty and dignified livelihoods for farmers, rural women, indigenous peoples, local communities and Afro-descendant communities. We will promote sustainable cities, designed with nature-based solutions, resilient infrastructure and clean mobility. And we propose to build a circular economy that eradicates plastic pollution and transforms waste into innovation and opportunities. Gender equality and women's empowerment will be transversal principles of this transformation. With this we will protect public health and well-being, reducing the risks of heat waves, diseases and extreme events.

No one can be left behind. Women, children, youth, indigenous peoples, Afro-descendants, rural and coastal communities, older persons, persons with disabilities, entrepreneurs, business people, investors, academics and activists. This Pact includes every person, community and living being in our land. Climate justice is not an aspiration — it is an ethical and political mandate.

To the whole world we say: enough of empty rhetoric. Join us. Governments, companies, academia, peoples, business people, philanthropists, investors, youth. Bring capital, technology, political will. Our commitment aligns with an agenda of effective cooperation that converts ambition into results. Nature does not wait, and neither do we. Panama is ready. Not to keep following a broken game, but to change the rules. Not to manage the crisis, but to lead the transformation.

In coherence with this integrating vision, we call on the United Nations system, particularly the Secretariats of the Rio Conventions, to accelerate their own integration. It is not sustainable to negotiate interdependent solutions in three separate conferences. We propose coordinating agendas and calendars, establishing joint high-level segments, creating integrated financing windows and harmonising reporting frameworks, with full respect for mandates. We also invite consideration of decision-making modalities that ensure timeliness and effectiveness: when, after good faith efforts, consensus is not possible, to adopt decisions by majority, transparently and inclusively, in accordance with the practices of the General Assembly. Just as we are asked to integrate, we ask that the multilateral system integrate its governance to convert ambition into results.

This is our manifesto. This is Panama's Pact with Nature: a living movement, an irrevocable commitment to life, justice and hope. Climate justice is not an aspiration — it is an ethical and political mandate. Fragmentation weakened us; working together will make us invincible today and tomorrow. This is the path. Our future begins today.

Juan Carlos Navarro, Minister of Environment

National Circumstances

Strategic Objectives

Panama's Pact with Nature adopts an integrated national vision that unites climate, biodiversity and sustainable land management commitments under a framework of sustainability, resilience and environmental justice. Its approach seeks to ensure that all actions simultaneously contribute to mitigation, adaptation, restoration and conservation, avoiding duplicated efforts and maximising joint benefits.

National Strategic Vision

Panama's Pact with Nature guides the country's efforts towards protecting nature, restoring degraded ecosystems, adapting to climate change and strengthening community resilience. This vision reaffirms Panama's environmental leadership and its commitment to the well-being of the population and the health of ecosystems.

It is fully aligned with the Government Strategic Plan 2024–2029, which prioritises water sustainability, food security, climate change and territorial governance. Therefore, the Pact's actions directly respond to national development goals and strengthen the integration between environmental and socio-economic policies.

Panama's Pact with Nature is Panama's commitment to move from plans to action. More than a compilation of documents, the Pact is a living agenda that integrates our efforts on biodiversity, climate change, land degradation, oceans and plastics. Through it, we present in a unified manner the following strategic documents, adopting an integrated and technical-operational approach:

  • The National Biodiversity Strategy aligns with the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (CBD);
  • The National Land Degradation Neutrality Strategy (ENDT) governed by the UNCCD Strategic Framework;
  • The Nationally Determined Contribution 3.0, guided by the objectives of the Paris Agreement.

This approach directs the national vision towards three integrated strategic objectives of Panama's Pact with Nature and ensures traceability with the Global Biodiversity Framework Objectives, the UNCCD Strategic Framework Objectives and the Paris Agreement objectives, which are detailed below.

Cross-Cutting Principles

The implementation of the vision and strategic objectives of Panama's Pact with Nature will be based on cross-cutting principles that guide all actions and ensure coherence between biodiversity, climate change and land degradation neutrality.

  1. Institutional and territorial integration: Strengthening coordination mechanisms between institutions and levels of government (national, indigenous comarca and local). The proposal to integrate the National Committee on Climate Change, the National Committee to Combat Drought and Desertification, and the Inter-Institutional Commission on Biodiversity is promoted, ensuring the participation of local governments and indigenous peoples' authorities.

  2. Active and effective community participation: The inclusion of local communities, producers, indigenous peoples, youth and civil society will be promoted. This principle recognizes the efforts and roadmap for implementing the Escazú Agreement in the country, including proactive disclosure mechanisms, early and inclusive consultation, and safeguards for environmental defenders.

  3. Human rights approach, gender equality and intergenerational equity: Equal opportunities and the inclusion of vulnerable groups will be ensured.

  4. Synergistic biodiversity-land-climate approach: Actions will be designed to generate simultaneous co-benefits.

  5. Bioeconomy and circular economy approach: Value generation from the sustainable use of biodiversity will be promoted.

  6. Transparency, data and knowledge: Decision-making will be based on scientific information and traditional knowledge.

Source: Own elaboration, MiAMBIENTE, 2025.

The application of these principles will ensure that implementation is inclusive, equitable and coherent, and that it multiplies positive impacts. In practice, each programme, project or policy derived from this strategy will be evaluated in light of these principles, guaranteeing productive and recovering lands, resilient and prosperous communities and an integrated management of the territory that simultaneously contributes to national well-being and to the fulfilment of global environmental agreements.

Third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0)

Goals and Institutional Arrangements for National Climate Action

This annex constitutes Panama's response to the global call to communicate in a clearer, more transparent and more comprehensible manner its actions to mitigate and adapt to climate change. It reflects the country's commitment to climate ambition, accountability and effective implementation of the Paris Agreement, incorporating assumptions, methodologies and institutional frameworks that underpin its climate action. This information will be periodically updated and monitored through the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR), in accordance with the Enhanced Transparency Framework.

Panama's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0 comprises two main components:

  • A Mitigation objective, structured as a reduction target accompanied by policies, measures and instruments for its implementation.
  • An Adaptation Communication, which includes process goals based on measures aimed at strengthening the resilience of communities, ecosystems and key economic sectors against current and projected climate impacts, also considering measures of diversification and vulnerability reduction.

Mitigation Target in Accordance with Decision 4/CMA.1

Within the framework of the Paris Agreement, Panama presents the Information to Facilitate Clarity, Transparency and Understanding (ICTU), a mechanism designed to transparently report the update of Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). This instrument provides clarity on the objectives, methodologies applied and assumptions used, facilitating their understanding and international monitoring. Below, Panama sets out the progress and assumptions underpinning NDC 3.0.

Adaptation priorities are described in the Adaptation Communication. The incorporation of these co-benefits will be carried out in the future, in accordance with the assumptions and methodology established in section 5, as well as the availability of support and technical capacity.

4. Planning process

4(a)(i) Domestic institutional arrangements, public participation and engagement with local communities and indigenous peoples in a gender-responsive manner

Since the inclusion of climate change in environmental legislation through Law 8 of 25 March 2015, Panama has strengthened its institutional framework and consolidated a robust basis for climate action. On this basis, key instruments have been developed, such as the NDC, the National Climate Change Plan for the Agricultural Sector (2019), the Strategic Guidelines for Implementing the Energy Transition Agenda (2020), the National Forest Restoration Programme (2020), the National Gender and Climate Change Plan (2021), the Law on Comprehensive Solid Waste Management (2021), and the Roadmap for Implementing the Kigali Amendment (2022).

The preparation of NDC 3.0 has built on the participatory process of NDC1 and NDC2, through technical working groups for formulating and validating commitments with local actors, and sessions of the National Committee on Climate Change (CONACCP) as the leader in the institutionalization process.

The national targets were subjected to public participation processes in accordance with Law 125 of 2020, which approves the Escazú Agreement. In this regard, Panama is on the eve of the official publication of the Escazú Agreement Implementation Roadmap, through which priority actions will be developed leading up to 2030, to broaden processes of public participation, access to information and access to environmental justice during the implementation of commitments. As a result, the most comprehensive environmental public consultation process in the entire national territory was carried out, including indigenous comarcas, where more than 1,200 people were consulted, of whom 51% were women and 49% men, ensuring an equitable, representative process with a gender approach, including focused sessions with Afro-descendant communities.

Additionally, the country is advancing the development of an enabling legal framework through the draft Climate Change and Green Transition Framework Law, which seeks to establish a National Cabinet on Climate Change and Green Transition to ensure high-level decision-making and to integrate the climate change, biodiversity and desertification committees into a single structure that also includes representatives of organized youth, the private sector, academia, civil society, indigenous peoples, Afro-descendant communities, rural communities and other vulnerable groups.

4(a)(ii) Contextual aspects

National context: Details on the Republic of Panama's geography, climate, population and economic conditions are available in Panama's First Biennial Transparency Report (BTR, 2024). The preparation of Panama's NDC has considered national circumstances, recognizing the challenges and opportunities the country faces in mitigating climate change. In this process, capacity building and consolidation of governance have been key elements for coordinating national efforts and will be essential for achieving effective implementation that generates synergies between sectors.

Panama has prioritized actions that enable private sector participation, such as the Reduce Tu Huella programme, which currently covers approximately 10% of the emissions reported in the 2021 NGHGI.

Likewise, improvement of information to understand in detail the resources and ensure their protection is being promoted, through the development of a peatland map. Similarly, the importance of continuing with the implementation of high-impact sectoral initiatives such as the rice NAMA is recognized within the target. The information and results derived from these actions will contribute to future updates of the mitigation target, to the assessment within the carbon market strategy the country is developing, and to their incorporation into development plans.

All planned actions to meet the targets integrate an intergenerational approach, gender inclusion, development promotion and poverty gap reduction, aligned with respect for and protection of human rights, in particular the right to a healthy environment established in the National Constitution.

4(b) Country-specific information applicable to arrangements for joint action (Art. 4.2.)

Not applicable.

4(c) Application of the outcomes of the Global Stocktake in the preparation of this NDC

In relation to Decision 1/CMA.5, which contains the Outcome of the First Global Stocktake, the target is underpinned by the emphasis on the importance of conserving, protecting and restoring nature (paragraph 33), as well as oceans and coastal ecosystems (paragraph 35). The target to establish an emission limit is aligned with the importance of transitioning to sustainable lifestyles and patterns of consumption and production (paragraph 36). This update of the country's commitment is a response to the call in paragraph 37 and to align NDCs with the Long-Term Strategy (paragraph 41). Finally, this update responds to the call in paragraph 170 to communicate the NDC in 2025 with a 2035 target year.

4(d)(i) How the economic and social consequences of response measures have been considered within the NDC

The consequences of implementing measures such as the Energy Transition Agenda (ATE, 2020) have been considered, including their positive impact on generating green jobs, attracting investments in various parts of the country and, as a country that does not produce fuels and has a small manufacturing industry, the promotion of renewable energies that are produced domestically generates net positive impacts. Similarly, the promotion of new markets associated with clean technologies such as electric vehicles creates development opportunities that integrate the 4Ds (Decarbonization, Democratization, Decentralization and Digitalization), generating positive social impacts. However, we recognize the importance of deepening the analysis of the specific consequences of the NDC.

4(d)(ii) Specific projects, measures and activities to be implemented that contribute mitigation co-benefits

Under the coordination of MiAMBIENTE, Panama is developing national adaptation plans in prioritized areas: marine-coastal; agriculture, livestock and aquaculture, energy, infrastructure and tourism. The articulation of mitigation and adaptation actions with other public policies seeks emission reductions, and the generation of multiple co-benefits: conserving biodiversity, providing ecosystem services, reducing air pollution, improving public health, generating employment and income, reducing poverty and social and territorial inequalities, and strengthening the resilience of local communities and indigenous peoples. Any mitigation co-benefit will be included in accordance with the assumptions and methodology established in section 5. Adaptation priorities are described in the Adaptation Communication.

5. Assumptions and methodological approaches, including those for estimating and accounting for anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and removals

5(a) Assumptions and methodological approaches, including those for estimating and accounting for anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions and removals corresponding to the NDC, consistent with paragraph 31 of Decision 1/CP.21

A model was developed to construct decarbonization trajectories for the country, enabling the generation of prospective scenarios up to 2050. For this purpose, the Deep Decarbonization Pathways initiative tool was used and adapted. The model adopts a "top-down" approach, based on a global representation of economic relationships, and is grounded in the Kaya equations, which allow for a physical modelling of GHG emissions that evolve according to a limited number of variables. This approach enabled the estimation of activity data or emission intensity relationships. The reference trend for emissions was constructed based on the NGHGI (2024), prepared following the 2006 IPCC Guidelines, with 2019 refinement and the 2013 Wetlands Supplement.

5(b) Assumptions and methodological approaches used for accounting for the implementation of policies and measures or strategies in the NDC

Energy sector: It is assumed that the country achieves the targets of the National Electric Mobility Strategy by 2050, rather than 2030, with a 20% increase in electric vehicles in the private fleet, 15% in the bus fleet and 30% in the government fleet. It is also projected that 10% of heavy freight transport vehicles will use green hydrogen. For electricity generation, an installed capacity from non-conventional renewable sources of 30% of the installed capacity by 2050 is estimated, along with a 20% reduction in electricity consumption relative to the BAU scenario.

Agriculture sector: The application of policies aimed at improving the efficiency and/or reducing nitrogen contribution to the soil is envisaged. Following the State Agri-food Policy, the National Agricultural and Rural Sector Development Plan, the environmental protection and biodiversity guidelines are specifically established and applied, along with the contribution of the rice and livestock NAMAs. The combination of these policies allows for a 3% annual reduction in GHG intensity of GDP.

IPPU sector: It is assumed that the implementation of the Kigali Roadmap in Panama and the Panama Cooling Plan drives the modernization of air conditioning systems and the substitution of refrigerants, enabling a reduction in the carbon intensity of the sector from 2030.

Waste sector: It is assumed that the application of policies, plans and measures contributes to reducing the growth rate of waste generated per capita, beginning a progressive decrease from 2030.

5(c) If applicable, information on how the country will take into consideration existing methods in accordance with Article 4, paragraph 14 of the Paris Agreement

The country has used the IPCC Guidelines and their updates to establish GHG emission and removal values, in line with the provisions of Decision 18/CMA.1 and 5/CMA.3.

5(d) IPCC methodologies and metrics used to estimate anthropogenic GHG emissions and removals.

The 2006 IPCC Guidelines were used, with 2019 refinement and the 2013 Wetlands Supplement, and the Global Warming Potentials from AR5.

5(e) Sector, category or activity-specific assumptions, methodologies and approaches for GHG emissions and removals

5(e)(i) Approach for subsequent emissions and removals from natural disturbances on managed land

The priority areas for restoration and reforestation will exclude flood-prone and landslide-prone zones identified in the Risk Atlas. Forest fires in the country tend to be low-intensity, and prevention and control measures such as firebreaks and replanting of affected areas are planned. The Ministry of Environment, in coordination with other institutions, maintains surveillance, annual plans and effective coordination for fire prevention and control. Constant monitoring is also carried out to detect pests and diseases, although no significant impacts have been recorded. Furthermore, the risk from hurricanes is low, and wind events have not caused relevant damage to forest areas.

5(e)(ii) Approach for accounting harvested wood products

These emissions are not estimated at the national level nor included in the NGHGI due to lack of technical capacities and available information.

5(e)(iii) Approach used to address the effects of age-class structure in forests

To estimate the carbon removals associated with the LULUCF targets, the different prior land uses and their respective restoration modalities were considered, starting in 2026. A total of 100,000 hectares are projected to be intervened, distributed across seven modalities such as agroforestry systems, silvopastoral systems and reforestation in mangroves.

The estimates include all carbon pools and deduct emissions from biomass loss during land use conversion. IPCC 2006 emission factors and national studies were used for each modality. Additionally, a 20-year stabilization period for soil organic carbon was considered.

5(f) Other assumptions and methodological approaches to understand the NDC and estimate corresponding emissions and removals

5(f)(i) How the reference indicators, baselines and reference levels were constructed

A reference scenario (baseline) was developed from the historical trends of emissions and emission intensities.

For the energy sector, the projection was constructed based on the emission trend for the period 2011–2019, from the NGHGI presented in the National Inventory Document (DiN) 2024. The years 2020 and 2021 were not considered due to the distortions caused by the pandemic.

For the Agriculture, Waste, Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) sectors, a model with a "top-down" approach was developed, based on a global representation of economic relationships. The model is grounded in the Kaya equations, which allow for a physical modelling of GHG emissions that evolve according to a limited number of variables.

The scenarios are constructed to project these variables up to 2050: activity: evolution of sectoral Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (e.g. industrial GDP) or sector demand (e.g. mobility); the energy intensity of this activity: the amount of energy needed to perform one monetary unit of this activity; the carbon intensity of energy: the amount of carbon dioxide emissions per unit of energy used.

The trend scenario is an extrapolation scenario for each sector.

For the LULUCF sector, it is estimated that the carbon absorption capacity will progressively decrease from the value of the last NGHGI (DiN 2024), reaching a reduction of 9% by 2035 and 14.9% by 2050.

5(g) Intention to use voluntary cooperation under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement

The Government of Panama is implementing, in a pilot phase, its national carbon market. Both projects under this market and those developed in accordance with the Article 6 decision rulebook will use a unified registry and tracking mechanism, in order to ensure consistency between mechanisms, as well as environmental integrity and transparency of transactions. The national market will prioritize, among others, the use of Mitigation Contribution Units (MCUs), developed in accordance with the Rules, Modalities and Procedures (RPM) of Article 6.4, incorporating environmental and human rights safeguards, promoting effective participation and ensuring high standards of transparency and environmental integrity.

Panama recognizes the potential of cooperative approaches to mobilize timely investments, accelerate its transition towards resilient and low-emission development, and contribute to global climate action. In this context, the country will consider the use of ITMOs, provided they are technically assessed and have prior authorization from the National Government, under applicable regulations, existing or in development. These transfers will be subject to the guidance of Article 6.2, in accordance with the decisions adopted by the CMA.

In this process, it will be essential to ensure respect for the rights of indigenous peoples and Afro-descendant and local communities, including recognition of territorial rights, application of free, prior and informed consent standards, fair and equitable benefit sharing, the establishment of impact assessments and accessible grievance procedures, as well as transparency measures and participation in project monitoring.

Furthermore, Panama will prioritize the use of Article 6.8, as a key instrument to strengthen non-market-based cooperation, with emphasis on supporting the energy transition and national priorities in adaptation and resilience. This comprehensive approach will allow the country to progressively advance in meeting the NDC, while strengthening environmental integrity and trust in the international climate cooperation system and the protection of human rights.

Third Adaptation Communication

The Republic of Panama presents its Third Adaptation Communication, in fulfilment of Article 7, paragraphs 10 and 11 of the Paris Agreement, and in accordance with Decision 9/CMA.1, which establishes guidelines for its preparation and submission. This document sets out the country's national adaptation priorities and needs within the framework of Panama's Pact with Nature.

In Panama, adaptation is conceived as a transformative process that reduces risks and losses, strengthens knowledge, restores ecosystems and protects biodiversity. Through ecosystem-based adaptation, the country seeks to generate environmental, social and economic co-benefits that strengthen the resilience of the population, the productivity of sectors and the ecological integrity of the national territory.

In coherence with the Global Goal on Adaptation (Article 7.1 of the Paris Agreement), the State has established a national adaptation goal that guides long-term action:

The State establishes as its national adaptation goal to ensure that the country's population, economy and strategic natural ecosystems are resilient to the impacts of climate change, biodiversity loss and land degradation, guaranteeing sustainable livelihoods for present and future generations. This goal will be achieved through transformative, gradual and inclusive adaptation, based on science and traditional knowledge.

This national adaptation goal clearly aligns with the three components of the global adaptation goal established by the Paris Agreement:

  • Increase in adaptive capacity through climate information, robust institutional frameworks and the integration of scientific and local knowledge;
  • Reduction of vulnerability of populations, sectors and ecosystems by transforming the conditions that make them more sensitive to impacts; and
  • Strengthening of resilience, aspiring not only to resist and recover, but to thrive with more robust, flexible and interconnected social, economic and ecological systems.

The national adaptation commitment is organised around priority areas that concentrate the country's strategic action: energy; forests; sustainable agriculture, livestock and aquaculture; integrated watershed management; biodiversity; public health; sustainable infrastructure; resilient human settlements, tourism; and circular economy. These sectors reflect the areas where adaptation, biodiversity conservation and land degradation neutrality goals converge, enabling the generation of environmental, social and economic co-benefits and strengthening coherence between policies implemented under the three Rio Conventions.

Commitments, Goals and Institutional Arrangements for the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Biodiversity

Biodiversity, both terrestrial and marine, is one of Panama's most valuable assets and essential for sustaining human well-being, climate resilience and economic development. Panama, due to its strategic geographic location, possesses one of the greatest biological riches on the planet — unique natural ecosystems of high ecological value that also act as the continent's biological bridge through its terrestrial, marine and freshwater ecosystems. Panama has 67.1% of its territory covered by forest ecosystems and 54.7% of its seas under protection. Our oceans harbour coral reefs, seagrass beds, mangroves, fish banks and emblematic species that not only sustain marine life, but also artisanal fishing, sustainable tourism, food security and essential ecosystem services for climate change mitigation.

Panama adopts a comprehensive strategic vision to conserve and sustainably use its biodiversity. For the first time, the country compiles under a single framework its commitments in climate change, biodiversity and desertification, responding in a coordinated manner to these three global environmental crises.

In response, it reaffirms its commitment to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and to the implementation of the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework derived from decisions 4/COP.15 and 6/COP.15, as well as with the 2030 Agenda and its Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 14 (Life Below Water) and SDG 15 (Life on Land).

This strategy is the result of the goal-setting process within the project "Support for Early Action of the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF–EAS)" led by the Directorate of Protected Areas and Biodiversity, with the support of the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) that through participatory workshops during 2024 sought to strengthen the debate in the process of defining national, measurable, budgeted and explicit targets that will be periodically monitored through the National Biodiversity Reports.

Panama's Pact with Nature updates and replaces the 2018 National Biodiversity Strategy (aligned with the Aichi Framework) and incorporates national priorities for the period 2025–2035. It also integrates a common language and goals that will reinforce each other through joint implementation of the three Rio Conventions. Likewise, it lays the groundwork for aligning biodiversity conservation finances.

Caring for and restoring biodiversity is a direct path to achieving Land Degradation Neutrality and reinforcing climate change mitigation and adaptation — it keeps the land healthy and helps face changes in climate patterns. Natural ecosystems capture and store carbon and act as natural barriers against droughts, floods and storm surges.

Progress in Biodiversity Conservation

Since 2018, Panama has steadily advanced in implementing its National Biodiversity Strategy, consolidating a solid foundation for the new goals by 2035. The country now has more than 122 terrestrial and marine protected areas, a coverage that reflects its commitment to the conservation and sustainable use of its natural heritage.

Among the main achievements are the increase in protected marine area to 54%, nine years ahead of the global 30×30 target, the expansion of biological corridors and connectivity zones, and the strengthening of the National Protected Areas System (SINAP) through ranger training, inter-institutional cooperation and use of satellite technologies for environmental monitoring. Likewise, the adoption of policies such as the National Ocean Policy and the Wetlands Policy has made it possible to integrate biodiversity criteria into the management of productive and territorial sectors.

The Ministry of Environment has promoted flagship projects such as the Green Tourism Action Plan, Wildlife Rescue and Relocation Plans, and the Natural and Cultural Heritage Conservation Programme, which reinforce the articulation between conservation, sustainable development and local well-being.

The lessons learned are clear: biodiversity is not limited to protected areas; its management must be integrated into sectoral, local and community policies. Strengthening SINAP with technical capacities, sustainable financing and social participation is essential to face deforestation and ecosystem pressures.

The strategic use of technology and environmental traceability has improved transparency and evidence-based decision-making, promoting effective enforcement of the law and prevention of environmental crimes. These advances allow Panama to project itself as a regional reference in conservation and biodiversity governance.

Commitments, Goals and Institutional Arrangements to Combat Desertification

Land degradation constitutes one of the most relevant environmental challenges for Panama, directly impacting land productivity and biodiversity, human rights, socio-economic well-being and the country's climate resilience, especially for our local communities.

Panama adopts an integrated strategic vision to achieve Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN) by 2035, fully aligned with Panama's Pact with Nature. For the first time, the country integrates under a single framework its commitments in climate change, biodiversity and desertification, responding in a coordinated manner to these three urgent global environmental crises.

In response, Panama committed to the international Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN) initiative, in line with the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) and Sustainable Development Goal 15: Life on Land (SDG 15.3: on land degradation, SDG 15.3.1: on percentage of degraded land).

The LDN responds directly to the mandate derived from Decisions 3/COP.15 and 13/COP.15 adopted by the Parties at UNCCD COP 15, following which Panama was selected among the 18 countries that form part of the second phase of the Land Degradation Neutrality Target Setting Programme (LDN TSP 2.0 at Doha). This programme, led by the Water Security Directorate with the support of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), seeks to ensure that national land degradation neutrality targets are specific, time-bound, coherent with national policies, quantitative, spatially explicit, gender-sensitive and adequately integrated into the country's planning frameworks and will be periodically monitored through the National Implementation Reports.

Land Degradation Neutrality is understood as the condition in which the quantity and quality of land resources is maintained or increased relative to a baseline, avoiding net losses of productive land necessary to sustain ecosystem functions and increase food security.

This LDN Strategy reaffirms Panama's commitment to the joint implementation of the three Rio Conventions (Climate Change, Biological Diversity and Combating Desertification) so that goals reinforce each other and generate tangible co-benefits: conservation of biodiversity and ecosystems, carbon sequestration and adaptation to climate change, and greater food and water security. With an approach based on human rights, science and a common language, these synergies are articulated under Panama's Pact with Nature to guide coherent and effective actions.

Progress in Land Neutrality

Since 2018, Panama has advanced in implementing its National Land Degradation Neutrality Strategy (ENDT), with targets focused on increasing forest cover, recovering degraded lands and avoiding the conversion of forests to other uses. These efforts lay the foundations for a new phase of more integrated action oriented towards measurable results by 2035.

Among the main advances are the restoration of more than 18,000 hectares of forests and mangroves through public-private and community partnerships, the rehabilitation of degraded agricultural lands and pastures through sustainable management practices, and the training of producers in conservation agriculture and regenerative livestock. These interventions have improved soil productivity and reinforced water and ecosystem services.

Environmental monitoring has been significantly strengthened through the use of remote sensing and participatory analyses, such as the National Forest Cover and Land Use Mapathon, which detects land use changes and guides corrective actions in real time. At the institutional level, cooperation between MiAMBIENTE, the Ministry of Agricultural Development (MIDA), local governments, academia and civil society has promoted integrated territorial management, encouraging the inclusion of LDN and adaptation criteria in watershed management plans.

Lessons learned confirm that the combination of massive restoration, geospatial information and community participation can reverse degradation at scale, improving food security and livelihoods. Panama has proven that sustainable soil management not only restores ecosystems, but drives a model of resilient, fair and globally sustainable territorial development aligned with global sustainability commitments.

Goals 2025-2035

Panama's Pact with Nature places biodiversity at the centre of climate action and sustainable development. By 2035, the national goal is to restore and conserve 100,000 hectares of strategic terrestrial and marine ecosystems, prioritising at least 25% of degraded lands with greater emphasis on key areas for biodiversity, priority watersheds for water and agri-food security, buffer zones, mangroves, forestry vocation lands, agricultural lands and biological corridors.

With this, Panama will strengthen ecological and community resilience, increase carbon sinks with at least 1 million tonnes of CO2e absorbed or reduced in the LULUCF (Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry) sector through land degradation neutrality actions, improving sustainable productivity by 20% in key productive landscapes.

This goal will integrate Nature-based Solutions (NbS) approaches, participatory ecological restoration practices, agroforestry and silvopastoral systems, and reforestation with native species, and participatory ecological restoration mechanisms, ensuring that at least 50% of the restored area is achieved by 2030.

Likewise, the country will consolidate a functional network of at least three resilient biological corridors in the Azuero, Coclé and Veraguas regions, promoting ecological connectivity and reducing unplanned agricultural expansion in those areas by 30%.

These actions will simultaneously contribute to the goal of achieving net illegal deforestation zero, land degradation neutrality and an additional 11% emissions reduction by 2035. These goals are directly linked to the vision of conserving, restoring and sustainably using the country's natural capital.

Along these lines, the strengthening of the National Protected Areas System (SINAP) towards international standards will enable 40% of the land territory and 60% of the marine and continental water space to have effective management, participatory monitoring and ecological connectivity.

These integrated actions will increase ecological resilience, restore nature's contributions to people such as ecosystem functions and services including air and water regulation, soil fertility, protection against disasters and disease risk reduction, and will strengthen connectivity between terrestrial and marine ecosystems, from mangroves to watersheds and tropical forests.

Under this framework, Panama presents the following national biodiversity commitments, which materialise the convergence between the climate, biodiversity and land agendas, and consolidate the country as a global leader in results-based conservation and restoration.

Governance

Panama is moving towards a more unified and coordinated environmental governance that simultaneously fulfils its commitments in climate change, biodiversity and combating desertification. This vision responds to the need to overcome fragmented institutional structures and consolidate an articulated, transparent and participatory system.

Currently, the country has differentiated mechanisms for the three Rio Conventions: the National Committee on Climate Change, the Interinstitutional Biodiversity Commission and the National Committee to Combat Drought and Desertification, each under current sectoral policies. However, these spaces operate independently, which limits coordination and reduces the scope of policies.

In response, the Climate Change and Green Transition Framework Bill contemplates the creation of a National Green Transition Cabinet as a high-level inter-institutional body, supported by a National Climate, Biodiversity and Land Committee (CONACLABIT). In essence, this body will integrate existing committees into a single national committee, ensuring greater scope in decision-making, promoting agenda alignment and avoiding isolated or duplicated efforts, while also providing space for multi-sectoral representation.

The new institutional structure seeks to bring governance closer to the territory by leveraging existing local capacities and spaces. In line with this, the Watershed Committees present in the country's 52 watersheds will be strengthened so that they act as operational arms of the system, integrating municipalities, communities, water users, the private sector, academia and social organisations. In turn, these committees, together with the Rural Aqueduct Management Boards (JAAR) and local authorities, will play a key role in executing, monitoring and providing feedback on adaptation, conservation and sustainable water management actions at the community level.

In this way, by connecting CONACLABIT with local committees, a two-way flow of information will be generated: from the national level, guidelines and strategic direction will be provided, while from the territories, data, experiences and real needs will be contributed.

In summary, the proposed governance integrates unified national leadership with decentralised implementation, encompassing all relevant sectors: ministries, agencies, academia, NGOs, private sector and communities, to coherently address the challenges of drought, climate change and biodiversity loss, leveraging synergies between these interconnected agendas.

Financing

The success of Panama's Pact with Nature will depend on having sustainable, sufficient and constant financial resources that make it possible to effectively launch conservation, restoration and climate action (mitigation and adaptation) measures. Both the National Biodiversity Strategy, the Land Degradation Neutrality Strategy and the Nationally Determined Contribution agree that financing is an essential basis for achieving the country's goals in biodiversity, climate and sustainable land management.

The combination of national resources with international cooperation has become key to expanding the scope and effectiveness of environmental and climate policies. In this regard, international cooperation has been a decisive complement. The Green Climate Fund supports the National Adaptation Plan (NAP Panama), which strengthens risk management and sectoral planning, while the Global Environment Facility (GEF) promotes projects on drought, land restoration and community actions through the Small Grants Programme. These initiatives not only provide financing, but also technical assistance, institutional strengthening and direct benefits for rural communities.

In turn, the Climate Change and Green Transition Framework Bill proposes new instruments to expand sources of climate financing, among them the creation of a National Carbon Market and the regulation of green and sustainable bonds. This will enable attracting private investment towards emissions reduction, adaptation and conservation projects, generating market incentives with criteria of transparency, traceability and environmental and human rights safeguards.

Looking to the near future, Panama seeks to consolidate a solid and innovative environmental financial model capable of integrating different resource sources and ensuring their long-term sustainability. The articulation of biodiversity, climate and land agendas will open the path to preparing joint proposals before international funds, showing a unified and coherent country vision that improves opportunities to access new resources that drive the Pact's actions.

Indeed, Panama is heading towards a new phase of environmental financial management. The Panama Natural Fund will be the main axis of the Pact, channelling public, private and international cooperation resources to promote restoration, water management and climate resilience programmes, guaranteeing continuity of actions beyond political cycles.

At the same time, the country will continue strengthening its relationship with funds such as the Green Climate Fund, the Adaptation Fund and the GEF, and will maintain partnerships with multilateral and development banks, seeking co-financing schemes, technical assistance and capacity building. According to the First Biennial Transparency Report 2024, Panama needs around USD 1,230 million to finance ongoing or design-phase adaptation actions focused on resilience, water security and ecosystem management.

In summary, Panama's financing strategy articulates public budget, international cooperation and innovative market mechanisms, aligning flows with low-emission and climate-resilient development. Its implementation will be mixed and multi-sectoral, with integrated governance and rigorous monitoring through the National Climate Transparency Platform and sectoral systems.

Implementation and Monitoring

The implementation of Panama's Pact with Nature is conceived as a living, coordinated and participatory process that unites efforts from the State, the private sector, academia, communities and civil society. Its success will depend not only on available resources, but also on the country's capacity to coordinate, monitor and evaluate progress clearly and constantly.

Environmental goals and commitments will be supported by an integrated Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system within the National Climate Transparency Platform (PNTC), which will bring together in one place the progress of the climate, biodiversity and land agendas, using common indicators, generating periodic reports and facilitating independent verification of results.

With this integration, information will be accessible to all and inter-institutional coordination will be strengthened, ensuring decisions based on real data. Achievements will be measurable transparently and comparably, enabling the identification of progress, gaps and opportunities for improvement.

Monitoring will be closely linked to the main national and international planning instruments, the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC), the National Adaptation Plan (NAP), the National Biodiversity Strategy and the Land Degradation Neutrality Strategy, ensuring coherence between commitments and their evaluation mechanisms.

The information generated will guide new decisions, investments and actions, strengthening capacities both at institutional and territorial levels. This monitoring methodology will maintain alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals and the three Rio Conventions, reflecting qualitative and quantitative results in communities and ecosystems across the country.
