Foreword

The third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) submitted by the Republic of Chad for the period 2026-2035 comes at a time when the effects of climate change are already a reality for our country. Since the 1960s, average temperatures have risen by 1 to 1.5 °C, while irregular rainfall, droughts, floods and land degradation directly affect populations, ecosystems and economic activities. In a country where more than three quarters of the population live in rural areas and where agriculture and livestock remain the main sources of employment, climate action is both an imperative for resilience and a condition for our development. Between 2019 and 2025, loss and damage linked to climate events is thus estimated at nearly 6,886 billion CFA francs.

Minister of Environment, Fisheries and Sustainable Development

NDC 3.0 extends and strengthens the commitments made by Chad under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Paris Agreement. It draws on lessons learned from the implementation of the 2021 updated NDC and takes into account the guidance resulting from the first Global Stocktake. It is based in particular on a national greenhouse gas inventory updated to 2022 and on a revised reference scenario.

By 2035, Chad aims to reduce its net greenhouse gas emissions by 1.7% compared to the reference scenario through national means, an ambition that could reach 21.2% subject to the mobilisation of adequate international support. At the same time, adaptation remains a central priority of our climate action, while strengthening the consideration of loss and damage and of cross-cutting dimensions, notably gender, social inclusion, just transition, health, peace and security.

This climate ambition is inseparable from our development priorities. NDC 3.0 is aligned with the National Development Plan “Tchad Connexion 2030” and the country’s sectoral policies and strategies, in order to reconcile climate action, the preservation of natural resources and sustainable economic and social transformation.

This contribution is the outcome of a collective process led under the aegis of the Ministry in charge of the environment, involving sectoral ministries, national institutions, local authorities, experts, the private sector, civil society and technical and financial partners. I wish to commend the quality of this collective commitment, which has provided Chad with a climate action framework grounded in national realities and the priorities expressed by the various stakeholders.

The success of this ambition will depend on our collective capacity to mobilise the resources needed for its implementation. A significant share of the commitments made in this NDC remains conditional on international support commensurate with the challenges Chad faces. The Government of the Republic of Chad reaffirms its determination to fully assume its share of the collective effort and calls on its technical and financial partners to strengthen their commitment alongside it. The task now is to translate the orientations of NDC 3.0 into concrete results for populations and territories and to contribute to sustainable and inclusive development for the benefit of present and future generations.

1. Executive summary

The Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0 covers the period 2026-2035 and constitutes a new stage in Chad’s climate planning. Informed by the first Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement, it draws on lessons from the 2021 updated NDC, builds on a national greenhouse gas (GHG) inventory updated to 2022, revises the reference scenario and further structures the adaptation contribution around results to be monitored. It also strengthens the place of loss and damage, cross-cutting dimensions, means of implementation and transparency.

National circumstances and ambition of NDC 3.0

A landlocked Sahelian country with an area of 1,284,000 km², Chad is highly exposed to rising temperatures, irregular rainfall, droughts, floods, heatwaves and ecosystem degradation. Average temperatures have already increased by about 1 to 1.5°C since the 1960s and projections indicate a further increase of around 1.5 to 2.5°C by 2050. These changes directly affect agricultural and pastoral systems, water resources, ecosystems, infrastructure and the living conditions of populations. Climate vulnerability is compounded by significant socio-economic constraints: nearly 77% of the population lives in rural areas and the primary sector accounts for more than 70% of employment. By 2050, the impacts of climate change could lead to GDP losses estimated at about 4.2% per year under an optimistic scenario, and up to 10.5% under a pessimistic scenario. NDC 3.0 is in line with the priorities of the National Development Plan “Tchad Connexion 2030”, which aims at a structural transformation of the economy. It links climate action with national priorities relating to infrastructure, energy, agricultural transformation, human capital, governance and industrialisation. It marks progress compared with the previous NDC through the broadening of sectoral coverage, the methodological consolidation of the mitigation component, the structuring of a results framework for adaptation and the strengthening of monitoring and transparency. Beyond its vulnerabilities, Chad has significant assets to strengthen its resilience and support its development in a context of climate change. Its agricultural and pastoral resources, its lands and ecosystems, its water resources and its large solar potential offer levers to develop more resilient production systems, improve access to energy and diversify economic activities. The youth of its population also represents potential for skills development, innovation, entrepreneurship and the creation of green jobs.

Mitigation contribution

The updated national inventory covers the period 2010-2022. In 2022, Chad’s net emissions reached 93,801 kilotonnes of CO₂ equivalent (ktCO2e), compared with 52,488 ktCO2e in 2010, an increase of 79%. Agriculture accounts for more than 92% of emissions excluding land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF), mainly due to enteric fermentation from livestock and N2O emissions from agricultural soils. The LULUCF sector remains a net sink (-4,540 ktCO₂e in 2022). Although its removal capacity has generally declined over the historical period, a recovery of the sink has been observed in recent years. The reference scenario (Business As Usual – BAU) projects an increase in net emissions to 131,839 ktCO2e in 2035. The unconditional contribution would bring them down to 129,587 ktCO2e, a reduction of 1.7% compared with the reference scenario. With adequate international support, the additional reduction would bring the total effort to 21.2%, for a net emissions level of 103,829 ktCO2e in 2035.

Adaptation contribution

Adaptation is a central priority of NDC 3.0. Its vision for 2035 is to durably strengthen the resilience of populations, production systems, ecosystems and territories, by prioritising the most vulnerable areas and groups and by integrating adaptation into development policies and investments.

The contribution is structured around five strategic objectives: resilience of agricultural, pastoral and food systems; sustainable access to water, sanitation and essential services; preservation and restoration of ecosystems and natural resources; reduction of exposure to risks through prevention, early warning and planning; capacity building for adaptation of populations and institutions. The adaptation contribution translates these objectives into a set of priority measures to be implemented or strengthened by 2035.

Loss and damage

NDC 3.0 treats loss and damage as a distinct area, in connection with Article 8 of the Paris Agreement, the Warsaw International Mechanism, the Santiago Network and the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage. Over the period 2019-2025, loss and damage linked to climate events is estimated at 6,886 billion FCFA, with floods accounting for nearly 70% of recorded losses and droughts 19%. By 2035, projected loss and damage over the period 2026-2035 could reach 12,804 billion FCFA, bringing the overall recovery needs estimated for the entire period 2019-2035 to about 24,613 billion FCFA, or nearly 41 billion USD. Given the scale of these needs, a first operational roadmap for loss and damage covering the period 2026-2028 identifies 270 billion FCFA of priority interventions. This amount therefore does not constitute an estimate of the country’s total recovery needs, but corresponds to a portfolio of priority actions to be undertaken in the short term. The 2026-2028 roadmap thus presented constitutes a first operational programme focused on four priorities: mobilisation and governance of finance; observation and early warning; knowledge, monitoring and transparency; institutional framework and recovery.

NDC 3.0 also integrates, in a cross-cutting manner, issues of gender, social inclusion, just transition, human rights, health, human mobility and peace and security, with the aim of promoting inclusive and equitable climate action. Particular attention is paid to the populations most vulnerable to the effects of climate change, notably children and young people, whose specific needs must be taken into account in prevention, response and recovery actions.

Means of implementation, governance and transparency

Implementation of NDC 3.0 requires a scaling up of climate finance, capacity building and access to technologies. For mitigation, investment needs are estimated at 7.16 billion USD by 2035, of which 6.73 billion USD (about 94%) for the component conditional on international support and 434 million USD (about 6%) for the unconditional component. The financial needs associated with the adaptation contribution are estimated at about 17.3 billion USD over the period 2026-2035, according to the high estimate adopted in the preparatory work for NDC 3.0. This amount includes about 15.4 billion USD for the priority adaptation sectors, in addition to which needs are estimated at 1.75 billion USD for the climate-peace-security nexus and 0.18 billion USD for gender mainstreaming. These estimates are planning orders of magnitude that will need to be progressively refined on the basis of detailed costing of the measures and their implementation targets.

Governance and national transparency system

Overall coordination of implementation is ensured by the Ministry of Environment, Fisheries and Sustainable Development (MEPDD), in conjunction with sectoral ministries, data-producing institutions, local authorities, the private sector, civil society and technical and financial partners. The objective is to consolidate a permanent coordination framework, with clearly defined responsibilities and formalised data-sharing mechanisms.

Strengthening and sustaining the national Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) system is a central element of NDC 3.0 implementation. The current system, coordinated by the Directorate for Climate Change Control (DLCC), relies in particular on the MRV Steering Committee established in 2024 and on sectoral focal points. The period 2026-2028 will make it possible to consolidate this architecture, strengthen capacities, data-sharing and quality control mechanisms and the national transparency platform. In this context, the establishment of a dedicated National MRV Agency may be considered in order to strengthen the system over time, subject to its validation at national level. The objective is to have, from 2029, a sustainable and fully operational national transparency system, contributing in particular to the regular production of Biennial Transparency Reports.

Chad’s NDC 3.0 combines a raising of mitigation ambition, adaptation that is more results-oriented, explicit recognition of loss and damage and a strengthened implementation and transparency framework. Its full level of ambition will depend on sustained national mobilisation and substantially strengthened international support in terms of finance, technologies and capacities.

2. Introduction

The Republic of Chad, a Party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and to the Paris Agreement, submits its Nationally Determined Contribution 3.0 (NDC 3.0) for the period 2026-2035. This new contribution builds on the climate commitments made by the country since its first NDC and its updated contribution of 2021, while opening a new stage: that of more ambitious climate action, more firmly anchored in national development priorities and oriented towards implementation and the monitoring of results.

As a landlocked Sahelian country, Chad is particularly exposed to the effects of climate change. This vulnerability is closely linked to the dependence of a large part of the economy and of livelihoods on natural resources and climatic conditions. Agriculture, livestock, forestry and fisheries account for a significant share of national value added, while the primary sector accounts for more than 70% of employment. Rising temperatures and more intense heat episodes, increasingly irregular rainfall, droughts, floods and land degradation are already affecting agricultural and pastoral systems, the availability of water resources, ecosystems and infrastructure. Their repercussions on production, food security, health and livelihoods exacerbate already significant socio-economic vulnerabilities, to which children are particularly exposed.

The consequences of these changes are already tangible and represent a considerable cost for the country. The national loss and damage assessment conducted as part of the preparation of the NDC estimates the cumulative damage, economic losses and non-economic losses between 2019 and 2025 at 6,886 billion FCFA. In 2024 alone, nearly 1.93 million people were affected and losses are estimated at 2,908 billion FCFA. These orders of magnitude illustrate the scale of the challenges facing the country and are a reminder that, for Chad, climate action is not solely an environmental commitment: it is a matter of development, economic resilience and protection of the population.

Beyond its vulnerabilities, Chad also has significant assets for strengthening its resilience and supporting low-carbon development, including its agricultural and pastoral resources, its renewable energy potential, its water resources, as well as a predominantly young population and local knowledge adapted to Sahelian conditions. The sustainable use of these assets is a lever for adaptation, economic diversification and job creation. At the same time, the intensification of climate hazards calls for strengthened anticipation and risk management, taking into account their interactions with population displacement, access to natural resources, social cohesion, peace and security.

The NDC 3.0 also comes at a decisive moment in the implementation of the Paris Agreement, following the first Global Stocktake (GST). In accordance with Article 14.3 of the Paris Agreement, the outcomes of the GST have informed the preparation of this new contribution. The NDC 3.0 thus translates the orientations of the GST through national priorities: development of renewable energy and improvement of energy efficiency, conservation and restoration of land and ecosystems, strengthening the resilience of agricultural and pastoral systems, improving water and climate risk management, and increased attention to the financial, technological and institutional conditions needed for implementation.

This linkage between climate action and development lies at the heart of the NDC 3.0. It is notably aligned with the vision set out in the National Development Plan “Tchad Connexion 2030”, which aims to bring about a profound transformation of the economy and of living conditions and to enable Chad to join the group of middle-income economies during the 2030s. In this perspective, the NDC 3.0 aims to support Chad’s development ambitions by fully integrating climate resilience and emission reduction issues into the economic and social transformations undertaken by the country.

Finally, this new contribution builds on the experience gained since Chad’s previous NDCs. The assessment of the 2021 updated contribution highlighted progress, but also limitations relating to data availability, the monitoring of policies and measures, institutional coordination, implementation capacities and the mobilisation of finance. The NDC 3.0 intends to take a further step by drawing on these lessons: its mitigation scope is broadened, its methodological foundations are updated, adaptation is more structured around results to be achieved and their monitoring, and greater emphasis is placed on loss and damage, the social dimensions of the transition, the means of implementation and transparency.

Through its NDC 3.0, Chad thus puts forward a vision in which climate action supports the transformation and development of the country: strengthening the resilience of people and territories, preserving the natural resources on which present and future generations depend, progressively undertaking the transformations needed for a low-emission economy and ensuring that this transition is inclusive and equitable. Achieving this vision will depend on the commitment of national institutions, local authorities, communities, the private sector and civil society, as well as on strengthened international cooperation enabling the country to obtain the finance, technologies and capacities needed to reach its full level of ambition.

3. National circumstances and development priorities

3.1 Climate profile and vulnerabilities

Chad, the fifth largest country in Africa, is a landlocked Sahelian State whose geographical and climatic characteristics make it particularly vulnerable to the effects of climate change. The territory is structured along a marked north-south climatic gradient, comprising three major bioclimatic zones: the hyper-arid Saharan zone in the north, the semi-arid Sahelian zone in the centre and the sub-humid Sudanian zone in the south. This diversity shapes production systems, natural resources and the livelihoods of the population.

The spatial distribution of rainfall remains highly contrasted, with less than 100 mm per year in the Saharan regions, between 200 and 600 mm in the Sahelian zone and up to 1,200 mm in the southern regions (FAO, n.d.). However, the partial recovery in rainfall observed since the 1990s has not been accompanied by a return to stable climatic conditions. Rainy seasons in the region now show later onsets and endings and a greater concentration of rainfall over short periods, simultaneously increasing the risks of agricultural drought and flooding (IPCC, 2022).

Average temperatures have risen by approximately 1 to 1.5°C since the 1960s and are expected to continue rising over the coming decades. This increase accentuates evapotranspiration, reduces the availability of water resources and fosters desertification and land degradation processes, particularly in the Sahelian and Saharan zones.

These climatic changes are resulting in an intensification of extreme weather events. Droughts remain recurrent, while the increasing intensity of rainfall is leading to more frequent flood episodes (IPCC, 2022). Heatwaves are also becoming increasingly frequent (IPCC, 2021). Furthermore, the profound hydrological and socio-environmental transformations of the Lake Chad basin (UNEP, 2008), linked to the interaction between hydro-climatic variability, climate change, water withdrawals, changes in land use and demographic dynamics, illustrate the high vulnerability of the country’s natural systems.

Climate projections indicate a continuation of these trends, with a temperature increase of between +1.5°C and +2.5°C by 2050 (World Bank – CCKP, 2021), high uncertainty regarding annual rainfall but an expected increase in extreme rainfall, as well as a decrease in groundwater recharge and a decline in rain-fed crop yields. These changes increase the vulnerability of the population and of economic sectors dependent on natural resources.

Beyond these vulnerabilities, Chad has significant natural and socio-economic resources capable of supporting its development and its response to climate change. The country has approximately 40 million hectares of arable land, still under-utilised, and a livestock herd of more than 100 million head. Agriculture, pastoral livestock farming and several value chains adapted to Sahelian conditions, notably millet, sorghum, cowpea, sesame and gum arabic, are levers for food security, rural employment and local processing. Water resources, in particular those of the Chari and Logone rivers, wetlands and groundwater reserves, also represent significant potential, the use of which remains closely linked to the challenges of sustainable management and adaptation to climate change.

Chad also has significant renewable energy potential, particularly solar, complemented by opportunities to develop hydropower, wind and biomass. The sustainable mobilisation of these resources can help improve access to energy, notably through decentralised solutions, and support productive uses such as irrigation, pumping and cold chains. Natural heritage, biodiversity and non-timber forest products are also resources that can contribute to the diversification of economic activities and livelihoods.

3.2 Socio-economic context

With a population estimated at 18,765,512 inhabitants in 2024 (INSEED), of whom 58% are children under the age of 18 (UNICEF, 2026), Chad is one of the countries with the fastest population growth in the world, with an annual rate close to 5% in 2024 (World Bank, 2025). The population remains overwhelmingly rural, with nearly 74.4% of inhabitants living in rural areas in 2023 (INSEED). Livelihoods there rely mainly on agriculture, livestock and the exploitation of natural resources. This demographic dynamic, combined with progressive urbanisation, increases needs for infrastructure, housing, basic services, jobs and natural resources.

The Chadian economy remains dominated by the primary and extractive sectors. Oil production is the main source of export earnings and public revenue, while agriculture and livestock remain the main providers of employment and ensure the livelihoods of a large majority of households. Despite economic growth estimated at 4% in 2023 according to INSEED, driven mainly by the oil sector, public investment and the recovery of agricultural production after the major floods of 2022, the Chadian economy remains highly exposed to climate hazards as well as to external shocks.

Progress in human development remains limited. In 2022, 44.8% of the population lived below the national poverty line2 (World Bank, 2025), with marked territorial disparities and poverty particularly concentrated in rural areas. Access to essential services also remains insufficient: only 13.4% of the population had access to electricity in 2024 (World Bank, 2025), while health, education and human capital indicators are among the lowest in the world. According to the 2025 Human Development Report (based on 2023 data), Chad has a Human Development Index (HDI) of 0.416, ranking it 190th out of 193 countries and territories. This situation limits the population’s capacity to adapt to climate crises and undermines long-term development prospects.

Climate change exacerbates Chad’s socio-economic vulnerabilities, in particular by weakening production systems and livelihoods that are highly dependent on natural resources. Its effects weigh disproportionately on the most vulnerable populations, making the strengthening of resilience a central issue for the country’s sustainable development.

3.3 National development priorities

Sustainable development is a central pillar of Chad’s national strategy. Through Vision 2030 and the National Development Plan (PND) “Tchad Connexion 2030” (2024-2030), adopted in May 2025, the Government aims to undertake a structural transformation of the economy in order to promote strong, inclusive and resilient growth, while sustainably improving the living conditions of the population. The PND notably sets the objective of accelerating economic growth, reducing poverty, strengthening human capital and harnessing the country’s significant agro-pastoral, mining and energy potential.

This ambition is structured around four major areas of intervention broken down into 17 programmes comprising more than 268 development projects, measures and reforms, including 133 structuring interventions, covering in particular infrastructure development, access to energy, agricultural transformation, human capital, governance, industrialisation and private sector promotion. The Plan also gives significant importance to climate resilience, considered an essential condition for achieving economic and social development objectives.

This orientation is supported by a progressively strengthened policy and institutional framework. In addition to Vision 2030 and the National Development Plan, Chad has adopted a National Strategy to Combat Climate Change (2017), a National Environmental Policy (2020) and a National Adaptation Plan (2022). Climate governance is ensured by the Ministry in charge of the Environment, in coordination with the sectoral ministries. Significant progress has been made, notably the update of the national GHG inventory in accordance with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (and the 2019 Refinement), the development of a measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) system and the use of modelling tools such as the Greenhouse Gas Abatement Cost Model (GACMO). However, challenges remain, notably insufficient climate and sectoral data, inter-institutional coordination that still needs improvement and a strong dependence on external financing.

In this context, the NDC 3.0 is an instrument for implementing national orientations, linking climate action with Chad’s economic and social development priorities. The implementation of the NDC will also seek coherence with the national biodiversity strategy and action plan, as well as with relevant sectoral policies relating to agriculture, livestock, water, energy, health, forests, waste and land-use planning.

4. NDC 3.0 development process

4.1 Overview of the revision process

The development of the NDC 3.0 is part of a revision process aimed at strengthening Chad’s climate ambition while improving the technical robustness, coherence and operationality of its national contribution. This process was conducted under the leadership of the Ministry of Environment, Fisheries and Sustainable Development, with the participation of the relevant sectoral ministries and institutions, building on the lessons learned from the implementation of the updated NDC (2021) and on the updating of national policies, strategies and frameworks adopted since its submission.

The development of the NDC 3.0 sought to strengthen its alignment with national development priorities and its technical credibility, through the updating of data, the revision of assumptions and better consideration of sectoral realities.

The development of the NDC 3.0 also took into account the conclusions of the first Global Stocktake adopted in 2023. These notably reinforced the contribution’s orientation towards strengthening the ambition and implementation of climate action, accelerating the energy transition, conserving and restoring ecosystems, strengthening adaptation and resilience, as well as increased attention to the means of implementation. The NDC 3.0 translates these orientations taking into account national circumstances, Chad’s development priorities and the country’s implementation capacities.

The development process was based on a participatory approach involving sectoral administrations, technical institutions, data producers, national experts and partners involved in the implementation of climate policies. Throughout the revision, technical meetings, working workshops and bilateral exchanges made it possible to share the available information, consolidate sectoral data, discuss the assumptions adopted and gather contributions from the various stakeholders.

This collaborative approach concluded with a national validation workshop, during which the results of the work were discussed in order to consolidate the orientations and validate the main elements of the NDC 3.0 before its finalisation.

4.2 Lessons learned from the implementation of the updated NDC (2021)

The assessment of the implementation of the updated NDC (2021) was an essential step in the process of revising the national contribution. The analyses conducted using complementary approaches – based both on the evolution of national emissions and on the monitoring of the implementation of policies and measures – made it possible to identify the main achievements, the limitations encountered and the improvements to be integrated into the NDC 3.0.

A first lesson concerns the need to update the reference scenario of the updated NDC (2021). The analyses showed that the assumptions adopted during its development no longer fully reflected the dynamics observed in several sectors. The NDC 3.0 therefore relies on a revised reference scenario, built on more recent data and updated methods, in order to have a more representative basis for assessing mitigation efforts and monitoring progress.

The work also highlighted uneven implementation of the actions planned in the updated NDC (2021). While some measures were deployed and monitored, others could not be assessed owing to insufficient data or the lack of appropriate monitoring arrangements. This situation underlined the importance of better defining the implementation modalities of actions, clarifying institutional responsibilities and associating each measure with indicators enabling its results to be assessed.

One of the main challenges identified concerns the availability, quality and structuring of the data needed to monitor the national contribution. The monitoring exercises highlighted gaps in data collection and management systems, as well as heterogeneity in the information available across sectors. These difficulties have at times limited the ability to measure the results achieved or to compare observed changes with the targets initially set.

These findings directly guided the preparation of the NDC 3.0, through the updating of the reference scenario, the revision of measures in light of national realities and the strengthening of monitoring arrangements. Increased attention was also paid to the conditions for implementing the contribution, notably to capacity needs and international support.

Main advances of the NDC 3.0 compared with the previous contribution:

The NDC 3.0 marks progress compared with the previous contribution that is not limited to the quantitative level of emission reductions. First, it broadens mitigation coverage, with the integration of actions in sectors not previously covered, notably transport and agriculture, and a more precise definition of measures in the waste sector. The revision of the inventory and of the reference scenario, based on updated national data and a broader scope, also strengthens the robustness of the contribution and the transparency of the assumptions adopted.

With regard to adaptation, the NDC 3.0 strengthens the shift from sectoral orientations to a framework more oriented towards implementation and monitoring, through the updating of risks and vulnerabilities and the identification of priority measures accompanied, where the available information allows, by indicators, baselines and targets for 2030 and 2035. It also gives greater emphasis to cross-cutting dimensions, loss and damage and the means of implementation. Finally, the strengthening of the governance, monitoring and transparency framework aims to improve the tracking of progress and the alignment of the NDC with the Enhanced Transparency Framework of the Paris Agreement.

4.3 Mitigation

The development of the Mitigation component of the NDC 3.0 was based on three complementary pieces of work: the update of the national greenhouse gas inventory, the revision of the reference scenario and the assessment of policies and measures likely to reduce emissions or increase removals. This approach aims to ensure consistency between historical data, emission projections and the level of ambition adopted in the national contribution.

Update of the national greenhouse gas inventory

The revision of the Mitigation component was first based on an update of the national inventory of GHG emissions and removals.

The updated inventory covers the period 2010-2022, compared with 2010-2018 in the previous edition. The inclusion of the most recent years was accompanied by a recalculation of the entire historical time series, in order to ensure methodological consistency and comparability of results over time.

The estimates were prepared using the 2006 IPCC Guidelines and, where necessary, their 2019 Refinement. The process included a review of the improvements identified during the previous exercise, an update of sectoral data needs, the collection of activity data, the calculation of emissions, the compilation of sectoral results and the implementation of quality assurance and quality control procedures.

The update of the inventory increased the use of national and sectoral data, notably for fuel consumption in the energy sector, while continuing to use international sources where national data were unavailable or insufficiently detailed.

The scope of the inventory was also broadened, with the inclusion of emissions from petroleum refining in the energy sector and of first estimates, in the industrial processes and product use sector, of emissions associated with non-energy uses of fuels and solvents and with fluorinated gases. Finally, the global warming potentials were updated on the basis of the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report.

These improvements strengthen the robustness of the basis used for the analysis of trends, the construction of the reference scenario and the definition of the mitigation contribution.

Revision of the reference scenario

The reference scenario, or business-as-usual scenario, represents the counterfactual trajectory of emissions and removals from 2023 to 2035 in the absence of additional mitigation policies and measures. It is the point of comparison against which the expected emission reductions and the level of effort associated with the national contribution are assessed.

The monitoring work on the updated NDC (2021) highlighted a gap between its initial reference scenario and the emissions observed in the updated inventory, not attributable to the implementation of mitigation policies. The previous scenario therefore no longer sufficiently reflected recent emission levels and dynamics, which limited the relevance of monitoring and the clarity of the mitigation effort.

A first revision of the reference scenario was carried out as part of the monitoring of the updated NDC (2021) in order to restore its consistency with the updated national inventory. Recalibrated to the year 2018, the trajectory was reconstructed by adjusting the observed emissions for the reductions attributed to measures already implemented, in order to represent a counterfactual situation without mitigation action. This revision provided a trajectory more closely aligned with historical trends and was a preparatory step towards the development of the NDC 3.0 reference scenario.

For the NDC 3.0, the reference scenario was established from 2022, the last year covered by the available national inventory, and projected to 2035. Its construction is based on a sectoral analysis of historical trends, supplemented by the main available macroeconomic, demographic and sectoral drivers. Projection methods were adapted to the characteristics of each sector and, where data or trends were insufficient, prudent assumptions were adopted. Given the uncertainties specific to the LULUCF sector, its net balance was projected in an aggregated and conservative manner. This approach aims to establish a trajectory consistent with the observed national dynamics, without overestimating the future evolution of emissions or removals.

Assessment of policies and measures

The assessment of the mitigation potential of the NDC 3.0 is based on an analysis of the policies and measures (P&M) identified by national stakeholders and on the estimation of their effects on GHG emissions by 2035. This approach made it possible to construct two mitigation trajectories: an unconditional scenario, corresponding to actions whose implementation is not dependent on additional international support and for which Chad intends to mobilise domestic public and private resources, and a conditional scenario, incorporating additional measures whose implementation depends on obtaining international support, notably financial, technological and capacity building support.

The policies and measures were identified and discussed at the technical workshop held in December 2025 with representatives of the sectoral ministries, national experts and international consultants. For each of them, the implementation assumptions were specified, notably the units concerned, current levels of deployment and the targets set for 2035. Following this workshop, the proposals underwent consolidation work in order to ensure their technical coherence, realism and level of ambition in light of the objectives of the NDC 3.0.

The quantification of emission reductions was carried out using the GACMO tool, already used during the development of the updated NDC (2021). The parameters of the mitigation options available in GACMO were adapted to reflect implementation assumptions specific to the national context. Emission reductions are estimated by comparing the trajectories of the mitigation scenarios with the reference scenario, constructed independently of GACMO. This methodological separation makes it possible to clearly distinguish assumptions about the natural evolution of emissions from the effects attributable to mitigation policies and measures.

The unconditional scenario mainly comprises actions on energy efficiency, renewable energy development, improvement of electricity grids, transport and waste management, as well as the implementation of the Kigali Amendment for the phase-down of hydrofluorocarbons (HFC). The conditional scenario complements these actions with more ambitious interventions in the agriculture, forestry, energy, fugitive emissions and waste sectors, whose implementation depends on the mobilisation of international finance and support.

The results obtained constitute a first estimate of the mitigation potential of the NDC 3.0. The potentials and associated costs are established at a strategic level and will be refined during the preparation and implementation of programmes and projects, as more detailed data and technical studies become available.

4.4 Adaptation

The development of the adaptation component of the NDC 3.0 was based on an update of the analysis of Chad’s climate vulnerabilities, supplemented by an assessment of the implementation of the adaptation measures included in the 2021 updated NDC. This approach aimed to draw lessons from the 2021-2026 period in order to identify the priorities to be pursued or strengthened and to better structure the monitoring of adaptation results under the NDC 3.0. The assessment covered the main adaptation sectors identified in the previous NDC, notably agriculture and livestock, forests and the environment, water and sanitation, health and nutrition, fisheries resources and aquaculture, education and communication, as well as risk management, infrastructure and land-use planning.

The analysis combined a desk review, field surveys and direct observations. The desk review drew in particular on national strategies and policies, monitoring and evaluation reports and the available information on interventions implemented since 2021. It was supplemented by exchanges with sectoral technical services, beneficiary communities, civil society organisations, research institutions and technical and financial partners. Approximately sixty interviews with key stakeholders and forty focus groups were thus organised in six provinces. The primary data collected in the field were supplemented by secondary data covering the entire national territory.

The vulnerability analysis was structured around climate hazards, exposure and vulnerability, in order to assess the differences between territories, sectors and population groups and to identify the main factors affecting their adaptive capacities. In parallel, the implementation of the measures set out in the 2021 updated NDC was assessed in terms of their relevance, coherence, effectiveness and sustainability. Efficiency could not be assessed systematically owing to limited access to financial data and detailed information on certain projects.

The results of these analyses were then used to define the sectoral adaptation orientations and objectives of the NDC 3.0. The measures of the previous NDC were thus compared against the observed vulnerabilities, the results of their implementation, the proposals made by communities and the sectoral recommendations arising from the interventions analysed. This consolidation also took into account the orientations of the national planning frameworks considered in the studies, notably the National Adaptation Plan and the National Development Plan.

Finally, this work was complemented by the structuring of a results framework for the period covered by the NDC 3.0, associating with the priority measures implementation and outcome indicators, the responsible entities and, where the available national data allow, baselines and targets for 2030 and 2035. Where the preliminary studies do not allow a sufficiently consolidated baseline, target or cost to be established, these elements remain to be specified in the course of implementation and the progressive strengthening of the national adaptation monitoring system.

5. Cross-cutting dimensions of climate action

Cross-cutting dimensions are an essential lever for strengthening the effectiveness, equity and sustainability of the implementation of NDC 3.0. Beyond the mitigation and adaptation objectives, they aim to integrate the social, economic and institutional issues that determine the success of climate action. Accordingly, this section highlights the main cross-cutting principles and approaches adopted by Chad to ensure inclusive and coherent implementation that generates co-benefits for sustainable development.

5.1 Gender

Climate change does not affect everyone in the same way. In Chad, women, girls and children bear a disproportionate share of its consequences, not because of any inherent vulnerability, but because of the structural inequalities and social roles that shape their daily lives. Integrating a gender perspective into climate action is both an ethical and a strategic imperative for Chad. Pre-existing gender inequalities are systematically exacerbated by climate change, while women, who play a central role in agricultural production, food security and the livelihoods of rural households, have structurally limited access to the productive resources, land and finance needed to cope with it. NDC 3.0 aligns with the orientations of the National Strategy on Gender and Climate Change 2025-2030, SNGCC (MEPDD, 2025), and translates them into criteria applicable to mitigation, adaptation, finance and monitoring actions.

Gender disparities remain very deep in Chad: the country is ranked 146th out of 148 countries in the Global Gender Gap Index (WEF, 2025) and 163rd out of 193 countries in the UNDP Gender Inequality Index (2024). Only 5% of women have a bank account and 11.8% have access to credit (ECOSIT 4, 2018-2019). Women represent 52% of the population but only 14% of senior managers and 10% of middle managers (DAI, 2021). These inequalities are aggravated by climate hazards and resource degradation. In particular, the increasing scarcity of water and fuelwood increases women’s workload, while declining agricultural incomes and pressures on resources heighten their economic and social vulnerability. Migration and displacement of men linked to climate pressures can also lead women to bear greater sole responsibility for the household, while crisis situations can increase the risks of gender-based violence and child marriage.

5.2 Just transition

Chad considers that the transition to a resilient, low greenhouse gas emission economy is an opportunity to diversify the economy, create decent jobs, strengthen national skills and improve the resilience of the population. To deliver these benefits sustainably, the transformation will need to be conducted in a just and inclusive manner, involving the workers, communities and territories concerned.

In this perspective, Chad relies on three pillars of a just transition – social dialogue, social protection and skills development – in coherence with the Guidelines for a just transition of the International Labour Organization (ILO) and with the National Development Plan “Tchad Connexion 2030” (PND 2024-2030).

The national socio-economic context underlines the scale of the challenges to be addressed. Around 44.8% of the population lives below the poverty line (World Bank, 2025) and 92.2% of employment is in the informal sector (ECOSIT 3 and 4). Social protection coverage remains very limited, particularly for informal workers and rural populations. Moreover, hydrocarbons account for 56% of public revenue (UNCTAD, 2025), which exposes public finances to market volatility and changes in demand.

The sectoral diagnostic carried out in 2026 in the energy, agriculture, livestock, pastoralism, forestry, water and employment sectors shows that alignment with the three pillars of the just transition is still partial. Social dialogue and skills development are generally assessed at a medium level, while social protection remains low to medium, particularly for rural, pastoral and informal workers.

Furthermore, Chad’s demographic dynamics constitute both a challenge and a potential for the implementation of NDC 3.0. The demographic dividend does not result mechanically from population growth: realising it requires strengthening human capital and creating the conditions that allow a predominantly young population to contribute fully to economic and social development.

Through its NDC 3.0, Chad commits to making just transition a cross-cutting principle of its climate policies, so that the benefits of the transformation are shared equitably and no population group is left behind. Particular attention will be paid to workers in exposed sectors, rural and pastoral communities, women, children, young people and vulnerable populations.

Beyond being considered as beneficiaries of the transition, young people are also essential actors in its implementation. Developing education, vocational skills and access to employment and entrepreneurship can strengthen their contribution to the transformations needed in the NDC priority sectors. Their participation in the development of renewable energy, the adoption of resilient agricultural and pastoral practices, the sustainable transformation of agri-food systems, natural resource management and climate services can thus contribute jointly to adaptation to climate change, the transition to a low-emission economy and the creation of sustainable economic opportunities.

To realise this ambition, Chad will focus its action on three complementary priorities:

  • Mitigate social risks, by preparing the professional retraining of workers in the extractive sector and the fuelwood value chain towards promising occupations, particularly in solar energy and grid management, and by applying social and land safeguards to reforestation and conservation projects, notably within the framework of the approach for reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation (REDD+).
  • Invest in green jobs and skills, through the updating of the National Employment and Vocational Training Policy (PNEFP, 2014), the mapping of green occupations in five priority value chains - solar, agroecology, natural resource management, resilient construction and environmental services - increased access of girls, adolescent girls and young women to training and jobs in the ecological and energy transition, as well as the progressive inclusion in energy concession contracts of social clauses setting targets for the use of a qualified national workforce.
  • Strengthen adaptive social protection, in particular by progressively extending the Adaptive and Productive Safety Nets Project (Republic of Chad, 2024) - financed with USD 100 million from the International Development Association (IDA) and USD 20 million from the Sahel Adaptive Social Protection Program (SASPP) - to nomadic pastoralists, seasonal workers and refugee host communities, while developing gender-responsive climate budgeting.

The implementation of these priorities will rely on a coordinated governance framework, led by the MEPDD in association with the ministries responsible for employment, vocational training, social protection, energy and the productive sectors, as well as with the social partners. Financial needs are estimated at around USD 117 million over the period 2026-2031. Their mobilisation will rely on a combination of national resources, international climate finance and contributions from technical and financial partners.

5.3 Climate–Peace–Security Nexus

Chad recognises the growing importance of the interactions between climate change, natural resource management, population mobility, peace and security. Rainfall variability, droughts and floods, as well as land degradation and the increasing scarcity of water and pasture, exert growing pressure on livelihoods and access to natural resources. In agro-pastoral areas, notably in Batha and Guéra, these dynamics can heighten tensions related to access to water, land and pasture, land disputes and the difficulties associated with transhumance.

Climate factors do not, however, constitute a cause of conflict on their own. Their effects on peace and security result from their interaction with other factors, including socio-economic conditions, demographic and mobility dynamics, natural resource governance, and the capacity of institutions and local mechanisms to prevent and resolve disputes. The consideration of the Climate–Peace–Security (CPS) nexus in NDC 3.0 thus aims to better anticipate these interactions and to promote climate interventions that contribute jointly to the resilience of populations, the sustainable management of resources and conflict prevention.

In this perspective, NDC 3.0 promotes a conflict-sensitive approach to climate action that is adapted to territorial realities. In particular, it prioritises the restoration of degraded land and pasture, improving and securing access to water, the concerted management of transhumance corridors, the strengthening of local conflict prevention and mediation mechanisms, and early warning systems integrating climate and security risks. These orientations complement the measures set out in the adaptation contribution, particularly in the sectors of agriculture and livestock, water, ecosystems, risk management and land-use planning. Tensions over water, pasture and pastoral mobility are moreover already identified among the consequences of climate vulnerabilities in the adaptation contribution.

The implementation of this approach will rely on inclusive governance involving national and territorial authorities, local communities, civil society organisations and actors involved in natural resource management and conflict prevention. Particular attention will be paid to the participation and specific needs of women, children and young people, displaced populations and host communities. NDC 3.0 also aims to progressively strengthen the integration of a CPS analysis into policies and major investments concerned by climate action.

Monitoring of the Climate–Peace–Security nexus will rely primarily on the indicators already included in the results framework of the adaptation contribution (section 7.5), notably those relating to the national early warning system, local mechanisms for preventing and resolving conflicts linked to natural resources, the restoration of land and pasture, and securing access to water and pastoral resources. These indicators will be analysed jointly in order to better assess the interactions between climate vulnerabilities, pressure on natural resources and the risks of tensions or conflicts.

5.4 Other cross-cutting dimensions

The elements presented in this sub-section are based mainly on the Analysis Report on the five cross-cutting dimensions and the document Indicators and Recommendations, prepared by the Centre de Recherche en Anthropologie et en Science Humaine (CRASH) on behalf of the MEPDD in 2026 as part of the preparation of NDC 3.0. This work constitutes the main documentary basis used for the dimensions relating to equity and social inclusion, human security and conflict prevention, human mobility, health and human rights.

Equity and social inclusion

Equity is a principle of the design and implementation of NDC 3.0. Climate programmes will incorporate, where relevant, social and territorial targeting criteria in order to improve access of vulnerable populations and territories to water, sustainable energy, means of production, climate services, finance and social protection schemes. The participation of local communities in decision-making and the valorisation of local adaptation knowledge will also be sought.

Monitoring will rely on data disaggregated, where available, by sex, age, territory, disability and forced displacement status (refugee, returnee, internally displaced person). It will examine in particular the territorial distribution of finance, the share of beneficiaries belonging to vulnerable groups, their access to essential services and their participation in climate governance mechanisms. Children and adolescents are accordingly a priority group for social targeting: age-disaggregated data, including for those under 18, will be systematically sought in order to document their access to essential services (water, health, nutrition, education, protection) and to direct climate investments towards the areas where their vulnerability is highest.

Human security and conflict prevention

NDC 3.0 integrates human security, social cohesion and conflict prevention issues into the climate actions implemented in territories where tensions over water, land, pasture and transhumance corridors may be heightened by climate and environmental pressures. Action will prioritise the concerted management of resources, local and traditional prevention and mediation mechanisms, securing transhumance corridors and water points, and strengthening early warning systems. Monitoring will focus in particular on trends in conflicts linked to natural resources, the functioning of local prevention committees, the securing of transhumance corridors, the participation of women and young people in peace mechanisms and the coverage of at-risk areas by warning systems.

Human mobility and migration

NDC 3.0 recognises human mobility as a strategic dimension of adaptation to climate change in Chad. Recurrent droughts, desertification, floods, land degradation, variability of water resources and growing pressure on natural resources directly affect household livelihoods, particularly in exposed agro-pastoral, Sahelian, lakeside and urban areas. IOM studies on human mobility in the context of climate change and environmental degradation, as well as the climate vulnerability analysis conducted as part of NDC 3.0, show that these dynamics contribute to various forms of seasonal, temporary, permanent or forced mobility, which can be both a household adaptation strategy and a factor of vulnerability when they are not anticipated, supported or integrated into territorial planning. These efforts will contribute to the implementation of the Kampala Ministerial Declaration on Migration, Environment and Climate Change.

By 2035, Chad intends to systematise the consideration of climate mobility in policies on adaptation, disaster risk reduction, land-use planning, sustainable natural resource management, adaptive social protection and local development. This approach aims to prevent forced displacement, reduce the risks of tensions related to access to resources, strengthen resilient livelihoods in areas of origin, improve access to essential services in host areas, secure transhumance and pastoral mobility, regulate any planned relocations according to a rights-based approach, support innovation led by adolescents and young people in vulnerable territories, develop disaggregated data on the migration-environment–climate nexus, and promote durable, inclusive solutions based on human rights, gender, protection and the participation of the communities concerned. Particular attention will be paid to the protection of unaccompanied or separated children and adolescents during climate-related displacement, and to maintaining their access to education, health and protection services in areas of origin, transit and destination, with regard to child protection.

Health and resilience of the health system

NDC 3.0 plans to strengthen the integration of climate risks into health policies and plans. Priorities include surveillance of climate-sensitive diseases, the development of health early warning systems, training of healthcare staff, community awareness-raising and strengthening the resilience of health facilities, notably through access to water, sanitation and reliable energy and through the preparation of risk and emergency management plans. Particular attention will be paid to the continuity of child health services (vaccination, nutrition, maternal and child health) during climate shocks, as well as to the climate resilience of health services for children. Likewise, attention will also be paid to nutrition, particularly among children and pregnant and breastfeeding women, whose vulnerability to climate shocks (droughts, floods) increases the risk of acute malnutrition.

Monitoring will draw primarily on existing national systems and will cover in particular the coverage of climate-health surveillance, the number of districts with a warning system, the proportion of health facilities with a climate risk management plan, access to water and energy, and the number of staff trained. This monitoring will include, as far as possible, age-disaggregated indicators in order to measure the impact of climate shocks on the health of children under five and the continuity of vaccination and nutrition services.

Education, climate vulnerability and climate action

Chad’s education system is highly exposed to the effects of climate change, particularly floods and extreme heat. According to the national loss and damage assessment covering the period 2019-2025, the education sector recorded economic losses estimated at 55 billion FCFA, mainly linked to floods. These impacts translate notably into the deterioration of school infrastructure, the interruption of teaching and an increased risk of school drop-out, particularly for children and the most vulnerable groups.

In this context, NDC 3.0 makes the resilience of the education system an important axis of adaptation. In particular, it plans to strengthen the resilience of school infrastructure, improve access to water, sanitation and learning conditions adapted to extreme heat, and strengthen the integration of climate change education into school and training curricula. Arrangements to ensure continuity of education and the protection of children during climate shocks will also be encouraged.

These interventions aim to reduce disruptions to schooling caused by climate hazards, strengthen the adaptive capacity of the education system and promote, among younger generations, the knowledge and skills needed for a society that is more resilient to climate change.

Human rights

NDC 3.0 applies an approach based on human rights, as well as children’s rights, to the design, implementation and monitoring of climate actions. This approach is based on equality, non-discrimination, equitable access to essential services, community participation and access to climate information. It also provides for strengthening the dissemination of climate texts and policies in accessible formats, the protection of vulnerable groups, the regulation of land rights and rights of access to resources, and the consideration of human rights in environmental and social impact assessments. This approach draws in particular on the Convention on the Rights of the Child, ratified by Chad, and ensures that the rights and best interests of the child are explicitly taken into account in the design, implementation and monitoring of climate actions.

Monitoring will rely on the progressive integration of dedicated indicators into national monitoring mechanisms and will examine in particular the access of affected populations to essential services, the participation of vulnerable groups in decision-making processes, the dissemination of climate information and the handling of documented cases of violations linked to climate projects.

6. Mitigation contribution

6.1 Summary

Chad’s NDC 3.0 is based on updated data, in particular a national greenhouse gas emissions inventory covering the period 2010–2022, the latter being the selected reference year.

In its NDC 3.0, Chad thus reaffirms and strengthens national mitigation ambition. By 2035, Chad aims for an unconditional reduction of 1.7% in net emissions relative to the reference scenario. Subject to international support in the form of finance, technology transfer and capacity building, an additional reduction of 19.6 percentage points would bring the total reduction to 21.2%. The corresponding emission levels are 128,299 ktCO₂e and 102,473 ktCO₂e respectively.

6.2 Greenhouse gas emissions profile

NDC coverage

The NDC covers GHG emissions from the national territory for the sectors of energy, industrial processes and product use (IPPU), agriculture, land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF), and waste. The inclusion of the IPPU sector in the inventory underpinning NDC 3.0 marks a notable improvement over previous versions, allowing almost complete coverage of national emissions.

NDC 3.0 is based on a GHG emissions inventory updated to 2022, whose scope has been broadened and whose use of national data has been strengthened. The GHGs covered by the inventory include CO₂, methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and hydrofluorocarbons (HFC). Perfluorocarbons (PFC) and sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) are not estimated in this edition of the inventory owing to the unavailability of sufficient data. Nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃) is not estimated as no emitting activities have been identified on the national territory.

Among these gases, methane and hydrofluorocarbons are also short-lived climate pollutants. The other pollutants in this family, notably black carbon and tropospheric ozone precursors, fall outside the UNFCCC accounting scope and are not quantified in this contribution. They will be addressed under the National Plan on Short-Lived Climate Pollutants and the National Methane Roadmap, the development of which is under way.

The 100-year global warming potentials from the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) are applied uniformly to the reference year, the BAU scenario and the mitigation scenarios.

The 2006 IPCC Guidelines are applied to all sectors covered. Fugitive emissions related to charcoal production have been added, following the method of the IPCC 2019 Refinement.

GHG emissions inventory

Maintaining and strengthening this absorption function is therefore an important issue for the mitigation contribution.

The waste sector

Emissions from the waste sector reach 1,997 ktCO₂e in 2022, an increase of 50% compared with 2010. They come mainly from solid waste disposal, open burning and wastewater treatment. This trend highlights the mitigation potential associated with improving waste collection, treatment and recovery.

6.3 Reference scenario

Methodological framework of the BAU projection scenario

The reference scenario (Business As Usual – BAU) is the projection scenario used to quantify the mitigation contribution of NDC 3.0. It represents the evolution of GHG emissions in the absence of new mitigation policies and measures implemented after 2022. The BAU thus includes policies adopted and implemented as of 31 December 2022. The projections are based on the national GHG emissions inventory, whose reference year is 2022, the latest year with consolidated data. They cover the period 2023–2035 and are based on an analysis of historical trends as well as the main assumptions regarding the country’s demographic, economic and sectoral development.

Under this scenario, national GHG emissions would increase continuously over the projection period, rising from 93,801 ktCO₂e in 2022 to 131,839 ktCO₂e in 2035, an increase of approximately 41%. This trend reflects the continuation of current development dynamics, characterised by population growth, increasing energy demand, the development of economic activities and growing needs for infrastructure and services.

The growth in emissions is driven mainly by the agriculture sector, which remains the largest contributor to national emissions throughout the period, owing to the importance of livestock and agricultural activities in the Chadian economy. The energy, industrial processes and product use (IPPU) and waste sectors also show sustained growth in their emissions, reflecting the gradual development of transport, energy access, refrigeration and air-conditioning uses, as well as the increase in waste generation linked to population growth and urbanisation.

Conversely, the land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF) sector remains a net carbon sink throughout the projection period. However, its absorption capacity gradually decreases, from –4,540 ktCO₂e in 2022 to – 2,520 ktCO₂e in 2035, as a result of land-use changes and increasing pressure on natural resources.

This reference trajectory reflects Chad’s main development dynamics, notably the growing needs for energy, mobility, infrastructure and services, as well as the continuing weight of the agricultural and pastoral sectors. It constitutes the basis used to assess the emission reductions associated with the mitigation policies and measures of NDC 3.0.

The action plan to 2030 also provides for protection actions aimed at avoiding deforestation on 877,000 ha and restoration actions on 50,000 ha. 6.6 Short-lived climate pollutants and sustainable cooling

Chad recognises the importance of reducing short-lived climate pollutants (SLCP), in particular methane (CH₄) and hydrofluorocarbons (HFC), to strengthen mitigation action while generating co-benefits, notably in terms of air quality. Several measures included in NDC 3.0 already contribute to reducing these emissions, in particular the reduction of associated gas flaring, the promotion of biodigesters, the improvement of certain agricultural practices and of waste management, as well as the phase-down of HFC consumption and emissions under the Kigali Amendment.

Chad has also joined the Global Methane Pledge (Global Methane Pledge), which collectively aims for a reduction of at least 30% in global methane emissions by 2030 compared with 2020. With the support of the Climate and Clean Air Coalition (CCAC), in 2026 Chad began developing a National Plan on short-lived climate pollutants and a National Methane Roadmap. Their finalisation is planned for 2027. These instruments will make it possible to consolidate knowledge of emission sources, assess reduction potentials and costs, identify priority measures and define the indicators needed to monitor them. Black carbon, which is an aerosol and not a greenhouse gas covered by the quantified targets of the NDC, will also be taken into account in this work, notably with regard to its effects on the climate and air quality.

As the results of this work were not available when the present contribution was finalised, they are not included in the quantification of its mitigation targets. They will nevertheless constitute complementary instruments for the implementation of NDC 3.0 and may feed into the progressive updating of the relevant policies and measures. They therefore do not, at this stage, modify the quantified mitigation targets presented in the present contribution.

Sustainable cooling is also a growing issue, at the interface between mitigation and adaptation, in a context of increasing cooling needs due to extreme heat. The development of sustainable cooling solutions must make it possible to meet these needs while limiting the emissions associated with energy consumption and the use of refrigerants with a high global warming potential. To this end, NDC 3.0 provides for the deployment of highly energy-efficient air-conditioning and refrigeration equipment and the continued phase-down of HFC in accordance with the Kigali Amendment. These orientations will be complemented by the promotion of passive cooling solutions and, where technically appropriate, of refrigerants with a low global warming potential.

The implementation of these orientations will be accompanied by the building of national capacities, in particular those of refrigeration technicians and customs services, as well as by improved monitoring of equipment and refrigerants. The development of a National Action Plan for sustainable cooling will make it possible to progressively structure this action and to specify the priority measures, the financing and capacity-building needs and the monitoring indicators, consistent with the implementation of the Kigali Amendment and the objectives of NDC 3.0.

6.7 Financial needs

The implementation of all the mitigation measures selected in NDC 3.0 requires investments estimated at approximately 7.16 billion USD by 2035. These needs are mainly concentrated in the LULUCF and energy sectors, which together account for nearly 99% of the total estimated amount. The conditional contribution represents approximately 6.73 billion USD, i.e. 94% of the estimated investments, while the needs associated with the unconditional contribution are estimated at approximately 435 million USD.

These estimates are orders of magnitude intended to inform the programming and mobilisation of the investments needed to achieve the mitigation contribution. The breakdown of needs by sector, their distribution between conditional and unconditional contributions, as well as the methods and limitations of their estimation, are presented in chapter 9 on the means of implementation of this contribution.

7. Adaptation contribution

Adaptation is a central priority of Chad’s NDC 3.0, given the country’s high exposure to climate change and the vulnerability of its populations, its productive systems and its ecosystems. The contribution for 2035 is based on an updated analysis of risks and vulnerabilities, the lessons learned from the implementation of the 2021 updated NDC and the definition of priority measures accompanied by a results and monitoring framework.

7.1 Priority climate risks and vulnerabilities

Evolution of the main climate hazards

Chad is experiencing a progressive rise in temperatures and an intensification of extreme heat episodes. For the hot season, maximum temperatures could increase by 1.0 to 1.7°C by 2030 under Representative Concentration Pathways (Representative Concentration Pathway – RCP) 4.5 and RCP 8.5, then by 1.7 to 3°C by 2050. By then, the number of days exceeding 40°C could reach 60 to more than 100 days per year across large parts of the territory (UNDP, 2026).

Precipitation projections remain more uncertain. They nevertheless converge towards increased variability, a possible intensification of extreme precipitation, more frequent dry spells and an increased risk of flooding. Surveys conducted in 2026 confirm that the phenomena most frequently reported by communities are floods, excessive heat, delayed rains, drought and strong winds. In 2024, floods hit all 23 provinces of the country, affected approximately 1.9 million people and caused 576 deaths according to the data used in the assessment.

Climate hazards show significant territorial disparities: floods particularly affect riverside and urban areas as well as certain agricultural territories, while the risks related to drought, extreme heat and land degradation are more pronounced in the Saharan, Sahelian and agro-pastoral zones. These disparities call for territorially based implementation of adaptation, taking into account agroclimatic zones, exposure profiles and local capacities.

By way of illustration, the conditions associated with the El Niño episode observed in 2026 underline the importance of strengthening national capacities for anticipating and managing climate risks.

Sectoral and territorial vulnerabilities

Agriculture and livestock are the sectors most directly exposed to climate hazards. The consultations carried out in 2026 identify declining agricultural yields, disrupted cropping calendars, crop destruction, pasture degradation, livestock mortality and diseases, and increasing water scarcity as recurrent impacts. These effects also have repercussions on food security, household incomes, pastoral mobility and tensions over access to resources (UNDP, 2026).

In the water and sanitation sector, rainfall variability and droughts affect the availability of the resource, while floods degrade water quality, damage infrastructure and increase health risks. The communities consulted report in particular the drying up of wells, the lowering of the water table and the disappearance of some water points. These constraints affect domestic, agricultural and pastoral uses and exacerbate conflicts over use (UNDP, 2026).

Forests and ecosystems suffer the combined effects of drought, high temperatures, fires and anthropogenic pressures. The degradation of vegetation cover, erosion and the decline of ecosystem services reduce the capacity of territories to absorb shocks. In fishing and aquaculture areas, water fluctuations, the degradation of aquatic habitats and pressure on stocks affect the livelihoods of communities that depend on these resources (UNDP, 2026).

Infrastructure and human settlements remain particularly sensitive to floods, erosion, silting and extreme heat. Damage to roads, bridges, buildings, hydraulic structures and drainage networks disrupts mobility and access to essential services. Increasing urban exposure and the lack of infrastructure sized for future risks reinforce the need to move from an emergency response approach to an approach based on prevention, territorial planning and resilient investment (UNDP, 2026).

Climate vulnerabilities also differ across social groups and their adaptive capacities. These issues, developed in chapter 5, are integrated in a cross-cutting manner into the adaptation contribution and its monitoring framework (UNDP, 2026).

Socio-economic impacts and cost of inaction

Chad’s climate vulnerability directly induces economic vulnerability. Over the 2022-2024 period, livestock accounted for approximately 20.2% of national value added, agriculture 12.0% and forestry and fishing 4.9%. The primary sector also accounted for approximately 71.7% of employment in 2025. This structure directly exposes growth, employment, food security and household incomes to shocks affecting natural resources and agropastoral systems (UNDP, 2026).

At the national level, the estimates used in the macroeconomic assessment of the updated NDC indicate that, in the absence of adaptation, gross domestic product (GDP) losses related to climate change could reach approximately 4.2% of annual GDP in 2050 under an optimistic and wet scenario, and up to 10.5% under a pessimistic scenario, compared with a reference trajectory without climate change. Agriculture and livestock concentrate a significant share of the risks, notably through yield losses, reduced animal production, water stress and the degradation of pastoral resources (UNDP, 2026).

These estimates reinforce the economic case for adaptation, but must be interpreted with caution, as some are based on regional extrapolations in the absence of consolidated national data. They are therefore used as orders of magnitude to guide and prioritise action, rather than as an exhaustive estimate of future losses.

7.2 Review and lessons learned from the implementation of the 2021 updated NDC

Main achievements

The assessment of the updated NDC highlights several positive results of the measures undertaken since 2021. In agriculture, the dissemination of improved seeds and short-cycle varieties, the development of market gardening and of certain irrigation systems have contributed, in the intervention areas, to improving production, diversifying incomes and strengthening food availability. The continuation of certain activities by producer, women’s and youth associations after the end of support also demonstrates local ownership of these interventions (UNDP, 2026).

In the water and sanitation sector, the construction and rehabilitation of boreholes, notably equipped with solar pumps, have locally improved access to water and supported certain productive uses. In 2023, Community-Led Total Sanitation notably enabled the certification of 768 villages as open defecation free, covering more than 454,000 people (UNDP, 2026).

Progress has also been noted in land and landscape restoration. Between 2021 and 2025, the interventions identified by the study made it possible to restore more than 30,000 ha of agricultural and pastoral land, to produce and plant more than 4 million seedlings and to reach more than 470,000 beneficiaries in 18 provinces. Another project enabled the restoration of approximately 4,000 ha around Lake Chad. However, monitoring of plantation survival rates and of the effective restoration of ecosystem functions still needs to be strengthened (UNDP, 2026).

The early warning system has also been strengthened. The Climate Risk and Early Warning Systems (CREWS) project notably contributed to the installation of 70 weather stations, 15 hydrological stations and 215 rain gauges, as well as to the digitisation of 80% of the national climatological archives. In 2025, 248 warning bulletins were issued by the National Meteorological Agency (ANAM). These achievements provide a basis for further extending territorial coverage and improving the dissemination of warnings down to communities (UNDP, 2026).

Finally, nearly 780,000 people, i.e. approximately 4% of the population, benefited from social safety net schemes in 2025. In the fisheries and aquaculture sectors, the experiences identified in fish farming, spirulina production and the organisation of producers also show encouraging results, but their coverage remains limited (UNDP, 2026).

Limitations and lessons for NDC 3.0

Despite these achievements, the implementation of the updated NDC remains characterised by strong fragmentation. Many interventions remain concentrated in accessible areas or within project perimeters, with insufficient coverage of several vulnerable territories. The assessment also highlights limited complementarity between actors, duplication, weak capitalisation of achievements and still only partial involvement of local authorities and decentralised services.

Implementation capacities are a major constraint. Both studies point to shortcomings in human resources, access to data, technical extension, equipment maintenance and monitoring and evaluation. In budgetary terms, the execution of resources allocated to adaptation priorities remains variable across sectors and years, underlining the importance of strengthening capacities for programming, executing and monitoring adaptation investments.

The sustainability of interventions remains uneven. Actions that combine local ownership, direct economic benefits and technologies adapted to community capacities tend to be better maintained, while the absence of maintenance arrangements or institutional anchoring undermines their durability. In the areas of infrastructure and risk management, emergency responses also remain predominant compared with long-term structural investments.

Finally, the measurement of progress remains limited by the absence of consolidated reference scenarios, weak financial tracking of adaptation and the incomplete nature of the MRV system. The assessment recommends consolidating statistical bases, improving coordination with the National Institute of Statistics, Economic and Demographic Studies (INSEED) and sectoral ministries, establishing a single statistical file and territorialising monitoring. (UNDP, 2026; UNDP, 2026).

Consequently, NDC 3.0 prioritises the consolidation, scaling up and territorialisation of the most relevant measures, rather than a proliferation of new actions. It also aims to strengthen measurable results, the functionality of investments, their maintenance and their effective contribution to reducing vulnerability.

7.3 Vision and objectives of the adaptation contribution for 2035

National vision

By 2035, Chad sustainably strengthens the resilience of populations, productive systems, ecosystems and territories to the current and future impacts of climate change, by giving priority to the most vulnerable areas and groups, improving access to essential services and resources and integrating adaptation into development policies and investments.

This vision is in continuity with the priorities identified in the updated NDC, the National Adaptation Plan and the development documents used by the preliminary studies for the preparation of the adaptation component of this contribution. It is based on sectoral interventions complemented by cross-cutting measures on social protection, climate information, education, climate-peace-security and the governance of natural resources.

Strategic objectives

The adaptation contribution is organised around six strategic objectives that summarise national priorities:

  • Strengthen the resilience of agricultural, pastoral, fisheries and food systems to climate variability, droughts, floods and land degradation;
  • Sustainably secure access to water, sanitation and essential services by strengthening the availability, quality, functionality and resilience of infrastructure;
  • Preserve and restore ecosystems, land, forests, pastures and aquatic resources in order to maintain ecosystem services and livelihoods;
  • Reduce the exposure of populations, infrastructure and territories to climate risks through prevention, early warning and the integration of risk into planning;
  • Strengthen the adaptive capacities of populations and institutions through climate information, education, social protection, the inclusion of women and youth and the prevention of conflicts related to natural resources.

7.4 Adaptation priorities and measures for 2035

The adaptation contribution translates these objectives into a set of priority measures to be implemented or strengthened by 2035. These were defined on the basis of the identified vulnerabilities, the lessons from the implementation of the 2021 updated NDC and the sectoral priorities documented in the preparatory work.

In the agriculture and livestock sector, the priorities mainly aim to strengthen the resilience of production systems through improved soil and water management, the dissemination of adapted practices and equipment, the development of irrigation, agroforestry and fodder resources, as well as improved management of pastoral areas and resources.

In the forestry, environment and ecosystems sectors, action focuses primarily on the restoration of degraded land and landscapes, the strengthening of vegetation cover, the sustainable management of natural resources and the development of activities that simultaneously contribute to the resilience of ecosystems and livelihoods.

For water and sanitation, the measures aim to secure and facilitate access to the resource, strengthen the resilience of water, hygiene and sanitation infrastructure in communities, schools and health centres, improve knowledge and monitoring of water resources and their quality, and develop sanitation and drainage capacities in the face of climate risks.

With regard to risk management, infrastructure and land-use planning, the priority is to strengthen prevention and anticipation, notably through improved early warning systems, the reduction of the exposure of populations and infrastructure and better consideration of climate risks in territorial planning.

In the area of fisheries resources and aquaculture, interventions aim to strengthen the sustainability of resource exploitation, develop more resilient aquaculture practices and support the associated value chains.

Finally, several enabling factors accompany these sectoral priorities, notably access to energy for certain uses contributing to adaptation, the strengthening of information and knowledge, adaptive social protection and the improvement of institutional and monitoring capacities. Particular attention is also paid to the resilience of the education system to climate shocks, notably through strengthening the resilience of schools and educational continuity arrangements to limit interruptions to schooling and the risk of dropping out during floods, heatwaves and other extreme climate events. Dimensions relating to gender, equity, health, human mobility and conflict prevention are integrated in a cross-cutting manner and developed in chapter 5.

The corresponding measures, indicators, baseline situations, implementing entities and targets are consolidated in the results framework presented in the following section.

7.6 Monitoring, evaluation and learning for adaptation

Adaptation monitoring must make it possible to distinguish between needs, the implementation of actions and the results achieved in terms of resilience. The source studies converge on the need to strengthen baseline data, the coordination of data producers, the upward flow of information from the provinces and the link between operational monitoring and financial monitoring.

Monitoring of risks and adaptation needs

The first level of monitoring will focus on changes in climate risks, exposure and sectoral and territorial vulnerabilities. It will draw in particular on meteorological and hydrological data, information on water resources, agricultural yields, pastures, infrastructure, health, food security and vulnerable groups. The objective is to periodically update adaptation needs and adjust the geographical targeting of interventions.

Implementation monitoring

The second level is based on the indicators of the results framework: areas covered by resilient practices, functional infrastructure, water points, warning systems, beneficiaries, governance mechanisms, social protection schemes, technology adoption and other output and outcome indicators. Monitoring should, as far as possible, disaggregate data by sex, age, territory and vulnerability group where statistical systems allow.

Monitoring of results and effectiveness

The third level will aim to measure the effectiveness of adaptation beyond the mere volume of activities. It will progressively favour indicators relating to the functionality of equipment, the adoption of practices, the stability of yields and livelihoods, the reduction of exposure, the effective restoration of ecosystems, water availability, warning coverage and the reduction of vulnerabilities. This shift is essential in order to move from project monitoring to monitoring of adaptation results.

Institutional responsibilities, the architecture of the national transparency system and the detailed arrangements for data collection, consolidation and reporting are developed in chapter 10 on the governance, monitoring and transparency framework.

8. Loss and damage

Distinct from mitigation and adaptation, but closely linked to risk management and resilience, loss and damage now constitutes an issue in its own right in Chad’s climate action. The country frames its action within Article 8 of the Paris Agreement, in connection with the Warsaw International Mechanism, the Santiago Network and the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage. This orientation is based on a national assessment of climate-related loss and damage over the 2019-2025 period, conducted using the international Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA) methodology.

This assessment highlights the growing scale of climate impacts: cumulative damage, economic losses and non-economic losses over the 2019-2025 period are estimated at 6,886 billion FCFA. The year 2024 alone accounts for 2,908 billion FCFA in losses and nearly 1.93 million people affected. Floods account for 70.35% of the losses observed, ahead of droughts (18.89%) and land erosion or degradation (6.86%). Overall recovery needs associated with the impacts observed over 2019-2025 are estimated at around 8,607 billion FCFA. These recovery needs do not constitute additional losses, but rather an estimate of the resources required for the reconstruction and recovery of the affected sectors. The effects are particularly pronounced in the provinces of Lac, Tandjilé, Mandoul, Mayo-Kebbi Est and Moyen-Chari, where high exposure to flooding, the weight of agropastoral activities and weak protective infrastructure combine.

Given the scale of these medium- and long-term needs, Chad plans an initial operational roadmap for the 2026-2028 period. With an estimated cost of 270 billion FCFA over 36 months, this roadmap does not aim to cover all the loss, damage and recovery needs estimated at national level. It constitutes an initial priority programme intended to strengthen the country’s institutional, financial, technical and operational capacities to prevent, document and respond to loss and damage. This roadmap is structured around four strategic priorities:

  • Mobilisation and governance of finance: establishment of a national mechanism facilitating access to the resources of the Fund for responding to Loss and Damage and creation of a National Climate Resilience Fund (Fonds National de Résilience Climatique) in order to channel finance and strengthen crisis response capacity.
  • Observation and early warning: strengthening the capacities of the national meteorological service and deploying an early warning system to anticipate climate events and reduce their human and economic consequences.
  • Knowledge, monitoring and transparency: creation of a national platform for monitoring loss and damage, under the coordination of INSEED and MEPDD, and systematic integration of the corresponding data into the MRV system of NDC 3.0.
  • Institutional framework and recovery: development of a framework law on the assessment of climate loss and damage and implementation of a national programme for the restoration of degraded land, with the objective of reducing environmental damage by 30% by 2030. This priority will integrate the early recovery of essential basic social services — health, water and sanitation, education — for children and the most vulnerable communities, in line with the orientation adopted for the 2026-2028 loss and damage roadmap.

The implementation of this roadmap may draw in particular on the available international technical assistance arrangements, especially the Santiago Network for loss and damage, in order to support the strengthening of national capacities, the improvement of data systems and the development of methodologies suited to the national context.

9.2 Capacity building and technology transfer

Mitigation

Beyond the mobilisation of financial resources, the implementation of Chad’s mitigation contribution requires a sustained strengthening of institutional, technical and operational capacities, as well as increased access to technologies suited to the national context. These needs concern all priority mitigation sectors and relate to the production and management of data, the planning of sectoral pathways, the design and implementation of measures, the monitoring of their results and the mobilisation of the finance required for their deployment.

Institutional, technical and sectoral capacity building

Strengthening data collection, management and analysis capacities is a cross-cutting need for the implementation and monitoring of the mitigation contribution. This involves in particular consolidating data currently spread across different actors, harmonising collection and reporting procedures and strengthening the capacities required to monitor emissions and the reductions achieved. The development of MRV systems will thus need to accompany the implementation of sectoral measures and help improve the availability, quality and traceability of the information required to monitor NDC 3.0.

In the energy sector, needs relate primarily to improving data, planning and monitoring systems, required in particular for establishing the energy balance and the GHG emissions inventory. The strengthening of analytical capacities and technical skills will need to accompany the deployment of renewable energy, mini-grids, storage and energy efficiency measures, as well as the installation, operation and maintenance of the associated equipment.

In the agricultural sector, needs mainly concern strengthening the skills of producers and technical services for the dissemination and uptake of practices that help reduce emissions and improve the sustainability of production systems. They relate in particular to climate-smart agriculture, soil and water conservation, agroecology and agroforestry, as well as to strengthening monitoring and evaluation and MRV capacities. Several of these needs are common to adaptation and mitigation objectives.

In the forestry and land use sector, priorities concern improving forest inventories and carbon data, monitoring deforestation and restoration, and developing capacities in remote sensing, geographic information systems and MRV. These capacities will need to support landscape restoration, assisted natural regeneration, reforestation and sustainable land management, by involving local communities and strengthening the skills required to develop sustainable value chains.

In the waste sector, needs mainly relate to improving data on waste generation, collection and treatment and to monitoring the associated emissions. Strengthening the capacities of local authorities and local actors will need in particular to support the development of MRV and the preparation and implementation of projects for waste recovery, composting and the reduction of emissions from waste.

Priority technology needs

The technology needs associated with the mitigation contribution concern both technologies that directly reduce or avoid emissions and the tools required for their planning, monitoring and operation.

In the energy sector, technology needs mainly concern solar equipment, storage systems, mini-grids and hybrid systems, as well as the equipment required for the development and management of electricity grids. Their deployment will need to be accompanied by the development of the national skills required for their installation, operation and maintenance.

In agriculture, the technologies identified include in particular improved seeds, water-efficient irrigation systems, low-emission agricultural equipment, solar pumps and dryers and biodigesters. Digital, climate and geospatial tools also constitute means of supporting planning and decision-making.

For forests and land use, needs relate mainly to remote sensing, geographic information systems and tools for monitoring forest resources, deforestation, restoration and carbon, in order to support both the sustainable management of resources and the monitoring of results.

In the waste sector, technology needs mainly concern recovery and composting solutions as well as technologies to reduce emissions associated with landfills and waste burning. Their deployment will need to be accompanied by data and monitoring systems to measure their results.

The progressive development of advanced digital tools, including solutions based on artificial intelligence, could also help strengthen national capacities for the production, analysis and use of climate data. In addition to remote sensing, geographic information systems and observation data, these tools could in particular support risk mapping and anticipation, the monitoring of land use and restoration, water resources and pastures, as well as the use of the data required to monitor the NDC. Their deployment will, however, need to be adapted to national capacities and be accompanied by skills development, improved data structuring and quality, and partnerships with research institutions, universities and the private sector.

Cross-cutting capacities for financing and implementation

Beyond sectoral needs, the implementation of the mitigation contribution requires strengthening national capacities in climate finance and in the preparation of projects capable of mobilising finance. Needs relate in particular to the development of bankable projects, access to international financing mechanisms and strengthening the skills of the public and private actors involved in their preparation and implementation.

The development of the cooperative approaches provided for in Article 6 of the Paris Agreement also requires continuing to put in place the necessary institutional, regulatory and technical capacities. The needs identified concern in particular the development of MRV guidelines, the training of public and private actors and the identification of pilot projects capable of mobilising climate finance.

Conditions for the deployment and uptake of technologies

Capacity building and access to technologies will need to be considered jointly. The deployment of new technologies must in particular be accompanied by the development of the national skills required for their installation, operation, maintenance and inspection, as well as the capacities to monitor their performance and their contribution to the NDC objectives.

The link between technology transfer and capacity building is essential to ensure not only access to the solutions required for the transition, but also their uptake, operation and long-term maintenance.

Adaptation

Beyond the mobilisation of financial resources, the effective implementation of Chad’s adaptation contribution depends on the sustained strengthening of the institutional, technical and operational capacities of the actors responsible for its implementation, as well as on access to technologies suited to the country’s climatic, socio-economic and territorial contexts. The preparatory work for NDC 3.0 highlights needs concerning both the planning and monitoring of adaptation and the capacity of sectoral and territorial actors to deploy, operate and maintain the selected solutions.

Institutional, technical and territorial capacity building

The assessment of the implementation of the 2021 updated NDC reveals persistent constraints in terms of institutional coordination, technical capacities, monitoring of interventions and data availability. Adaptation actions have often been implemented through one-off projects, with variable territorial coverage and continuity. The study also highlights the still limited capacities of certain deconcentrated structures and the need for better involvement of territorial actors in adaptation planning and monitoring (UNDP, 2026).

Capacity building should therefore focus primarily on the planning, programming, coordination and monitoring and evaluation of adaptation interventions. Sectoral ministries and their deconcentrated structures will in particular need the capacities required to report on the indicators selected in the NDC results framework, establish or update baselines, track progress and document the results of interventions. These needs are particularly important given the statistical gaps identified in several sectors and the absence of baseline values for some of the indicators proposed in the preparatory work for NDC 3.0.

Needs also concern the strengthening of the sectoral and local technical capacities required to deploy the priority measures. In agriculture and livestock, they relate in particular to resilient production practices, the management of water and pastoral resources, agroforestry and veterinary services. In the water sector, priorities concern the monitoring and management of surface and groundwater resources, as well as the operation and maintenance of infrastructure and water quality monitoring. For forests and ecosystems, needs relate mainly to land restoration, plantation monitoring and the sustainable use of non-timber forest products.

The maintenance and sustainability of investments constitute a cross-cutting need. The assessment of the previous NDC shows that insufficient upkeep and monitoring after project completion can limit the durability of results, particularly for water infrastructure, equipment and restoration operations. Strengthening local skills for the operation, upkeep and maintenance of equipment must therefore accompany its deployment to prevent adaptation from relying solely on one-off investments.

Finally, capacity building should also benefit local authorities, community organisations, producers and other local actors who participate directly in the implementation of measures. The experiences analysed show in particular the importance of local ownership in ensuring the continuity of certain practices beyond the duration of projects.

Priority technology needs

Needs concern first and foremost the production of and access to climate and hydrological information. The progress already made in terms of meteorological and hydrological stations, rainfall measurement, data digitisation and the production of warning bulletins constitutes a foundation to be consolidated. Strengthening the coverage of observation systems, data processing and the dissemination of information to users and exposed communities remains essential for risk prevention and decision-making.

In the agricultural and pastoral sectors, technology needs relate in particular to adapted seeds and varieties, irrigation and water control systems, soil and water conservation equipment, as well as agroforestry solutions, fodder production and storage and pastoral water infrastructure. The available studies highlight the value of several of these solutions for strengthening resilience and, for some, generating economic benefits (UNDP, 2026 ; UNDP, 2026).

In the water sector, needs mainly concern infrastructure for water mobilisation, storage and distribution, sanitation and drainage systems and resource monitoring equipment. Solar pumping solutions also constitute an option to support access to water and certain productive uses, subject to sustainable management of the resource and sufficient operation and maintenance capacities (UNDP, 2026 ; UNDP, 2026).

Climate risk management also requires strengthening early warning systems, observation networks and risk mapping and information tools, as well as developing infrastructure better adapted to climate hazards, particularly in terms of drainage and flood protection. In this regard, the lessons from the previous NDC highlight the need to progressively favour structural and sustainable risk reduction solutions rather than a response relying mainly on one-off emergency interventions.

For ecosystems and natural resources, technology needs relate mainly to land and landscape restoration, monitoring of vegetation and plantations and the sustainable use of natural resources. In the fisheries sector, they concern in particular the development of more resilient aquaculture, fish farming equipment and improved sustainable management of aquatic resources (UNDP, 2026 ; UNDP, 2026).

Conditions for deployment and ownership

Finally, the available studies show that access to a technology does not, on its own, guarantee a lasting adaptation outcome. Its deployment must be accompanied by training, technical assistance, maintenance arrangements, performance monitoring and ownership by local users. This link between technologies, capacities and governance is particularly important for water infrastructure, irrigation systems, solar equipment, early warning systems and ecosystem restoration measures.

Capacity building and access to technologies should therefore be designed as integrated components of the implementation of the priority measures presented in the adaptation results framework, and not as stand-alone interventions. They should contribute to scaling up relevant solutions, to their long-term functioning and to improving national and territorial capacity to plan, monitor and adjust adaptation action.

The preparatory work for NDC 3.0 made it possible to identify the main categories of capacity-building and technology needs required for the implementation of the mitigation and adaptation contributions. However, it highlights the need to continue, over the coming years, their characterisation and prioritisation, in particular in order to establish more precisely the capacities and technologies available, the needs still to be covered, their level of priority, their costs and their deployment timeline. This work may be deepened as part of the operationalisation of NDC 3.0 and of future sectoral programmes and projects, in conjunction with the institutions and stakeholders concerned. It should make it possible to progressively translate the identified needs into capacity-building programmes and targeted technology investments, adapted to national and territorial realities and directly linked to the priority measures of the NDC.

9.3 Mobilisation of climate finance and use of Article 6 mechanisms

In order to support the implementation of its mitigation actions, Chad intends to mobilise all available sources of finance, in particular bilateral and multilateral partners, the financial mechanisms of the UNFCCC and the opportunities offered by the cooperative approaches provided for under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, whether market-based mechanisms (Articles 6.2 and 6.4) or non-market approaches (Article 6.8).

In this perspective, Chad envisages, subject to a national decision, an operational legal and institutional framework and an analysis of compatibility with the achievement of the NDC, using part of the emission reductions and removals generated by programmes and projects implemented on its territory, in particular in the land, agriculture, forestry, renewable energy and waste management sectors. This approach should help mobilise additional finance for the implementation of the NDC while preserving the environmental integrity of emission reductions.

The development of these approaches will be based on compliance with the rules adopted under the Paris Agreement, in particular with regard to transparency, environmental integrity, avoidance of double counting, treatment of permanence and leakage risks and contribution to sustainable development. To this end, Chad will continue to progressively strengthen its institutional framework, its measurement, reporting and verification systems and the capacities needed for effective participation in the international cooperation mechanisms provided for under Article 6.

10. Governance, monitoring and transparency framework

10.1 Institutional arrangements

The implementation of NDC 3.0 is based on a governance arrangement involving all the public institutions concerned, under the coordination of the Ministry of Environment, Fisheries and Sustainable Development (MEPDD). This governance aims to ensure the coherence of climate policies, the coordination of sectoral interventions and regular monitoring of the progress made in implementing the NDC and Chad’s relevant obligations under the Paris Agreement.

The MEPDD provides overall coordination of NDC implementation, in conjunction with the competent sectoral ministries, in particular those responsible for agriculture, livestock, energy, water, transport, forests, industry, land-use planning and waste. Each of these ministries remains responsible for the planning, implementation and monitoring of the measures falling within its area of competence.

This governance also relies on the national data-producing institutions, in particular ANAM and INSEED, local authorities and the public bodies concerned. The private sector, civil society organisations, Indigenous Peoples, women’s and youth organisations as well as technical and financial partners also contribute to the implementation and monitoring of the NDC through national consultation mechanisms.

The consultations held during the preparation of NDC 3.0 nevertheless highlighted that institutional coordination still remains largely dependent on international reporting deadlines. In order to strengthen the sustainability of the arrangement, Chad intends to consolidate a permanent coordination framework, based on clearly defined responsibilities, formalised data-sharing mechanisms and regular collaboration among all the institutions concerned.

10.2 National climate monitoring and transparency system

In order to meet the requirements of the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) of the Paris Agreement, Chad is continuing to establish a national climate transparency system, whose scope covers GHG inventories, tracking of progress made in implementing the NDC, adaptation and support, going beyond MRV monitoring of climate actions alone.

This system is based on the designation, within each ministry and data-producing institution, of climate transparency focal points responsible for the collection, verification and regular transmission of information according to harmonised procedures. Sectoral data will feed into a national climate transparency platform, intended to ensure their centralisation, archiving, quality control and security before their processing at national level.

To ensure the sustainability of this arrangement, Chad plans to create the AN MNV, whose draft law is being finalised. This agency will be the permanent institution responsible for coordinating the national climate transparency system and producing the reports submitted to the UNFCCC, in particular the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR), National Communications and other international reporting obligations.

The Agency will in particular be responsible for:

  • coordination of the national climate transparency system;
  • consolidation and analysis of climate data;
  • quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) of the information produced;
  • monitoring the implementation of the NDC and of adaptation actions;
  • preparation of national reports submitted to the UNFCCC.

Advisory committees bringing together the administrations concerned, scientific experts, the private sector, civil society organisations, Indigenous Peoples, women and youth will support this arrangement in order to strengthen the scientific quality, transparency and national ownership of the information produced.

This system as a whole will help improve the quality of national climate data, strengthen the credibility of international reporting and facilitate Chad’s access to climate finance by ensuring transparent, consistent and verifiable information.

10.3 Tracking NDC progress and continuous improvement

Monitoring of NDC implementation will be part of a continuous process making it possible to measure the progress made, guide public decisions and feed into national and international reporting exercises. The current arrangement, under the coordination of the DLCC, relies in particular on the MRV Steering Committee established in 2024 and on sectoral focal points. The capacities of the latter are being progressively strengthened, notably with the support of the CBIT project. The work undertaken under the ICAT-supported project is also helping to consolidate the tools, coordination mechanisms, data management and quality, and the information-sharing arrangements needed for the sustainable functioning of the MRV system.

In this context, NDC monitoring will progressively be based on four complementary functions:

1. Collection of sectoral data, carried out by the climate transparency focal points from the competent administrations and institutions; 2. Centralisation and control of data within the national climate transparency platform, including consistency, completeness and quality checks; 3. Analysis, consolidation and quality assurance; 4. Production and validation of national reports, including national greenhouse gas inventories, tracking of NDC implementation indicators, and information on adaptation, climate finance, technology transfer and capacity building, before their submission to the UNFCCC in accordance with the Enhanced Transparency Framework.

The system will make it possible to regularly track the progress made in implementing Chad’s commitments, in line with the principles of transparency, accuracy, completeness, consistency and comparability provided for by the Enhanced Transparency Framework. It will thus contribute directly to the preparation of the Biennial Transparency Reports (BTR).

Beyond international reporting, the information produced will feed into national planning, public policy monitoring, the programming of climate investments and future revisions of the NDC, with a view to the continuous improvement of climate policies.

The period 2026-2028 should make it possible to consolidate the architecture and capacities of the existing arrangement, in particular through strengthening sectoral focal points, improving data-sharing and data quality control mechanisms and developing the national climate transparency platform. It will also make it possible to continue reflection on the institutional and technical evolution of the system.

In this perspective, the establishment of a National Agency dedicated to MRV could, in time, strengthen technical coordination, data consolidation and quality, and the management of the national transparency platform. Its institutional anchoring, mandate and operating arrangements will nevertheless remain subject to the validation and decision-making process at national level. The objective is to have, from 2029, a fully operational national transparency system, capable of producing the BTRs autonomously and regularly and of durably integrating climate indicators into the national statistical system coordinated by INSEED.

11. Conclusion

NDC 3.0 marks a new stage in Chad’s climate commitment. Drawing lessons from previous contributions and informed by the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake of the Paris Agreement, it strengthens the country’s ambition while paying greater attention to the feasibility, monitoring and implementation conditions of the commitments made for 2035.

This contribution responds to a national reality in which climate and development challenges are closely linked. The country’s vulnerability, the growing scale of loss and damage and the dependence of a large part of the population on natural resources make strengthening resilience essential. At the same time, the economic and social transformations driven in particular by Tchad Connexion 2030 offer an opportunity to further integrate climate issues into the country’s investment and development choices. NDC 3.0 reflects this link by combining adaptation and emission reductions with the pursuit of national priorities.

Compared with previous contributions, NDC 3.0 broadens and clarifies the scope of climate action. On mitigation, it draws on an updated national greenhouse gas inventory and incorporates new actions in several sectors. On adaptation, it defines priorities and measures accompanied by a results framework for 2030 and 2035. It also gives specific attention to loss and damage and strengthens the consideration of social issues, in particular gender, inclusion, security and just transition.

The challenge now is to translate these orientations into investments, programmes and projects that are actually implemented. This requires continuing to consolidate data and monitoring arrangements, progressively refining certain targets and certain costs, strengthening institutional and technical capacities and ensuring lasting coordination among national, territorial and non-state actors. The progressive operationalisation of the national transparency arrangement and of the AN MNV should help track the progress made, strengthen the quality of climate information and inform the next planning cycles.

Achieving the full level of ambition of the NDC will require the mobilisation of resources commensurate with the transformations envisaged. National efforts will therefore need to be complemented by increased international support in terms of finance, technology transfer and capacity building. In this perspective, the preparation of bankable programmes and projects, improved access to climate finance and the diversification of funding sources will be priorities for the implementation of the NDC.

NDC 3.0 should thus be considered not as the culmination of a planning exercise, but as the framework for action that is set to be refined and strengthened over the next decade. Its success will depend on the collective capacity to convert the commitments made into measurable results for the people, the territories and the economy of Chad, while contributing to the global effort to combat climate change.

Chad considers that its nationally determined contribution (NDC) is both fair and ambitious in the light of its national circumstances, its limited institutional and financial capacities, and its low historical responsibility for global greenhouse gas emissions. Despite a marginal contribution to global emissions, the country faces high vulnerability to the effects of climate change, notably desertification, rainfall variability, recurrent droughts, floods and food insecurity.

In this context, the NDC reflects a progressive and realistic level of ambition through the simultaneous integration of mitigation, adaptation and sustainable development priorities, consistent with the national objectives of climate resilience and poverty reduction. It is based on structuring sectoral actions in the areas of energy, agriculture, forests, water and resilient infrastructure, while taking into account socio-economic constraints and the need for enhanced international support for finance, technology transfer and capacity building.

The NDC thus reflects the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in accordance with the Paris Agreement, by combining climate ambition, technical feasibility and the imperatives of sustainable national development.

b) Fairness considerations, including reflecting on equity;

Equity is a design and implementation principle of the NDC 3.0. It relies in particular on the social and territorial targeting of climate actions so that vulnerable populations and territories can benefit equitably from climate action. Programmes will thus aim to improve their access to water, sustainable energy, means of production, climate services, finance and social protection schemes.

The equity approach also provides for strengthening the participation of local communities in decision-making and for valuing local adaptation knowledge. Particular attention is paid to the different situations of vulnerability through disaggregated monitoring, where data are available, by sex, age, territory, disability and displacement status. Monitoring will examine in particular the territorial distribution of finance, the share of beneficiaries belonging to vulnerable groups, their access to essential services and their participation in climate governance mechanisms.

c) How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 3, of the Paris Agreement;

The NDC 3.0 represents a progression from the previous contribution across several dimensions. With regard to mitigation, it is based on a GHG inventory updated to 2022 and a reference scenario revised to 2035; it also strengthens the portfolio of measures, notably through the inclusion of new actions in agriculture and transport. In the LULUCF sector, the actions were reassessed and the area-based targets were maintained. The contribution sets a target for 2035 of −1.7% unconditionally and −21.2% with adequate international support, relative to the reference scenario.

With regard to adaptation, the NDC 3.0 updates risks and vulnerabilities and strengthens the operational nature of the contribution through a results framework comprising priority measures, indicators, responsible entities and, where data are available, baselines and targets for 2030 and 2035. The progression also concerns the stronger integration of cross-cutting dimensions — notably gender, equity and social inclusion, and just transition — as well as monitoring, transparency and the identification of the finance, capacity and technology needs required for implementation.

d) How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 4, of the Paris Agreement;

Given its status as a least developed country, its national circumstances and its capacities, Chad defines its mitigation contribution in the form of a reduction in net GHG emissions relative to a reference scenario. The NDC covers the main emission and removal sectors considered in the national GHG inventory and sets a national target for 2035.

e) How the Party has addressed Article 4, paragraph 6, of the Paris Agreement.

As a least developed country, Chad prepared its NDC 3.0 taking into account its national circumstances, its capacities and its priority development needs. In accordance with Article 4, paragraph 6, of the Paris Agreement, the contribution defines resilient, low-emission development strategies and measures adapted to the national context.

The NDC thus links emission reductions and the enhancement of carbon sinks with national priorities such as access to energy, the sustainable management of land and natural resources and strengthening the resilience of vulnerable systems and populations. In view of national financial capacities, full implementation of the NDC remains closely linked to the mobilisation of adequate international support, with conditional needs constituting the predominant share of the identified financing needs. This support is also decisive in facilitating access to the required technologies and in durably strengthening the institutional, technical and human capacities needed to implement the planned actions.

7. How the nationally determined contribution contributes towards achieving the objective of the Convention as set out in its Article 2:

a) How the nationally determined contribution contributes towards achieving the objective of the Convention as set out in its Article 2;

The NDC 3.0 contributes to the objective of the Convention by combining the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, the enhancement of carbon sinks and increased resilience of vulnerable populations, ecosystems and economic sectors. It thus places Chad’s climate action on a resilient, low-emission development pathway, while taking into account national priorities for sustainable development, vulnerability reduction and the preservation of natural resources.

b) How the nationally determined contribution contributes towards Article 2, paragraph 1(a), and Article 4, paragraph 1, of the Paris Agreement.

The NDC 3.0 contributes to the temperature goal set out in Article 2.1(a) of the Paris Agreement by committing Chad to reduce its net greenhouse gas emissions by 2035, consistent with the global effort to limit the increase in global average temperature to 1.5°C.

As a least developed country, Chad places this contribution on a pathway adapted to its national circumstances, capacities and development priorities. Through emission reductions in the covered sectors and the enhancement of removals, notably in the LULUCF sector, the NDC contributes to the collective effort under Article 4.1 to achieve a balance between anthropogenic emissions by sources and removals by sinks in the second half of the century, on the basis of equity and in the context of sustainable development and efforts to eradicate poverty.
