CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Purpose of developing NDC 3.0

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 establishes the country’s updated climate targets: a 10% reduction in total greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 and a 15% reduction by 2035 relative to 1990 levels (including the LULUCF sector); and, subject to expanded access to international support, a 25% reduction in total greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 and a 40% reduction by 2035 relative to 1990 levels (including the LULUCF sector).

The total financing requirement for implementing NDC 3.0 is estimated at USD 20.0–22.5 billion, depending on the scenario, while the achievement of the conditional targets depends on international financial, technical and technological support.

Climate change is one of the most serious challenges facing the Republic of Tajikistan in the twenty-first century. Accelerating glacier melt, increasingly frequent extreme hydrological events and the degradation of mountain ecosystems already threaten sustainable water management, food supplies and infrastructure resilience, both within the country and across the Central Asian region.1

The Republic of Tajikistan is consistently fulfilling its commitments under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement: it has prepared and submitted National Communications, Biennial Transparency Reports and its existing Nationally Determined Contribution (2021).

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 is an official document prepared pursuant to Article 4 of the Paris Agreement and intended for inclusion in the public NDC registry maintained by the UNFCCC secretariat. Under Article 4, paragraph 12, of the Paris Agreement, NDCs communicated by Parties are recorded in a public registry and become part of the international climate process.2, 3

NDC 3.0 was prepared in accordance with decisions of the Conferences of the Parties to the UNFCCC governing the content, structure and quality requirements of NDCs. The document was developed with due regard to the principles of transparency, accuracy, completeness, comparability and consistency, in line with the Enhanced Transparency Framework adopted at COP 24 in Katowice.

NDC 3.0 demonstrates progress in climate targets compared with the 2021 NDC and reflects Tajikistan’s “highest possible ambition” in climate change mitigation and adaptation, taking into account the country’s particular national circumstances.4

The principal objective of preparing NDC 3.0 is to establish the Republic of Tajikistan’s updated climate commitments for the period up to 2035, in accordance with the new cycle for communicating contributions introduced by decisions of CMA 5 (COP 28, Dubai, 2023). The parameters of the new cycle provide for 2035 targets in NDCs communicated in 2025–2026, ensuring continuity and predictability in the international climate process.5

The preparation of NDC 3.0 takes account of the outcomes of the first global stocktake (GST 1), concluded at COP 28 in Dubai in December 2023. GST 1 found that collective climate efforts were insufficient to keep global warming within 1.5°C and called upon Parties to submit more ambitious NDCs covering all sectors of the economy, all greenhouse gases and all categories of emission sources.6

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 pursues the following interrelated objectives:

  • update and raise the level of the country’s climate commitments in line with IPCC recommendations on the need to reduce global emissions by 43% by 2030 and 60% by 2035 compared with 2019 levels in order to limit warming to 1.5°C;7
  • integrate climate policy into key national strategic documents;8
  • ensure coherence between climate change mitigation and adaptation measures and synergies with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs);
  • establish an instrument for attracting international climate finance and technical support to implement the conditional targets of NDC 3.0;9
  • establish a basis for monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) of climate action under the Enhanced Transparency Framework.

1.2. Structure of NDC 3.0

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 was prepared in accordance with Article 4 of the Paris Agreement, taking into account the outcomes of the first global stocktake and applicable decisions of the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement. Information necessary to facilitate clarity, transparency and understanding of the contribution is provided in accordance with decision 4/CMA.1.

The five-year cycle for communicating contributions is provided for in Article 4, paragraph 9, of the Paris Agreement. Decisions 6/CMA.3 and 1/CMA.5 were taken into account in determining the end target year.

The structure of NDC 3.0 is consistent with the requirements of the Enhanced Transparency Framework under Article 13 of the Paris Agreement and ensures comparability with previous NDCs, the first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1) and the Fourth National Communication, thereby establishing a foundation for the practical implementation of climate commitments.

CHAPTER 2. NATIONAL CONTEXT AND CIRCUMSTANCES

2.1. National characteristics

2.1.1. Socio-economic context

According to the World Bank classification, Tajikistan is a lower-middle-income country with significant potential for further sustainable socio-economic development. In 2024, the country’s GDP amounted to approximately USD 14.2 billion, while GDP per capita stood at USD 1,341. Real GDP growth in 2024 reached 8.4% (one of the highest rates in the Europe and Central Asia region), remaining above 8% for several consecutive years. According to IMF and World Bank projections, GDP growth is expected to gradually stabilize at 4.5–5.0% by 2027–2029.10

The population of the Republic of Tajikistan was approximately 10.6 million in 2024 and reached 10.8 million by 2025. Annual population growth averages approximately 2.3–2.4%, creating substantial demand for job creation. The country’s demographic structure is characterized by a high proportion of young people, representing both a development opportunity and a challenge for education, health care and labour market systems.11

The country remains predominantly rural: approximately 73.8% of the population lives in rural areas, and around 63% of the working-age population is employed in agriculture. Tajikistan’s Human Development Index in 2024 was 0.685, corresponding to the medium human development category among 193 countries.12, 13

The structure of the economy remains substantially dependent on a limited number of sectors. Agriculture accounts for an estimated 24% of GDP, industry for 16–18%, construction for approximately 8%, and services for around 40–44%. Remittances from migrant workers, primarily from the Russian Federation, play an important role in supporting household incomes and well-being. According to the World Bank, remittances amounted to approximately 38.4% of GDP in 2023 and 47.9% in 2024. At the same time, the significant role of remittances underscores the importance of further economic diversification, expanding domestic employment and strengthening the country’s resilience to external economic factors.14, 15

Inflation was approximately 3.4% in 2024, indicating sustained macroeconomic stabilization. Total public debt declined from 49.5% of GDP in 2020 to around 31% in 2024, reflecting improvements in fiscal management. Access to electricity is approaching 100% of the population.16

According to projections by the Ministry of Economic Development and Trade of the Republic of Tajikistan, sustained economic growth, increasing investment and expanding foreign trade are expected over the medium term.

2.1.2. Natural and climatic conditions

The Republic of Tajikistan is a landlocked country located in the heart of Central Asia. It covers an area of approximately 143,100 km² and borders Afghanistan to the south, China to the east, Kyrgyzstan to the north and Uzbekistan to the west.

Mountainous terrain covers approximately 93% of the country and largely determines its economic development, transport connectivity and vulnerability to climate impacts. About half of the territory lies more than 3,000 metres above sea level; the highest point, Ismoil Somoni Peak in the Pamirs, reaches 7,495 metres. The country’s mountain systems include the Pamirs in the east; the Kuhiston (Gissar-Alay), Zeravshan and Kulamin ranges in the north; and the Darvaz and Peter the First ranges in the central part of the country.

Tajikistan has a sharply continental, arid to semi-arid climate with pronounced altitudinal zonation. Hot summers and mild winters prevail in the valleys and lowlands (up to 1,000 metres); a moderately continental climate characterizes the middle mountains (1,000–2,500 metres); higher elevations (above 2,500 metres) have a cold and severe climate. The Pamir Plateau has an extremely continental climate, with temperatures ranging from −63°C to +47°C. Precipitation is highly unevenly distributed, with most falling in winter and spring. Combined with summer droughts, this creates persistent risks of water scarcity during the principal agricultural season.

Observed climate change in Tajikistan is proceeding significantly faster than global average trends. Over the period of systematic observations, mean annual air temperature has increased by 1.2°C (twice the global average rate). In mountain areas at elevations of 1,000–2,500 metres, warming is occurring at a rate of 0.3–0.5°C per decade, and in high mountain areas at 0.2–0.4°C per decade. Projections indicate that, under a high-emissions scenario, temperatures in certain parts of the country could rise by 5°C by 2100.

Tajikistan is one of Central Asia’s major centres of glaciation and has substantial glacier and water resources. According to 2023 data, the country has 11,528 glaciers covering a total area of 7,624 km², or approximately 6% of its territory. However, more than 1,000 glaciers have already disappeared completely; between 2003 and 2023, glacier area declined by 629 km², at an average annual rate of approximately 0.33%. In particular, the Tajikistan Glacier (the largest mountain-valley glacier) lost 44 km², or 6% of its area, between 1966 and 2000.18

Projections indicate that by 2050, glacier area will decrease by 75.5% in the Panj River basin and by 53% in the Vakhsh River basin, resulting in a reduction of approximately 30% in the flow of the Amu Darya River.

The country is exceptionally rich in water resources: Tajikistan’s rivers generate approximately 55.4% of the average annual runoff in the entire Aral Sea basin (about 64 km³ per year).19

The country’s largest rivers are the Amu Darya (formed at the confluence of the Panj and Vakhsh rivers); the Panj (921 km, a right-bank tributary of the Amu Darya forming the border river with Afghanistan); the Vakhsh (a basin of 39,100 km², annual runoff of approximately 20 km³, and the location of the Nurek and Rogun hydropower plants); the Zeravshan (781 km); and the Kofarnihon. Approximately 80% of Amu Darya runoff originates within Tajikistan.20

Climate change is exacerbating the country’s already high natural vulnerability. Principal climate-related hazards include more frequent and severe droughts during the main agricultural season; increasing frequency and intensity of floods, including those caused by rapid snow and glacier melt; growing risks of glacial lake outburst floods; more frequent mudflows and landslides in mountain areas; permafrost degradation; and extreme winter cold causing energy supply crises.

According to the ND-GAIN Vulnerability and Readiness Index, Tajikistan is among the Central Asian countries most vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, with relatively limited adaptive capacity.21

2.1.3. Trends in energy development and natural resource use

Tajikistan’s energy sector is distinctive in Central Asia because of the exceptionally high share of renewables in electricity generation. Hydropower plants account for more than 95% of all electricity generated. The country’s largest hydropower plants are Nurek (3 GW) and the Rogun hydropower plant, currently under construction, with a design capacity of 3.8 GW.22

The country’s hydropower potential is estimated at 527 TWh per year, of which less than 4% has been developed to date. NDS 2030 sets a target of increasing installed power-generation capacity to 10,000 MW and electricity generation to 31.6–41.6 TWh per year. Immediate priorities in this area include completion of the Rogun hydropower plant, integration with regional power systems (in particular, through the CASA-1000 project, which provides for electricity exports to Pakistan through Afghanistan), and the development of small hydropower plants to electrify remote mountain areas.

Hydropower occupies a dominant position in the country’s primary energy resource consumption structure. However, petroleum products (23%), solid biomass, fuelwood and agricultural residues (21%), coal (19%) and natural gas (3%) continue to play important roles in final energy consumption. In the residential sector, which accounts for approximately 49% of fuel and energy resource consumption, biomass (72%) and coal (12%) remain the main heating fuels, creating significant health and environmental risks. Transport and industry each account for 17% of fuel and energy resource consumption.

Despite near-universal access to electricity, seasonal electricity shortages during the winter period (October–April) remain a significant problem because hydropower generation decreases when reservoir water levels are low. The commissioning of new generating units at the Rogun hydropower plant and implementation of CASA-1000 are intended to bring about a fundamental improvement in this situation.

The Republic of Tajikistan has limited agricultural land: arable land covers approximately 663,000 hectares (70% of which is irrigated), while pastureland covers approximately 3.86 million hectares. Forests and forest-covered areas occupy approximately 423,000 hectares (around 3% of the country’s territory), although their extent has declined considerably compared with the beginning of the twentieth century.

In the climate sphere, Tajikistan has made notable progress in integrating gender equality and social inclusion (GESI) into national climate governance and planning. Climate-relevant sectoral strategies increasingly recognize the differentiated impacts of climate change on women, rural communities, young people, persons with disabilities and other vulnerable groups, particularly in agriculture, water resources management, disaster risk reduction, health care and energy.

In NDC 3.0, the treatment of gender issues moves beyond a narrative centred on vulnerability and adaptation towards prioritizing women’s leadership, access to climate finance, green employment and participation in climate governance and decision-making structures.

2.3. International legal framework for climate policy

2.3.1. United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change and the Paris Agreement

The Republic of Tajikistan acceded to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) as a non-Annex I Party. The Paris Agreement was ratified by the Parliament (Majlisi Oli) of the Republic of Tajikistan on 13 February 2017.23

The Republic of Tajikistan ratified the Kyoto Protocol on 21 October 2008.

As a Party to the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, the Republic of Tajikistan has undertaken to contribute to holding the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.

Under Article 4, paragraph 2, of the Paris Agreement, each Party is required to prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions (NDCs), each of which is to represent a progression beyond the previous contribution and reflect the highest possible ambition (Article 4, paragraph 3).

The Republic of Tajikistan’s initial NDC (INDC, 2017) provided for limiting emissions to 80–90% of 1990 levels by 2030 under the unconditional scenario and to 65–75% under the conditional scenario. In the updated NDC communicated in October 2021, climate ambition was revised: the unconditional target envisaged total emissions not exceeding 60–70% of 1990 levels by 2030, while the conditional target (subject to international support) envisaged emissions not exceeding 50–60% of 1990 levels by 2030.

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement provides Tajikistan with opportunities to participate in international climate cooperation, including voluntary cooperative approaches (Article 6.2), the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (Article 6.4) and non-market approaches (Article 6.8). Tajikistan views participation in Article 6 mechanisms as an instrument for mobilizing additional international climate finance and technology transfer to implement the conditional targets of NDC 3.0.

2.3.2. Other international agreements on climate, the environment and water resources

The Republic of Tajikistan’s international climate and environmental policies are based on a broad framework of treaties and legal instruments.

In addition to the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, Tajikistan is a Party to the following key international treaties:

  • Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD);
  • Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety;
  • Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources;
  • Aarhus Convention on Access to Information, Public Participation in Decision-making and Access to Justice in Environmental Matters;
  • Ramsar Convention on Wetlands of International Importance;
  • Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer;
  • Bonn Convention (Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals).

Tajikistan plays a leading role internationally in water resources and glacier-related issues. The country is an active participant in the United Nations International Decade for Action, “Water for Sustainable Development”, 2018–2028.

At the initiative of the Republic of Tajikistan, on 14 December 2022 the United Nations General Assembly adopted resolution A/RES/77/158 proclaiming 2025 the International Year of Glaciers’ Preservation. The initiative promotes international scientific cooperation, cryosphere monitoring and increased attention to risks associated with changes in glacier conditions. Tajikistan leads the Group of Friends of Glaciers at the United Nations.

A United Nations High-Level Conference on Water was held in Dushanbe in 2024. These efforts reflect the strategic importance of water and glacier issues for the country and make a substantial contribution to shaping the international climate agenda.

Tajikistan actively participates in regional climate and environmental cooperation through the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) and the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS), and engages with international financial institutions, including the World Bank, Asian Development Bank (ADB), European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD), Green Climate Fund (GCF), and United Nations agencies.

The Republic of Tajikistan is also a Party to several other multilateral environmental and climate-related agreements that form part of the legal and treaty basis of national climate policy.

The Republic of Tajikistan acceded to the Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer (6 May 1996) and the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer (7 January 1998). The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol was ratified on 29 June 2022.

The Republic of Tajikistan also acceded to the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. The Convention entered into force for the country on 30 March 2016.

In combating desertification and land degradation, the country acceded to the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) on 12 August 1997 and ratified it through Decree No. 1140 of the President of the Republic of Tajikistan of 8 December 1998. Regarding the regulation of transboundary movements of hazardous substances and waste, the Republic of Tajikistan is a Party to the Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal and the Rotterdam Convention on the Prior Informed Consent Procedure for Certain Hazardous Chemicals and Pesticides in International Trade (ratified on 1 October 2024).

The Republic of Tajikistan is also a Party to the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants.

The Republic of Tajikistan actively participates in dialogue on the Convention on the Protection and Use of Transboundary Watercourses and International Lakes (UNECE Helsinki Convention) and works to strengthen regional cooperation on water resources.

Together, these agreements form a multi-layered system of international commitments by the Republic of Tajikistan on climate, the environment and water resources, complementing its commitments under the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement and aligned with the objectives of NDC 3.0.

CHAPTER 3. NDC 3.0 PREPARATION PROCESS

3.1. General aspects of NDC 3.0 development

NDC 3.0 was developed under the leadership of the Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan (CEP), the national focal point for the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, with technical support from the United Nations Development Programme through its global Climate Promise initiative. An Inter-Agency Working Group (IAWG), established pursuant to an instruction of the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan, participated in the process. It comprised representatives of 24 key ministries and agencies, as well as development partners, research institutions and civil society organizations.24

Preparation of the document comprised several stages:

  • collection and analysis of up-to-date data on GHG emissions and removals across all sectors of the economy;
  • scenario modelling of mitigation potential and assessment of the costs of climate measures;
  • development of sectoral plans;
  • multi-stakeholder consultations;
  • final editing and endorsement of the document.

The preparation of NDC 3.0 envisaged the active engagement of a broad range of stakeholders. Consultations were conducted with representatives of the private sector, civil society, the scientific and expert community, local self-government bodies, international organizations and development partners. Principles of inclusion and consideration of the interests of vulnerable groups (women, young people, rural communities and persons with disabilities) informed the identification of adaptation priorities, including through multisectoral consultations.

3.2. International context of NDC 3.0

NDC 3.0 was developed in the context of substantial changes in the international climate regime following the submission of NDC 2.0 in 2021. Foremost among these was the conclusion of the first global stocktake (GST 1) at COP 28 in Dubai in December 2023, following which Parties to the Paris Agreement were called upon to reflect its outcomes in their new NDCs and move towards a 2035 target year.

Decisions adopted at COP 29 in Baku in November 2024 on the new collective quantified goal (NCQG) on climate finance (at least USD 300 billion per year by 2035 for developing countries) create new opportunities to mobilize international finance in support of Tajikistan’s conditional NDC 3.0 targets.

The Republic continues to participate actively in the work of the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea and in the implementation of agreed regional initiatives on water resources, the environment and climate change adaptation.

At Tajikistan’s initiative, the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 2025 the International Year of Glaciers’ Preservation, drawing the international community’s attention to the accelerating degradation of the cryosphere.

NDC 3.0 is also aligned with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. The climate measures included in NDC 3.0 contribute to several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), notably SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), SDG 13 (Climate Action), SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) and SDG 15 (Life on Land). The adaptation measures included in NDC 3.0 also advance SDG 2 (End hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture), SDG 3 (Ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages), SDG 4 (Ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all), SDG 5 (Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls), SDG 8 (Promote sustained, inclusive and sustainable economic growth, full and productive employment and decent work for all), and SDG 11 (Make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable).25

NDC 3.0 of the Republic of Tajikistan is a comprehensive climate policy document reflecting the country’s strategic vision for low-carbon and climate-resilient development through 2035. The document is based on the principles of scientific rigour, transparency, inclusiveness and progressively enhanced climate ambition enshrined in the Paris Agreement.

The Republic of Tajikistan views NDC 3.0 not only as an international commitment but also as a strategic instrument for sustainable national development, attracting investment in the green economy, reducing climate risks, and ensuring the long-term security and prosperity of the country’s population in a changing climate.

3.3. Inter-Agency Coordination

NDC 3.0 is being prepared under the leadership of the Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan (CEP), which serves as the national coordinating body and the designated national authority for the UNFCCC in Tajikistan. Scientific and technical support and reporting to the UNFCCC are provided directly by the Agency for Hydrometeorology under the CEP (Tajikhydromet), which houses the Climate Change and Ozone Layer Centre.

An Inter-Agency Technical Working Group (IATWG) was established to coordinate the development of the NDC, with its work coordinated by the CEP’s Centre for the Implementation of the Paris Agreement. The IATWG brought together representatives of 24 key ministries and agencies, including the Ministry of Energy and Water Resources, the Ministry of Agriculture, the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Economic Development and Trade, the Ministry of Transport, the Ministry of Industry and New Technologies, the Ministry of Health and Social Protection of the Population, the State Committee on Land Management and Geodesy, the Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defence, the Committee on Housing and Communal Services, the Committee on Women and Family Affairs, and the Agency on Statistics under the President of the Republic of Tajikistan.

The national NDC 3.0 development process was officially launched in July 2025 at the National Inception Workshop held in Dushanbe with support from UNDP under its Climate Promise initiative, the NDC Partnership and the Government of the United Kingdom. Support under Climate Promise covers the entire NDC 3.0 preparation cycle, including analytical assistance, scenario modelling, consultations and national capacity development, and is not limited to the inception workshop. The work follows a sectoral approach, with separate technical working groups covering energy (including transport), agriculture, forestry and biodiversity, industry and construction, water resources, and waste.

The preparation process draws on the First Biennial Transparency Report, submitted by Tajikistan to the UNFCCC in December 2024, as well as data from the Fourth National Communication and the World Bank’s 2024 Country Climate and Development Report for Tajikistan.

The methodological basis for preparing the national GHG inventory is the 2006 IPCC Guidelines, as refined in 2019.

3.4. Stakeholder consultations

NDC 3.0 is being prepared through a comprehensive approach that provides for the broad engagement of all stakeholders: government bodies, academic and scientific communities, civil society, the private sector, international development partners, representatives of vulnerable population groups, young people and women.26

The consultation process follows a multi-level structure comprising the following stages:

  • an inception workshop for all stakeholders to explain the road map and methodology;
  • sectoral technical meetings in each priority area involving the relevant ministries, scientific institutions and sector specialists;
  • consultations within the IATWG to coordinate sectoral scenarios and targets across government institutions;
  • specialized events for civil society, NGOs, academia and business representatives.

Particular attention is given to integrating gender equality and social inclusion principles. In 2026, a UNDP-supported workshop on gender aspects of climate policy and the NDC was held, with participation from the Committee on Women and Family Affairs; NGOs, including YGPE (Khujand), ACTED, Gender and Development (Dushanbe), the Red Crescent Society of Tajikistan, HELVETAS Swiss Intercooperation, the Youth Ecological Centre (Dushanbe), Little Earth (Dushanbe) and AJO (Khujand); as well as private sector and community representatives and gender researchers.

An Inter-Agency Working Group on Gender Equality was established to ensure that gender dimensions are taken into account throughout the NDC.

Tajik academic and research institutions, including the Academy of Sciences, relevant higher education institutions and sectoral research institutes, have been involved in the consultation process, contributing to technical assessments, sectoral modelling and literature reviews. The country’s youth organizations were engaged in the NDC development process through dedicated youth dialogue platforms and participation in consultation events. Representatives of local executive government authorities and settlement and village self-government bodies (jamoats) participated in regional and local consultations, including discussions of adaptation priorities and disaster risk reduction at district and community levels.

3.5. Gender equality, inclusion and just transition in NDC 3.0

The Republic of Tajikistan regards gender equality, social equity and inclusion as integral components of climate policy.

The particular vulnerability of rural women working in the climate-dependent agricultural sector is an important consideration in designing climate measures. According to the World Bank, approximately 73.8% of the country’s population lives in rural areas, a substantial proportion of whom are women.27

With regard to a just transition, NDC 3.0 recognizes that the shift to a low-carbon economy should be accompanied by the protection of employment, incomes and living conditions for the most vulnerable segments of the population. In Tajikistan, a substantial proportion of the population depends on traditional biomass (fuelwood and agricultural waste) for household heating; gradually replacing these sources requires affordable alternatives and adequate social protection.

Just transition principles are reflected in Tajikistan’s Green Economy Development Strategy for 2023–2037, which is oriented towards creating green jobs, developing renewable energy and improving energy efficiency while maintaining social protection for the most vulnerable groups. NDC 3.0 provides for dedicated monitoring of social indicators during climate policy implementation to ensure that climate action does not exacerbate existing inequalities but instead contributes to sustainable and inclusive development.

Tajikistan recognizes the central importance of just transition principles reflected in the preamble to the Paris Agreement: the transition to low-carbon development must take into account the imperatives of decent work and quality of life. Accordingly, NDC 3.0 integrates employment and social protection considerations into sectoral mitigation strategies, particularly in the energy and agricultural sectors.

The monitoring and evaluation system provides for an assessment of the impacts of climate policies on poverty and inequality, consistent with the recommendations arising from the first global stocktake (COP 28) on reflecting the social and economic consequences of decarbonization in NDC 3.0.

Gender equality and social inclusion are addressed in detail in Chapter 7 of NDC 3.0.

CHAPTER 4. ASSESSMENT OF PROGRESS TOWARDS CLIMATE TARGETS

4.1. Consideration of the outcomes of the first global stocktake

The first global stocktake (GST 1), concluded at COP 28 in Dubai in December 2023, became a key mechanism for assessing the international community’s collective progress towards the goals of the Paris Agreement. Its decisions directly inform the content of the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0.

4.1.1. Climate change mitigation

GST 1 found collective ambition to be critically insufficient: implementation of existing NDCs would result in a warming trajectory of 2.4–2.6°C by the end of the century.

Key mitigation-related outcomes of GST 1 taken into account in developing Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 include:

  • transitioning away from fossil fuels (with a view to achieving climate neutrality by 2050);
  • tripling global renewable energy capacity to 11,000 GW by 2030;
  • doubling the rate of energy efficiency improvements by 2030;
  • scaling up finance for the transition to a low-carbon economy;
  • recognizing the importance of nature-based solutions for carbon sequestration.

Tajikistan reflects the conclusions of GST 1 in NDC 3.0 as follows: the country has substantial potential for renewable energy development. Commissioning the Rogun hydropower plant and other priority projects in this field will enable the national power system to transition entirely to renewable energy sources.

In forestry and land use, Tajikistan plans to increase forest-covered land from 423,000 to 430,000 hectares by 2030, fully consistent with the GST 1 objectives of scaling up nature-based solutions.28

4.1.2. Climate change adaptation

GST 1 identified a significant shortfall in adaptation finance relative to the actual needs of vulnerable countries. Decisions taken at COP 28 recognize the need for a substantial increase in climate finance for adaptation.

Tajikistan is among the most climate-vulnerable mountainous countries: accelerated glacier melt (more than 30% of glacier area has been lost since the 1960s) threatens the long-term sustainable management of water resources of the country and the wider Central Asian region. Projected annual GDP losses in the absence of timely adaptation amount to 5–6% of GDP, making adaptation an economically justified priority.29

NDC 3.0 reflects the following adaptation priorities arising from GST 1 outcomes: strengthening early warning systems, protecting water-intake infrastructure, adapting agrifood systems, strengthening public health and reducing natural disaster risks.

These priority areas require systematic international technical and financial support, which has been incorporated into the scenarios under development.

4.1.3. Investment readiness and feasibility

GST 1 highlighted an enormous climate finance gap: according to the IPCC, approximately USD 4–6 trillion per year must be invested in low-carbon technologies to pursue a 1.5°C pathway.

At COP 29 in Baku in November 2024, a new collective quantified goal (NCQG) on climate finance of USD 300 billion per year by 2035 for developing countries was adopted. In this context, Tajikistan has consistently advocated improved access to climate finance for mountainous and small island States in multilateral negotiations.

To enhance its attractiveness to investors, the Republic of Tajikistan is progressively improving the legislative framework for private investment in renewable energy; developing accredited entities to work with the Green Climate Fund and other multilateral climate funds; and establishing a national climate finance system based on public–private partnerships.

4.2. Assessment of progress towards climate targets

4.2.1. Progress and enhanced ambition

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 was prepared in full accordance with the principle of progression set out in Article 4.3 of the Paris Agreement: each successive nationally determined contribution is to represent a progression beyond the previous one and reflect the highest possible ambition, taking account of national circumstances.

Following the first global stocktake (GST 1), held at COP 28 in Dubai in 2023, countries were invited to reflect its outcomes in their NDC 3.0 submissions and substantially raise their climate ambition: triple renewable energy capacity, double the rate of energy efficiency improvement by 2030, transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems, and take account of adaptation objectives and UNFCCC decisions arising from the first global stocktake, including 11 adaptation targets. Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 was developed in the light of these recommendations, as applicable to the country’s national circumstances.

It is important to emphasize that, in accordance with the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, Tajikistan makes a minimal contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions (its total emissions account for less than 0.03% of the global total), while facing extremely high climate vulnerability. This gives adaptation a particularly important place among the country’s climate policy priorities, alongside the strengthening of its mitigation potential in the context of sustained socioeconomic development.30

The targets of NDC 3.0 cover all sectors of the economy and provide for a reduction in total greenhouse gas emissions of 10% by 2030 and 15% by 2035 relative to the 1990 level (including the LULUCF sector), and, conditional on expanded access to international support, a reduction in total greenhouse gas emissions of 25% by 2030 and 40% by 2035 relative to the 1990 level (including the LULUCF sector). Tajikistan thus demonstrates a progressive enhancement of climate ambition while maintaining continuity and progression relative to its previous NDCs.

4.3. Assessment of progress against green growth indicators

An analysis of the OECD green growth indicator framework for the Republic of Tajikistan shows that, between 2020 and 2024, the country maintained high economic growth alongside gradual improvements in the efficiency of energy and material resource use. Real GDP in constant 2020 prices rose from USD 35.6 billion at purchasing power parity in 2020 to USD 49.4 billion in 2024, indicating an expanding economic base that generates both additional energy needs and greater opportunities for investment in low-carbon development. This trend is important for NDC 3.0: the high share of renewables in electricity generation provides structural conditions for a long-term low-carbon transformation of the economy. At the same time, growing aggregate energy demand, rising greenhouse gas emissions from air transport, the persistently high energy and material intensity of certain sectors and significant reliance on fossil fuels require a systematic strengthening of measures related to energy efficiency, sustainable mobility, natural capital management and climate risk reduction.31

In 2020–2023, greenhouse gas emissions per capita remained low and virtually stable, rising from 0.74 to 0.75 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent. This trend shows that low per-capita carbon emissions are an important national advantage; however, achieving a more ambitious low-carbon development pathway requires moving beyond relative efficiency improvements towards stabilizing and subsequently reducing absolute emissions in the most energy-intensive and rapidly expanding segments of the economy.

Energy intensity and energy productivity are fundamental indicators of green growth because they reflect an economy’s ability to generate added value while consuming less primary energy.

In 2020–2023, energy productivity, expressed as GDP per unit of total energy supply, increased from USD 9,734 to USD 11,162 per tonne of oil equivalent (toe), at 2020 prices. This reflects more efficient energy resource use and establishes a favourable basis for reducing the carbon intensity of economic growth.

Energy consumption per capita remained relatively stable, changing from 0.38 to 0.39 toe per person. Accordingly, energy productivity improved against a backdrop of increasing gross consumption of fuel and energy resources.

These trends mean that mitigation measures must address not only the energy sector as a major emissions source but also final energy consumption in industry, transport, agriculture, services, housing and communal services.

For climate policy, this entails accelerating demand-side energy management, improving end-use efficiency and modernizing energy infrastructure, as well as implementing targeted energy efficiency measures in industry and transport, including equipment modernization, energy management, more efficient freight logistics and public transport development, so that economic growth does not cause a proportionate increase in energy demand. Other important areas include improving building energy efficiency, reducing losses in energy infrastructure, electrifying final energy consumption while maintaining a high share of renewable electricity generation, developing greenhouse gas monitoring, reporting and verification systems, and incorporating carbon efficiency considerations into public investment planning in energy-intensive industries.

At the same time, the high share of renewable electricity does not eliminate the energy sector’s long-term climate risks. Hydropower is sensitive to changes in hydrological regimes, seasonal runoff, extreme weather events, and glacier and snow conditions. Energy mitigation measures therefore need to be closely linked to adaptation through enhancing the resilience of hydropower infrastructure, managing the water–energy balance, developing distributed solar power, reducing technical losses, improving streamflow forecasting and integrating climate scenarios into energy investment planning.

Forest resources, biomass and protected areas have a dual significance for climate policy. On the one hand, they contribute to carbon removals, sustainable land use and reduced emissions in the land use, land-use change and forestry sector. On the other, they provide adaptation benefits, including regulation of the water regime, reduced erosion and mudflow risks, protection of agricultural land, provision of ecosystem services, protection of rural livelihoods, and increased resilience of territories to droughts, floods and land degradation.

In 2020–2024, Tajikistan recorded a decline in the share of biomass consumption (fuelwood, agricultural residues and other biological materials) in gross domestic material consumption, from 41.84% in 2020 to 40.30% in 2024. Per-capita biomass consumption fell from 1.96 to 1.64 tonnes per person over the same period. This trend may reflect a gradual change in the structure of material consumption and a declining relative share of biological materials in total resource use; it may also indicate reduced pressure on land and biological resources. However, further analysis is needed of food security, agricultural sustainability and the condition of pasture and forest ecosystems.

To ensure sustainable low-carbon development of the national economy, it is important that this decline not be accompanied by deterioration in agricultural land, forests and pastures, but instead result from better resource efficiency, sustainable land management, reduced losses along agrifood supply chains, and more rational use of woody and non-woody biomass.

Between 2020 and 2025, the share of naturally regenerating forests in Tajikistan remained stable at 72.4% of forest-covered area.

During this period, timber harvesting decreased from 2.20 million to 1.82 million tonnes, while domestic wood consumption declined from 2.26 million to 1.87 million tonnes. Per-capita wood consumption fell from 0.23 to 0.18 tonnes per person. These trends indicate stabilization in aggregate forest-use indicators and underscore the importance of systematic reforestation, sustainable forest management and action against the degradation of mountain ecosystems.

From a climate policy perspective, this trend is positive because it reduces pressure on the country’s forest resources. At the same time, the forestry sector requires systemic measures to ensure the legality and sustainability of timber harvesting, improve wood-use efficiency, develop alternative household energy sources and prevent forest degradation. In Tajikistan, where climate risks are closely linked to mountain landscapes, forest and shrub ecosystems should also be regarded as components of nature-based solutions and ecosystem-based adaptation.

Between 2020 and 2024, the share of national territory covered by protected natural areas increased from 21.91% to 22.09%. Maintaining and expanding these areas is important for achieving the interconnected objectives of climate policy and biodiversity conservation. Protected areas help maintain ecosystem resilience, preserve water-regulation functions, protect habitats and reduce the vulnerability of mountain and rural areas to climate impacts.

Practical measures to expand the application of nature-based solutions are therefore particularly important for NDC 3.0. These include restoring forest and riparian ecosystems along rivers and other water bodies, sustainably managing pastures, undertaking agroforestry and shelterbelt planting, preventing land degradation, accounting for carbon and adaptation benefits in forest management planning, and developing monitoring of ecosystem services. Such measures simultaneously advance mitigation, adaptation, disaster risk reduction and biodiversity conservation, consistent with the integrated nature of national climate policy.

Overall, the analysis of green growth indicators shows that the Republic of Tajikistan possesses structural advantages for further progress along a low-carbon development pathway. These systemic factors provide a basis for setting out measures in NDC 3.0 that strengthen development resilience without constraining economic growth. For NDC 3.0 purposes, green growth indicators are recommended as the basis for a monitoring system linking economic performance to climate and natural-resource outcomes. Such a system will make it possible to track the extent to which NDC 3.0 measures not only reduce emissions but also strengthen the economy’s resilience to climate risks.

CHAPTER 5. NATIONAL CLIMATE CHANGE MITIGATION TARGET

5.1. National climate change mitigation target

5.1.1. Target formulation

NDC 3.0 establishes absolute, economy-wide greenhouse gas (GHG) emission reduction targets relative to the 1990 base year. This approach is consistent with the methodological principles applied in Tajikistan’s 2021 NDC, ensuring continuity and verifiability of the stated targets.

The contribution covers all economic sectors and all greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluorocarbons (PFCs), sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) and nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃)), in accordance with the principles of completeness, transparency and consistency established under the Paris Agreement and the Katowice Climate Package. The NDC 3.0 targets are based on data from Tajikistan’s First Biennial Transparency Report and Fourth National Communication.

Tajikistan reaffirms its unconditional commitment to reduce GHG emissions to no more than 23.5 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2030 (10% below the base-year level) and no more than 22.2 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2035 (15% below the base-year level), including the LULUCF sector. These commitments represent a progression beyond the 2021 NDC, under which the unconditional target was to keep emissions at no more than 60–70% of the 1990 level by 2030 (before emissions recalculation), equivalent, after recalculation, to 21.3–24.9 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2030.32 In addition, Tajikistan sets a conditional target of further reducing emissions under the ‘With Additional Measures’ scenario to no more than 19.6 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2030 (25% below the base-year level) and no more than 15.7 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent by 2035 (40% below the base-year level), including LULUCF.

Achieving this target is conditional upon the provision of the necessary international financial support, expanded access to international climate finance mechanisms, and enhanced technical and technological assistance.

The conditional and unconditional commitments encompass the sectors with the greatest investment needs: energy, agriculture, transport, industrial processes, waste management and LULUCF.

5.1.2. Conditions for achievement

Two scenarios have been developed to achieve the targets of the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0: the ‘With Measures’ scenario (the unconditional scenario, implemented using the country’s own resources) and the ‘With Additional Measures’ scenario, whose implementation depends on the scale and quality of international support.

Methodological basis of scenario modelling: projected emissions trajectories were developed using the national NDC 3.0 calculation model, harmonized with national GHG inventory data; the latest national inventory covers 2022 and is reflected in BTR1. The business-as-usual (BAU), ‘With Measures’ and ‘With Additional Measures’ scenarios were developed through 2035 on the basis of macroeconomic development projections and sectoral programmes.

With Measures scenario

Under NDC 3.0, the Republic of Tajikistan pursues the unconditional target of reducing GHG emissions by 10% by 2030 and 15% by 2035 relative to the 1990 base year, including LULUCF, using its own financial, technological and institutional resources, together with projects already being implemented with development partner support.

With Additional Measures scenario

The ‘With Additional Measures’ scenario under the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 provides for further GHG emission reductions (25% by 2030 and 40% by 2035 relative to the 1990 base year, including LULUCF), conditional upon the receipt of appropriate international financial support, technology transfer and strengthened institutional capacity.

Conditional measures whose implementation depends on the mobilization of international climate finance include:

  • an expanded set of GESI measures and activities addressing the interests of children and young people, including large-scale investments to improve energy efficiency in social infrastructure;
  • implementation of an additional portfolio of projects under development by international financial institutions (EBRD, ADB, EBRD, Islamic Development Bank and other development partners);
  • more ambitious targets for transport and industrial decarbonization, including technological modernization and a shift to best available techniques (BAT) at existing cement, aluminium and chemical facilities, as well as construction of new facilities in line with sectoral best practices and standards;
  • realization of the full potential of the Rogun hydropower plant and commissioning of other planned hydropower projects;
  • diversification of renewable energy through the development of solar and wind power generation;
  • gradual examination and possible use of instruments under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, including bilateral cooperation (Article 6.2) and the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (Article 6.4); if such instruments are applied, accounting will follow applicable CMA decisions, including the avoidance of double counting and reporting of corresponding adjustments in BTRs;
  • scaling up nature-based solutions, including reforestation, restoration of degraded ecosystems, and sustainable management of pastures and mountain ecosystems as means of simultaneously enhancing CO₂ removals, adaptation and biodiversity conservation;
  • introduction of digital technologies into greenhouse gas monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV), including the creation of a national climate data platform.

5.3. Enhancing climate ambition

NDC 3.0 represents a progressive enhancement of Tajikistan’s climate ambition relative to previous NDCs, consistent with the principle of progression enshrined in Article 3 and Article 4, paragraph 3, of the Paris Agreement.

The principle of progression in climate ambition requires not only stronger quantitative targets, but also broader coverage of sectors, gases and implementation instruments from one cycle to the next.

Comparison of Tajikistan’s successive commitments demonstrates compliance over time with Article 3 and Article 4, paragraph 3, of the Paris Agreement, with each successive contribution reflecting the highest possible ambition in the light of national circumstances.

Each successive NDC sets a higher level of ambition:

  • NDC 1.0 (2017): unconditional target—to keep emissions at no more than 80–90% of the 1990 level by 2030. The recalculated projected volume of emissions under this scenario is 20.9–23.5 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (including LULUCF);
  • NDC 2.0 (2021): unconditional target—to keep emissions, after recalculation, at no more than 21.3–24.9 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (including LULUCF) by 2030; conditional target—to keep emissions at no more than 17.8–21.3 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (including LULUCF) by 2030. Owing to the recalculation of 1990 base-year emissions, the percentage expression of the targets relative to the updated baseline differs from that originally stated in NDC 1.0.
  • NDC 3.0 (2026): the unconditional target is to reduce emissions by at least 10% by 2030 relative to 1990 (≤23.5 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent including LULUCF) and by at least 15% by 2035 relative to 1990 (≤22.2 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent including LULUCF). The conditional target is to reduce emissions by at least 25% by 2030 relative to 1990 (≤19.6 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent including LULUCF) and by at least 40% by 2035 relative to 1990 (≤15.7 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent including LULUCF).

The proposed target is fair and ambitious in the light of national circumstances, considering Tajikistan’s high climate vulnerability, extremely small contribution to global emissions, already high share of low-carbon hydropower in the energy mix, and continuing substantial need for international support to implement the conditional component of the target (decision 4/CMA.1).

5.4.2. Energy

The energy sector has historically been the largest source of carbon emissions (15.6 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent in 1990, or 13.3 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent excluding transport emissions), and makes a major contribution to achieving the strategic transition to a low-carbon economy.

The flagship project of the energy transition is the Rogun hydropower plant, the largest hydropower facility in Central Asia. Its installed capacity will be 3,780 MW, comprising six units of 630 MW each. According to the Ministry of Energy and Water Resources, by 2030 annual electricity generation at Rogun will reach 12.6 billion kWh, and the plant will achieve its full design capacity by 2037. The commissioning of Rogun and other ongoing hydropower projects constitutes a key unconditional contribution by Tajikistan to reducing global GHG emissions.36

Emission reductions under the national target are calculated on the basis of the Republic of Tajikistan’s territorial inventory. Exports of low-carbon electricity are treated as an additional regional benefit and do not replace national accounting for NDC achievement.

Other major investment projects in the energy sector will also make a substantial contribution to achieving NDC 3.0 targets.

In accordance with the State Long-Term Energy Strategy for 2023–2037 and the Renewable Energy Development Programme for 2023–2027 (Government Resolution No. 51 of 1 March 2023), the following activities are also planned during the NDC 3.0 implementation period: completing the construction of solar power plants with a combined capacity of 260 MW and creating the technical conditions for the installation of wind turbines. Total electricity production is projected to reach 40.9 billion kWh by 2030.

In parallel with expanding generation capacity, a programme to reduce electricity network losses is being implemented: losses in distribution grids will fall from 15.9% (2025) to 9.5% (2030), equivalent to savings of approximately 2 billion kWh per year and a substantial reduction in emissions from thermal electricity generation.

Substantial additional reductions in GHG emissions through 2035 could also be achieved by implementing large-scale energy efficiency projects in housing, social infrastructure and industrial facilities, conditional upon systematic support from international development partners.

Energy efficiency and renewable energy measures will provide targeted support for women’s participation in energy entrepreneurship and the green workforce, including vocational training programmes for women in technical and engineering positions. Investment and regulatory decisions concerning distributed generation will prioritize rural households experiencing seasonal difficulties with electricity supply, explicitly accounting for gender-related energy vulnerability, the time women spend collecting solid fuel (2.5–3 hours per day), and associated health risks for women and young children.

5.4.3. Transport

In 2022, transport sector GHG emissions amounted to 2.08 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (approximately 9% of national emissions), with road transport accounting for more than 99%. More than 527,000 motor vehicles are registered in the country, of which electric vehicles account for less than 1%.37

Under the Electric Transport Development Programme for 2023–2028 (No. 532), Tajikistan is implementing a package of transport electrification measures: procurement of electric buses in Dushanbe, followed by expansion to other cities; development of public charging infrastructure; tax and customs incentives for electric vehicle imports; and support for local manufacturing (Aves- to Automotive JSC).

An accelerated increase in the share of electric vehicles in new vehicle sales is planned, from less than 1% in 2022 to 50% by 2030 and 75% by 2035. The electrification target for two- and three-wheeled vehicles is 50% by 2030. A 15–20% reduction in average transport energy intensity by 2030 will be achieved through fuel economy standards and fleet renewal programmes.

According to NDC 3.0 model calculations, transport sector emissions should not exceed 2.91 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent by 2030 under the ‘With Measures’ scenario (2.81 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under ‘With Additional Measures’), and 3.04 million and 2.77 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent, respectively, by 2035, while simultaneously achieving the stated operational fleet electrification and energy efficiency indicators.38

Planning of low-carbon, climate-resilient transport infrastructure will ensure safety and accessibility for women, children, persons with disabilities and older persons, including consideration of gender-specific mobility patterns (women’s shorter but more frequent and multipurpose trips related to childcare and household management) when designing public transport routes, timetables and infrastructure in rural and mountain areas.

5.4.4. Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU)

Emissions in the IPPU sector amounted to 3.0 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent in 1990 (approximately 12% of national emissions). The largest current contributors are aluminium production (the state-owned TALCO enterprise, the country’s largest industrial GHG emitter), as well as cement, lime and chemical production.39

Tajikistan plans to implement a range of IPPU emission reduction measures: technical modernization of TALCO using best available techniques (BAT) to reduce PFC emissions from aluminium electrolysis; increasing the share of hydropower in the electricity supply of aluminium production; introducing alternative raw materials (fly ash and granulated slag) in cement production to reduce clinker content; and modernizing technological processes in the chemical industry.

Implementing these measures requires substantial foreign investment and technology transfer, which means that IPPU commitments are predominantly conditional. The measures are aligned with the State Socioeconomic Development Programme for 2026–2030, its priority areas for industrial development, and other strategic national policies.

By 2030, IPPU sector emissions should not exceed 4.9 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under the ‘With Measures’ scenario (4.1 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under ‘With Additional Measures’), and by 2035 should not exceed 4.4 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (3.5 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent, respectively), while also achieving operational targets for technological modernization of production.40

Climate measures in industry aim to reduce the carbon intensity of production through technological modernization, greater energy efficiency and the adoption of new technologies. IPPU greenhouse gas emission targets are aligned with the accelerated industrialization objective (increasing industry’s share of GDP to 30% by 2030) and the Republic of Tajikistan’s Accelerated Industrialization Programme for 2026–2030.41

A competitive advantage of Tajikistan’s industry is access to electricity, almost 98% of which is produced from renewable sources. This creates opportunities to produce aluminium and other industrial goods with a low carbon footprint and enhances export competitiveness in markets applying carbon regulation mechanisms, including the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM).

The following have been identified as priority measures to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and implement climate investment projects in the IPPU sector:

  • in cement and building materials production—reducing the clinker content of cement, using alternative fuels and waste in kilns, introducing energy-saving technologies and dry-process production, and regularly monitoring emissions from cement plants;
  • in metallurgy—modernizing production facilities at TALCO OJSC, introducing energy-efficient electrolysis cells, reducing perfluorocarbon (PFC) emissions, and increasing the proportion of low-carbon (‘green’) aluminium;
  • in energy efficiency, new technologies and digitalization—introducing minimum energy efficiency standards for industrial equipment, recovering and utilizing waste heat, digitalizing production processes and energy accounting, and supporting innovation and technology parks;
  • developing a portfolio of low-carbon industry projects (TALCO modernization, low-clinker technologies in cement production, industrial energy efficiency, industrial waste processing and, at a later stage, green hydrogen production) as the basis for mobilizing climate fund resources and private capital and implementing Article 6 mechanisms under the Paris Agreement;
  • improving IPPU greenhouse gas monitoring, reporting and verification by including sectoral indicators (emissions intensity of cement and aluminium production, alternative fuel use, equipment modernization rates) and ensuring measurable emissions accounting at large industrial enterprises in cooperation with the CEP and the Agency for Hydrometeorology;
  • developing the workforce for low-carbon industry and creating green jobs while maintaining employment at existing facilities, including targeted training and retraining programmes in priority occupations in cooperation with sectoral scientific and educational institutions.

Measures under the unconditional industrial scenario will be implemented through existing sectoral programmes and allocated funds, without additional state budget appropriations beyond approved resources.

Under the ‘With Additional Measures’ scenario, the proposed activities will be implemented conditional upon access to international financing, technology transfer and technical assistance.

Industrial decarbonization and green production programmes will be accompanied by employment monitoring (including gender representation) and targeted vocational training programmes for women in technical and engineering positions.

5.4.5. Agriculture

Agriculture accounted for 19% of national base-year emissions (4.6 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent), with most sectoral emissions resulting from enteric fermentation in cattle and small ruminants, while N₂O emissions from soils accounted for 97–99% of national nitrous oxide emissions.42

Under the principal sector policy document, the Programme for Development of the Agrifood Sector and Sustainable Agriculture to 2030 (Government Resolution No. 54 of 1 March 2023), measures provide for the introduction of climate-smart farming, expansion of drip and sprinkler irrigation, soil fertility management using biofertilizers, and agricultural sector digitalization (2025–2029).

Measures to reduce emissions from livestock are also set out in the Comprehensive Development Programme for Livestock, Poultry, Fisheries and Beekeeping for 2023–2027 (Government Resolution No. 52 of 1 March 2023): improving feed availability and diets to reduce CH₄ emissions intensity, and improving manure storage technologies.

Sustainable pasture management is addressed in the Pasture Development Programme for 2023–2027 (Government Resolution No. 579 of 29 November 2022), including rotational grazing on 2.84 million hectares of agricultural pastures, restoration of degraded pastures, and establishment of a pasture cadastre.

The Investment Programme for the Modernization of Irrigation Systems (Agency for Land Reclamation and Irrigation under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan, Resolution No. 296 of 30 June 2023) and the Programme for Improving the Reclamation Status of Irrigated Land for 2024–2028 (Government Resolution No. 137 of 1 March 2024) improve pumping station energy efficiency and reduce N₂O emissions from excessive irrigation. As of 2026, 767,900 hectares of irrigated land were registered, and water supply contracts covered 523,800 hectares.43

By 2030, agricultural emissions should not exceed 7.1 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under the ‘With Measures’ scenario (6.0 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under ‘With Additional Measures’), and by 2035 should not exceed 6.8 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (5.2 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent, respectively).

Climate-resilient agriculture measures will ensure targeted access for women dehkan farmers to land, credit, climate-resilient seeds, agricultural technologies and extension services. In view of unpaid care work (an average of 7 hours 40 minutes per day for women compared with 40 minutes for men), investment is envisaged in labour-saving technologies and care infrastructure at rural community level.

5.4.6. Waste

In the base year, waste sector emissions amounted to 1.15 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (approximately 5% of national emissions), primarily CH₄ from anaerobic decomposition of organic matter in municipal solid waste landfills and N₂O from wastewater. Despite its relatively small absolute emissions, the sector has substantial untapped reduction potential at relatively low cost.44

Tajikistan plans to implement the following package of waste-sector measures: establishing separate municipal solid waste collection in Dushanbe and major cities, targeting at least 30% of municipal solid waste by 2030; constructing modern engineered landfills with gas collection systems; composting organic waste (target of at least 40% by 2035); and modernizing wastewater treatment facilities to reduce N₂O emissions.

Waste-sector measures are consistent with SDG 12, ‘Responsible Consumption and Production’, including waste prevention and reduction, reuse and recycling, environmentally sound waste management, and a gradual transition towards circular economy principles.

In line with international best practices, a priority for 2028–2035 is to establish a pilot project for capturing and utilizing landfill gas (biogas) at existing landfill sites around Dushanbe. Subject to international financial support, this measure will provide additional CH₄ emission reductions and may be implemented as a conditional contribution under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

By 2030, waste sector emissions should not exceed 3.0 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under ‘With Measures’ (2.70 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent under ‘With Additional Measures’), and by 2035 should not exceed 3.5 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent (2.94 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent, respectively), while also achieving operational targets for separate waste collection and recycling.45

Improved waste management measures take account of gender roles in household waste handling and provide for the involvement of women’s civil society organizations in community-level outreach and awareness campaigns on waste separation and recycling.

5.4.7. Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF)

In 1990, net emissions from the LULUCF sector amounted to 1.7 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent.46

Tajikistan’s forest cover extends over approximately 423,000 hectares (about 3% of national territory). Forests are primarily found in mountainous areas and include juniper, pistachio, walnut and tugai riparian ecosystems.47

In accordance with state programmes, forest-covered land should increase to 430,000 hectares by 2030. To achieve this indicator, activities are undertaken each year to establish new forests (at least 1,000 hectares per year) and restore forests (at least 2,000 hectares per year).

The key policy document is the State Environmental Programme of the Republic of Tajikistan for 2023–2028 (Government Resolution No. 53 of 1 March 2023), which provides for reforestation in mountain and foothill areas, restoration of protective forest belts, measures against illegal logging, and strengthening buffer zones around industrial enterprises.

Nature-based solutions are a priority for NDC 3.0 activities in LULUCF: agroforestry on degraded land, restoration of pistachio, almond and walnut ecosystems, and integration of LULUCF into the SDG framework (SDGs 13 and 15).

The unconditional LULUCF contribution includes annual reforestation and an increase in forest-covered area to 430,000 hectares by 2030; it provides for net removals of at least 1.0 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent per year by 2030, with this level of net removals maintained through 2035.

The conditional contribution provides for net removals of at least 1.0 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent per year by 2030 and at least 1.4 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent per year by 2035, conditional upon external support and transfer of monitoring technologies.

Forest carbon sequestration capacity is secured not only by establishing new plantations but also by conserving existing forests, enhancing their productivity and resilience, and ensuring the survival and maintenance of planted stands. LULUCF measures therefore provide for assisted natural regeneration, maintenance of newly planted stands, protection of forests from fires, illegal logging and uncontrolled grazing, and development of a national system to monitor forests and forest carbon.48

Reforestation and sustainable land-use programmes formally recognize women’s actual contribution to collecting non-timber forest products and fuelwood, an activity currently insufficiently reflected in official forest governance arrangements. Measures establish gender parity in joint forest management under the Forestry Agency model, including the provision of formal rights for women to use forest plots, and set participation indicators for young people (at least 50%) and women (at least 30%) in reforestation programmes.

6.1. Climate risks and vulnerability

6.1.1. Observed and projected climate impacts

The Republic of Tajikistan is significantly affected by climate change. According to the IPCC and national climate studies, these impacts are manifested through several interconnected pathways.

  • Rising temperatures. Mean annual temperatures in Tajikistan are increasing faster than the global average. Temperatures have risen by approximately 0.9°C since the 1940s and are expected to increase by 1.5–4°C by the end of the twenty-first century, depending on the climate scenario. Maximum summer temperatures in the valleys may rise by 5–6°C. These trends are supported by observations from the national hydrometeorological service and the World Bank CCDR models.
  • Glacier degradation. Tajikistan’s glaciers have shrunk by 30% over the past 70 years, while their volume has declined almost threefold. More than half of Central Asia’s glaciers may disappear by 2050 if current warming rates persist. This implies an increasing ‘glacier debt’: an initial temporary increase in meltwater runoff followed by a sharp decline, with critical consequences for irrigated agriculture in downstream areas.
  • Floods and natural disasters. According to the Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defence, 287 incidents were recorded in Tajikistan in 2025, including 83 mudflows, 79 landslides, 24 rockfalls and 14 episodes of rising water levels. Total damage amounted to 20.758 million somoni; 676 houses, 12 schools, three hospitals and 23.1 km of roads were damaged. Over the past five years, the number of emergencies tripled, from fewer than 800 to 2,400 incidents.
  • Land degradation and droughts. Annual losses from land degradation are estimated at USD 325 million (2.7% of GDP) and are projected to double by 2050. More than 50% of the country’s territory is affected by degradation. Water levels in the Nurek reservoir reached a ten-year low in 2024, leading to restrictions on electricity supply.

Identifying and ranking climate risks is a methodological prerequisite for adaptation planning, as it determines the prioritization of measures and allocation of limited resources among sectors. The analysis of observed and projected risks draws on hydrometeorological monitoring, national vulnerability assessments and climate change scenarios, providing an evidence base for subsequent decisions.

For Tajikistan, a mountainous country highly dependent on hydropower and irrigated agriculture, glacier degradation, changing runoff patterns and the increasing frequency of hazardous hydrometeorological events are particularly significant.

6.1.2. Sectoral and territorial vulnerability

Tajikistan’s climate vulnerability varies markedly across sectors and territories.

According to the ND-GAIN methodology, which assesses both vulnerability to climate impacts and countries’ readiness for adaptation, Tajikistan faces substantial climate risks in relation to food, water and ecosystems. Ranked 98th overall in ND-GAIN, the country has considerable scope to strengthen adaptive resilience, notably by expanding financial capacity, improving public governance mechanisms and building institutional capacity.50

Territorial vulnerability analysis identifies the areas most exposed to droughts, mudflows and water scarcity and compares these exposures with the adaptation readiness of local institutions. High sensitivity in the food, water and ecosystem sectors, coupled with limited financial resources, requires prioritizing efforts and resources in the most vulnerable areas.

6.2. Adaptation vision and goal

6.2.1. Long-term vision for adaptation priorities

The Republic of Tajikistan’s long-term adaptation vision is to build a climate-resilient society and economy capable of safeguarding well-being, food security, sustainable water management, public health and ecosystem services in the face of mounting climate stress.

This vision is based on the following strategic priorities:

  • conservation and restoration of water resources and the cryosphere;
  • resilience of agricultural systems and food security;
  • climate-resilient infrastructure and disaster risk reduction;
  • viability of mountain ecosystems and rural communities.

As a high-mountain country with enormous renewable energy potential, yet one of the countries most vulnerable to climate change, Tajikistan is both exposed to climate impacts and a key participant in the global energy transition. Its mountain ecosystems, glaciers, high-altitude pastures and rivers serve as a ‘water tower’ for a substantial part of Central Asia, making adaptation in Tajikistan an issue of regional as well as national importance.

The country’s long-term adaptation vision is aligned with the National Development Strategy to 2030, the State Socioeconomic Development Programme for 2026–2030, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030 and the UNFCCC framework decisions on the global goal on adaptation (the ‘Belém Adaptation Indicators’).

6.2.2. Adaptation goal

Under NDC 3.0, the Republic of Tajikistan sets the following climate change adaptation goal: by 2035, enhance the resilience of key life-support systems and economic sectors to projected climate risks, reduce losses from natural disasters by 40% compared with 2022–2024 levels, and strengthen the adaptive capacity of the most vulnerable communities.

To achieve this goal, Tajikistan has established the following specific, measurable objectives for 2030:

  • provide access to safe centralized or decentralized water supply for at least 70% of the population;
  • ensure that early warning systems for natural disasters cover at least four million people;
  • increase the share of land under climate-smart agricultural practices to 35%;
  • restore at least 200,000 hectares of degraded pastures and forests;
  • ensure that 75% of healthcare facilities are prepared for climate-related emergencies.

The NDC 3.0 adaptation goal represents progression beyond the 2021 NDC by introducing specific measurable targets, sectoral vulnerability analyses, a list of measures with responsible agencies and financing requirements, and a progress indicator framework aligned with national and international policy documents.

6.3. Priority adaptation areas

The adaptation component of the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 is a comprehensive, measurable and financially substantiated plan to strengthen the country’s climate resilience to adverse climate impacts.

Based on the climate risk and vulnerability analysis and national priorities, NDC 3.0 identifies priority adaptation areas, each forming a distinct cluster of measures and target indicators aligned with national programming documents.

6.3.1. Water resources and glaciers

Sustainable water management is among the highest-priority sectors in NDC 3.0 adaptation planning. Tajikistan has developed and is implementing a broad package of measures to adapt the water sector to climate impacts.

The Agency for Land Reclamation and Irrigation under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan is implementing an investment programme to modernize 69 pumping stations (approved by Government Resolution No. 296), with total funding of 889.7 million somoni. The Programme for Improving the Reclamation Status of Irrigated Lands for 2024–2028 (Government Resolution No. 137 of 1 March 2024) covers 18,000 hectares annually, with total funding of 22.3 million somoni.

Construction of the Rogun hydropower plant is the largest water-sector adaptation measure: the reservoir, with a capacity of 13.3 billion cubic metres, will provide flood protection for the downstream cascade, regulate flow during droughts and ensure reliable water supply for 10 million people.

The institutional foundation of water-sector adaptation is the water sector reform undertaken in accordance with the Republic of Tajikistan’s Water Sector Reform Programme for 2016–2025. The reform introduces basin-based integrated water resources management (IWRM), separates policy-making, regulatory and water service provision functions, and has established the National Water Council, basin organizations and councils, as well as the National Water Information System (NWIS) for collecting, processing and exchanging water-resource data.51

Long-term water-sector priorities are defined in the National Water Strategy of the Republic of Tajikistan to 2040, which provides for: completing the transition to IWRM at national and basin levels by 2030; increasing population coverage with centralized water supply to at least 90% and wastewater services to 50% by 2040; reducing irrigation system water losses and expanding water-saving technologies; digitalizing water resource management; and protecting against the harmful effects of water in the context of climate change. Implementation of the Strategy and its 2025–2027 Action Plan is coordinated by the Ministry of Energy and Water Resources.52

Water governance reform will help remove barriers to women’s leadership in water user associations. Water supply measures prioritize reducing the time women spend collecting water (currently between 50 minutes and 4–6 hours per day). Irrigation infrastructure development includes targeted training for women in water-saving technologies.

Interstate cooperation through the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS) is also a critically important component of climate adaptation in the Aral Sea basin.

Glacier conservation is an important strategic priority reflected in the Addresses of the President of the Republic of Tajikistan. Tajikistan initiated a global glacier preservation movement in international forums, contributing to the adoption of a United Nations General Assembly resolution on glaciers. National programmes cover cryosphere monitoring, glacial lake outburst flood modelling and preventive protection of populations in at-risk areas. Six automatic hydrological stations on key glacier-fed catchments provide real-time data for water resources management.

6.3.2. Agriculture and food security

Agriculture is among Tajikistan’s most climate-vulnerable economic sectors. It employs approximately 60% of the country’s workforce and contributes around 22% of GDP. Food imports account for approximately 42% of calories consumed. Available projections suggest that, under a pessimistic scenario, climate change could substantially threaten rainfed crop yields (potential declines of 15–20%) and livestock productivity (a decline of 20%) by 2050.

The main instrument for agricultural adaptation is the Programme for Development of the Agrifood System and Sustainable Agriculture to 2030 (Government Resolution No. 54 of 1 March 2023), which provides for climate-smart farming, expansion of drip irrigation, a 15% yield increase, 20% water savings and agricultural digitalization. Under the programme, 1,300 hectares of orchards and vineyards had been established by 2026.

Livestock adaptation is supported by implementation of the Comprehensive Development Programme for Livestock, Poultry, Fisheries and Beekeeping for 2023–2027 (Government Resolution No. 52 of 1 March 2023) and the Pasture Development Programme for 2023–2027 (Government Resolution No. 579 of 29 November 2022).

Food security measures include diversifying crop production by expanding drought-resistant cereal and legume varieties, introducing agroforestry, and establishing agroparks and logistics centres to reduce post-harvest losses.

Agricultural insurance and lending to climate-smart farms are expected to strengthen the financial resilience of rural households under adverse climate scenarios.

Several projects are being implemented with World Bank support, including ‘Strengthening Agricultural Resilience’ (USD 108 million) and ‘Community-Based Agriculture Support’ (USD 43.5 million).

6.3.3. Health

Climate change poses a serious threat to public health. Rising extreme temperatures, deteriorating water quality during floods and droughts, the expanding geographic range of vector-borne and intestinal infections, and increasing emergencies pose growing risks to the population.

Climate risks to healthcare infrastructure are also increasing. In 2025, 287 climate-related emergencies were recorded in Tajikistan, causing damage to health infrastructure of 853,000 somoni (three hospitals and diagnostic centres were damaged).

The principal sectoral adaptation instrument is the ‘Action Plan for Protecting Health from Heatwaves and Other Climate Factors for 2024–2026’, approved in December 2023 (Ministry of Health Order No. 816).

A new ‘National Action Plan for Protecting Health from Climate Factors for 2027–2032’ is scheduled for preparation in 2027.

Under these plans, more than 24 information leaflets have been produced on the health effects of heatwaves, dust and cold; an inter-agency working group involving WHO, UNICEF and Caritas Switzerland has been organized; and a Public Health Emergency Response Centre has been established.

At the same time, measures are being implemented to strengthen early warning of public health emergencies: a dedicated tool for assessing healthcare facility preparedness has been developed with development partners; emergency stocks of medicines and protective equipment are being established (Ministry of Health Order No. 474 of 28 July 2025); and sanitary and quarantine checkpoints are being expanded to prevent the spread of particularly dangerous infections.

Adapting the health system to climate risks will ensure gender-responsive maternal health protection during extreme heat (above 33–37°C, associated with increased pregnancy complications), prioritize investments in rural health infrastructure to address projected increases in climate-sensitive childhood illnesses, and integrate maternal and reproductive health monitoring into climate monitoring and evaluation systems.

6.3.4. Disaster risk reduction

Disaster risk reduction is one of Tajikistan’s main climate adaptation priorities. The country is highly exposed to flooding, landslides, fires and other natural hazards.

According to a World Bank regional assessment, natural disasters could cause losses equivalent to 18% of the country’s GDP under a worst-case scenario.

The main national disaster risk reduction document is the Republic of Tajikistan’s National Disaster Risk Reduction Strategy for 2019–2034 (approved by Government Resolution No. 630 of 29 December 2022), implemented in conjunction with the Medium-Term State Programme for the Protection of the Population and Territory from Emergencies for 2023–2028.

Climate risk assessment draws on statistics concerning hazardous hydrometeorological events and their impacts. Overall emergency statistics are presented separately, distinguishing climate-related events from other incidents. Assessments of impacts on people, infrastructure and the economy specify the observation period, coverage and calculation methodology.

In accordance with the Sendai Framework, Tajikistan is developing the following disaster risk reduction mechanisms: multi-level early warning systems; integration of climate and disaster risk into development planning; creation of a reserve fund for responding to emergencies and natural disasters; insurance-based risk-sharing instruments; gender-sensitive risk management approaches; and strengthened institutional capacity at local level.

Early warning and evacuation systems incorporate gender considerations into their design, including prevention of and protection from gender-based violence, multilingual and accessible digital warning systems, and inclusive safe areas that address the specific sanitation, health and childcare needs of vulnerable groups. Disaster risk management plans at all levels include mandatory gender-sensitive and socially inclusive risk assessments aligned with the Medium-Term Disaster Risk Management Programme (2023–2028), which already provides for stronger gender inclusion and private sector engagement. Targets include increasing women’s participation in local risk management institutions.

6.3.5. Energy and infrastructure

The country’s energy sector simultaneously offers substantial mitigation potential (hydropower as a low-carbon and dispatchable generation source) and faces climate risks (dependence of hydropower operations on runoff regimes). Seasonal electricity shortages occur during winter, highlighting the need to expand generation capacity, increase energy efficiency and strengthen the resilience of the energy system. Accordingly, key energy adaptation measures under NDC 3.0 will include developing systems for monitoring reservoir levels and forecasting hydrological conditions.

Tajikistan’s transport sector is highly vulnerable to climate impacts: much of the road network traverses mountainous terrain exposed to landslides, mudflows, avalanches and damage to engineering structures during extreme precipitation. Priority adaptation measures include incorporating climate parameters (design stormwater flows, asphalt pavement temperature limits and winter standards) into national building codes, regulations and design documentation; reinforcing riverbank protection, drainage structures and avalanche protection along international and national roads; establishing automated monitoring of mountain road sections and bridges; and developing alternative bypass routes and backup transport corridors.

6.3.6. Ecosystems, forests and biodiversity conservation

Tajikistan’s ecosystems perform critical regulatory functions, including water catchment, carbon storage, soil protection and biodiversity conservation. Conversely, forest and pasture degradation increases flood, landslide and drought risks, creating major threats to national ecosystems.

The State Environmental Programme for 2023–2028 (Government Resolution No. 53 of 1 March 2023) provides for reforestation in mountain and foothill areas (more than 1,000 hectares of new planting and more than 2,000 hectares of forest restoration annually); expansion of forest cover from 423,000 to 430,000 hectares by 2030; restoration of protective forest belts along roads and rivers; and greening of sanitary buffer zones. The programme includes cultivation and maintenance of seeds of more than 30 forest-forming tree species, including pistachio, walnut, almond and juniper.

The principal instrument for expanding vegetation cover is the State Greening Programme of the Republic of Tajikistan to 2040, which establishes annual targets for tree planting and greening of settlements. Measures in this section are aligned with the ten national targets set out in the National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP).

Nature-based solutions also play a special role in the adaptation component of NDC 3.0. The country intends to expand agroforestry as a means of increasing both productivity and carbon stocks; restore upper-catchment mountain ecosystems to regulate water flows; and develop REDD+ as a mechanism for financing reforestation.

Forestry adaptation measures include restoring mountain, floodplain (tugai) and protective forests that provide water regulation, erosion control and mudflow protection; improving forest resilience to drought, fire, pests and disease, including by selecting drought-resistant native species and improving forest health monitoring; and expanding joint forest management with local communities, forest users and dehkan farms, including contractual forest-use arrangements on State Forest Fund land.

Biodiversity conservation and management measures for protected natural areas and glacier zones will ensure gender-balanced participation in governance and prioritize the engagement of women scientists, including through the existing Association of Women Glaciologists at the National Academy of Sciences, which monitors glacier conditions under climate change.

6.3.7. Resilience of mountain and rural communities

Tajikistan’s mountain and rural communities (particularly those in GBAO, Rasht and the highland districts of Sughd and Khatlon) are especially vulnerable to climate change due to their remoteness, limited infrastructure, high dependence on natural resources and climate-sensitive income sources.

According to projections in the World Bank CCDR, up to 29–30 rural districts may be vulnerable to climate-related migration by 2030. Annual losses from land and pasture degradation disproportionately affect mountain communities because their livelihoods depend most heavily on natural ecosystems.

To strengthen the resilience of mountain communities, Tajikistan is implementing measures including livelihood diversification (agritourism, processing of pistachios, walnuts and medicinal herbs, traditional crafts); development of rural digital infrastructure; expanded coverage of adaptive social protection; promotion of women’s and young people’s employment in green sectors; and their participation in natural resource management.

Mountain ecosystem resilience is also directly linked to water and food security because high-altitude catchments generate river flows that supply valley irrigation systems. Protecting and restoring mountain ecosystems therefore creates adaptation synergies across water resources, agriculture, energy and disaster risk reduction.

The State Socioeconomic Development Programme for 2026–2030 includes a separate package of measures to support the country’s mountain districts.

6.3.8. Gender equality, social inclusion and just transition

Climate adaptation in the Republic of Tajikistan is guided by the principles of gender equality, social inclusion and just transition. Women, children, persons with disabilities, older people, rural households with high rates of labour migration, residents of remote mountain areas and young people bear a disproportionate burden of climate risks.

Key adaptation measures include expanding adaptive social protection and climate-responsive social transfers for vulnerable groups; recognizing and reducing unpaid care work and the additional burden placed on women during climate crises; improving systems for the collection, analysis and monitoring of sex-disaggregated data; integrating gender needs into early warning, evacuation and humanitarian assistance; increasing the participation of women and young people in local decision-making, including water, pasture and forest management; supporting green employment and new vocational skills among workers in climate-vulnerable sectors; and developing inclusive, gender-sensitive rural health, water and sanitation infrastructure. Implementation of these measures is reflected in the National Social Protection Strategy of the Republic of Tajikistan to 2040 and recommendations from the UNFCCC’s enhanced Gender Action Plan.

6.4.2. Expected outcomes

The envisaged adaptation measures are expected to deliver the following climate resilience outcomes across key sectors:

  • Sustainable water management. Completion of Rogun HPP will enable the regulation of 13.3 billion cubic metres of water, substantially improving sustainable water management in the Amu Darya basin. Modernization of 69 pumping stations under the Agency for Land Reclamation and Irrigation’s investment programme (total financing: 889.7 million somoni) and rehabilitation of irrigation systems will increase irrigation efficiency from 30% to more than 55% by 2030, saving up to 2 billion cubic metres of water annually. At least 70% of the population will have access to safe water supply by 2030.
  • Food security and resilience. Introducing climate-smart farming on 35% of agricultural land and expanding drip irrigation will raise wheat yields to 3 tonnes per hectare by 2030 (a 36% increase). Restoring 200,000 hectares of degraded pastures will reduce risks to livestock and methane emissions.

Disaster risk reduction. Implementation of national DRR programmes will extend early warning system coverage to four million people by 2030. Climate-resilient infrastructure will reduce flood damage to roads and bridges by 60%. A dedicated fund and insurance mechanisms will strengthen the financial resilience of households and jamoats during extreme climate events.

  • Reforestation and ecosystem services. Expanding forest cover from 423,000 to 430,000 hectares by 2030 and restoring 200,000 hectares of degraded land will increase carbon sequestration, reduce water erosion on slopes and improve the water catchment functions of mountain watersheds. Agroforestry and protected natural areas will create additional income opportunities for rural households.
  • Public health. Implementing action plans on protecting health from climate factors will reduce the incidence of climate-sensitive diseases by 25–30% by 2030. Preparedness programmes covering 75% of healthcare facilities will ensure timely medical care during natural disasters.
  • Macroeconomic benefits of adaptation. According to World Bank CCDR estimates, adaptation investment of up to 1.7% of GDP by 2050 will reduce climate-related GDP losses from 5–6% to 2–3.8% by mid-century. Each dollar invested in adaptation will generate economic benefits exceeding USD 3–5 in avoided losses.

The Republic of Tajikistan commits to including updated adaptation indicator data in Biennial Transparency Reports in accordance with UNFCCC decision 18/CMA.1.

Monitoring of NDC 3.0 adaptation indicators will be carried out through the MRV system, integrated with the Paris Agreement transparency mechanism.

The entities responsible for collecting and providing relevant data will include the Agency for Hydrometeorology, the Agency on Statistics under the President of the Republic of Tajikistan, the Ministry of Agriculture, the Forestry Agency under the Government, the Ministry of Health and Social Protection of the Population, and the Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defence.

CHAPTER 7. INTEGRATION OF GENDER EQUALITY AND SOCIAL INCLUSION INTO NDC 3.0

7.1. Importance and Relevance of Gender Equality and Social Inclusion

In the Republic of Tajikistan, climate change is one of the systemic factors affecting sustainable socioeconomic development, the population’s quality of life and the resilience of local infrastructure. An analysis of climate trends in the country indicates increasing risks associated with accelerated glacier melt, soil degradation, water scarcity and the growing frequency and intensity of hazardous hydrometeorological events.

Accelerated glacier melt, soil degradation, shortages of water resources, particularly during the growing season, and increasingly frequent and destructive mudflows and floods disproportionately affect women, children, young people and socially vulnerable groups because of socially determined roles and limited access to productive assets. According to the relevant government agencies, in some years dozens of flood and mudflow events have been recorded, confirming the need to further strengthen early warning measures and climate-resilient infrastructure.

In response to these challenges, from 2026 Tajikistan is strengthening its climate commitments through the preparation of a new generation of nationally determined contributions (NDC 3.0), taking account of the following key considerations:

  • Tajikistan’s commitment, as a Party to the Paris Agreement, to pursuing the temperature goal established in that Agreement and subsequent decisions of the Conference of the Parties serving as the meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement (CMA), aimed at limiting the increase in global temperature to 1.5°C by the end of the century. From a GESI perspective, remaining within this temperature threshold is important to prevent irreversible degradation of the ecosystems on which the most vulnerable rural communities depend.
  • The need, identified by the Global Stocktake, to strengthen global climate efforts and formulate ambitious but achievable targets for reducing emissions and enhancing removals. Ambition should be assessed not only in technical terms but also by the quality of social safeguards available to the population.
  • The need for alignment with national circumstances relating to climate change and the long-term pathway towards low-carbon development. This requires consideration of just transition principles, including expanded economic opportunities and social support measures for vulnerable population groups.
  • The broader context of the Paris Agreement, including the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake (2023) and decision 1/CMA.5, which establish gender responsiveness and inclusion as cross-cutting considerations in all climate action.
  • Mechanisms for mobilizing resources to implement climate policy, including the state budget, international climate finance and domestic and foreign private investment in sustainable development. For the Inter-Agency Working Group on Gender Equality, this underscores the value of further developing gender-responsive and results-based budgeting approaches so that mobilized resources effectively address local needs.
  • The Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action (1995), and the Beijing+30 Action Agenda adopted following the sixty-ninth session of the Commission on the Status of Women (March 2025), marking the thirtieth anniversary of the Fourth World Conference on Women and identifying climate justice as one of six priority areas for global action. The Republic of Tajikistan has identified climate justice among its priority national actions for the coming five-year period, and NDC 3.0 is regarded as one of the principal national instruments for implementing this commitment and further mainstreaming gender considerations in national climate policy.53

Integrating GESI considerations into climate policy contributes to:

  • Greater effectiveness and more targeted and sustainable policy decisions;
  • Stronger resilience, since women and vulnerable groups are key agents of adaptation;
  • Economic efficiency, as inclusive measures encourage innovation and productivity;
  • Risk reduction, as addressing inequalities reduces vulnerability and the risk of maladaptation.

Integrating GESI unlocks the transformative potential of climate policy: women and vulnerable groups possess substantial local experience in sustainably managing natural and land resources and play an important role in climate change mitigation and adaptation.

7.3. Linkages between GESI and Climate Change

Climate change does not affect all people equally. Accelerated glacier melt, soil degradation and increasingly frequent and destructive mudflows and floods compound existing socioeconomic challenges and exacerbate inequalities in access to resources and recovery opportunities. Women, children, young people and other socially vulnerable groups bear a disproportionately heavy burden of climate risks, largely because of structural inequalities in access to information, resources, services and decision-making.

Addressing these inequalities is a prerequisite for effective climate outcomes:

  • Children under five are especially vulnerable to climate-sensitive diseases, including diarrhoeal diseases, respiratory infections and malnutrition; the incidence of such diseases is projected to increase by 11% by 2030. Extreme temperatures increase the risk of pregnancy complications, while rural health-care infrastructure remains insufficiently prepared to respond. Girls’ school attendance falls during climate-related shocks as responsibilities for caregiving and water collection increase.
  • According to studies conducted in Dushanbe, air temperatures exceeding the threshold of 37°C are associated with a statistically significant increase in cases of fetal distress and postpartum infections. Climate-related food insecurity also increases the prevalence of anaemia, creating additional risks for pregnant and breastfeeding women.
  • In the context of male labour migration, women frequently assume a broader range of responsibilities for managing rural households. Climate risks and temporary disruptions to local infrastructure and water supply systems may further increase domestic and family responsibilities. Households headed by migrant workers’ wives, widows and single mothers may face additional barriers to participation in climate governance and post-flood and post-mudflow recovery programmes. In some cases, access to climate finance and credit may be impeded because land or other property is registered in the names of absent family members or adult sons. This highlights the importance of further improving inclusive mechanisms for access to relevant support programmes.
  • Women account for more than 70% of those employed in agriculture, but a substantially smaller proportion of heads of dehkan farms (approximately 10–12%) and land users with formally registered land rights. This limits women’s access to credit, agricultural technologies, advisory and insurance services, and reduces their adaptive capacity as droughts and land degradation become more frequent.
  • Women and girls in rural areas bear the principal responsibility for supplying their households with water and fuel. Declining availability of water resources and biomass as a result of climate change increases the time spent on unpaid domestic work, restricts educational and paid employment opportunities, and heightens health and safety risks.
  • Women, persons with disabilities, older persons and female-headed households have lower coverage by early warning, insurance and social protection systems, increasing their vulnerability to natural disasters and climate shocks and requiring targeted, differentiated adaptation measures.

Climate risks affect not only households but also the resilience of economic activity: water shortages and logistics disruptions create operational risks for businesses, while men continue to predominate in the private sector’s technical and production functions. Further improvements to government regulatory and incentive mechanisms could expand private sector participation in financing and deploying gender-responsive climate solutions. Some companies already demonstrate good practices by adopting environmental, social and governance (ESG) principles, maintaining sex-disaggregated personnel records and applying inclusive human resources policies.

The National Green Economy Strategy of the Republic of Tajikistan (2023–2037) provides for the creation of 16,000 green jobs annually. Introducing targets for women’s participation would help overcome existing gender segregation in technical occupations in energy, industry and transport. The principle of a just transition implies that expanding opportunities in renewable energy, energy efficiency and ecotourism should be accompanied by targets for women’s and young people’s participation and skills development programmes that remove barriers to entry into these sectors.

Climate vulnerability and access to essential resources vary by region. In the Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region (GBAO), important factors include the remoteness of some settlements, complex mountain terrain, seasonal transport constraints and the need to further extend early warning system coverage to all segments of the population. Khatlon Region is characterized by considerable female participation in agriculture, male labour migration and the exposure of certain areas to flood risks. Sughd Region has a comparatively developed institutional base, although reliable irrigation water supplies for agricultural land remain a priority in some districts. Pilot social and behaviour change communication (SBCC) programmes and initiatives to increase women’s participation in governance are focused on Khatlon and GBAO, where gaps between climate risks and institutional capacity are greatest.

Key vulnerabilities requiring targeted policy attention:

Groups at elevated climate and social risk include:

  • Female-headed households (wives of labour migrants, widows and single mothers), including households where land rights are formally held by male family members, limiting access to climate-related credit and flood recovery programmes;
  • Women and children in remote mountain areas, particularly the Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region (GBAO);
  • Young women not in education, employment or training (NEET);
  • Persons with disabilities and older people in remote mountain areas with limited access to early warning systems;
  • Members of local communities without access to services in their own languages;
  • Children from climate-vulnerable agricultural households, who may also experience shortages of safe drinking water in schools after floods.

NDC 3.0 applies an intersectional approach to ensure these groups are visible in aggregate statistics.

7.5. Priority Measures under NDC 3.0

The priority areas are structured in accordance with the analytical opinion of Working Group No. 12 of 14 May 2026 and the materials of the inter-agency working meeting held on that date, supplemented by recommendations from consultations with academic institutions, civil society organizations and the private sector.

The proposed measures are aligned with the Paris Agreement, the principle of just transition, the Enhanced Lima Work Programme on Gender and the Belém Gender Action Plan (COP30, 2025). They are also consistent with the Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action and the Beijing+30 Action Agenda in relation to the priority area of climate justice.

7.5.1. Institutional Governance, Accountability and Coordination

As a systemic mechanism responsible for coordinating action and monitoring GESI aspects of NDC 3.0, it is proposed to strengthen both the mandate and staffing capacity of the existing structure and transform it into an effective Working Group on GESI and Climate, chaired by the Committee on Women and Family Affairs and involving line ministries, the statistics agency, civil society, young people and development partners, with a clear mandate and reporting cycle.

Priority measures in institutional governance, accountability and coordination will include:

  • Regulatory reform: conducting a cross-cutting GESI audit of climate legislation to identify potential legal gaps and integrate commitments relating to equality and social inclusion into action plans under relevant strategic planning documents covering the environment and climate change;
  • Establishing an integrated digital platform (dashboard): developing departmental software to automate monitoring and evaluation (M&E). The dashboard could serve as one instrument for verifying achievement of the social and inclusion indicators under NDC 3.0;
  • Institutionalizing knowledge through e-learning: establishing a permanent professional development system to strengthen institutional memory and analytical capacity at national and regional levels;
  • Methodological standardization: harmonizing approaches to population vulnerability assessment;
  • Establishing a small grants fund for local initiatives and a public feedback mechanism for stakeholder recommendations (CSO recommendation); including researchers from relevant research institutes in NDC 3.0 technical working groups;
  • Establishing targeted youth representation in the GESI and Climate Working Group, following the model of existing youth networks: formalizing at least two permanent seats for representatives of youth organizations (following the example of the Tajikistan Climate NGO Network) on the Working Group to ensure systematic youth participation in the formulation and monitoring of climate decisions;
  • Building the capacity of regional and rural CSOs and local government structures to integrate GESI into climate policy: expanding targeted training, methodological support and assistance with NDC monitoring beyond Dushanbe.

7.5.2. Disaggregated Data and Evidence Base

A sound evidence base is an essential prerequisite for targeted policies. Accordingly, strengthening data collection and analysis is a priority and a necessary condition for comprehensive implementation of NDC 3.0.

Priority NDC 3.0 measures for disaggregating GESI data and strengthening the evidence base will include:

  • Creating a unified demographic data collection system and developing a sectoral database of disaggregated data: gradually aligning national climate statistics with disaggregated reporting standards;
  • Strengthening the analytical capacities of government agencies: training in methodologies for working with social and gender-sensitive indicators;
  • Mapping vulnerable groups and emergency risks: digitizing information on high-risk areas and population groups requiring priority access to early warning and assistance;
  • Regular scientific and social monitoring of the impacts of climate change on society’s social structure;
  • Cross-cutting coding (tagging) of climate projects during registration and monitoring;
  • Making mandatory disaggregated monitoring a condition of financing government climate programmes: establishing a regulatory requirement for reporting disaggregated by sex, age and intersecting vulnerability factors as a mandatory condition for allocating state budget resources to climate programmes and projects.

7.5.3. Economic Adaptation, Just Transition and Access to Resources

To facilitate women’s and young people’s access to water, agricultural land, credit and green jobs under the principle of a just transition, and to observe gender parity in climate leadership, NDC 3.0 proposes the following measures:

  • Reforming water users’ associations (WUAs) and scaling up the Women’s Basin Forum to all river basins and a national platform: removing barriers to women’s leadership in local water resource management;
  • Strengthening the climate resilience of small dehkan farms headed by women: targeted support for the adaptive capacity of women farmers and women dehkan farmers contributing to regional food security;
  • Inclusive labour market modernization (green jobs): creating conditions to reduce migration pressures on young people and expand vulnerable groups’ participation in low-carbon economic sectors;
  • Gender parity in climate leadership: introducing measures to increase women’s and young people’s participation in formulating and implementing climate decisions;
  • Applying regulatory incentives and establishing a dialogue platform for business, government and civil society organizations;
  • Farmer field schools with priority participation by women and young people: organizing practice-based training in climate-resilient farming practices through the Ministry of Agriculture’s existing farmer field school model, ensuring priority access to new agricultural technologies for women dehkan farmers and young people;
  • Gender-responsive local adaptation plans with women as formal co-leaders: developing local adaptation plans in priority climate-vulnerable districts with mandatory female co-leadership and integration of risk assessments for vulnerable groups and children;
  • Diversifying energy supply to rural households: prioritizing investment in decentralized renewable energy and efficient heating solutions for rural households experiencing seasonal electricity supply difficulties, with direct benefits for reducing gender-related energy vulnerability, the time women spend collecting solid fuels and health risks for young children.

7.5.4. Social Norms, Legal Safeguards and Digital Inclusion

Under NDC 3.0, barriers arising from social norms that restrict women’s and girls’ participation will be addressed through strategic communication (SBCC), legal safeguards for persons with disabilities and digital solutions for geographically isolated and mobility-impaired people by implementing the following measures:

  • Introducing SBCC models: developing long-term strategic communication programmes to raise public awareness and progressively change social and behavioural norms, overcoming attitudes and practices that impede women’s and girls’ participation in climate initiatives, natural resource management, technical occupations and green employment;
  • Accessible public communication: increasing climate awareness among the population using accessible, nontechnical language;
  • Strengthening legal safeguards for vulnerable groups: developing regulations governing access by persons with disabilities to climate protection measures;
  • Using digital solutions, including elements of artificial intelligence (AI), to promote inclusion: piloting modern digital tools to overcome geographical isolation and physical barriers;
  • Making gender-responsive risk assessments a mandatory standard component of disaster risk management plans: introducing standards for gender-responsive and socially inclusive risk assessments in all disaster risk management plans, taking account of the differentiated needs of women, young people, children, persons with disabilities and older persons;
  • Monitoring changes in social norms using qualitative and quantitative indicators to track changes in attitudes towards women’s participation in decision-making, the division of unpaid work, access to climate-related resources and services, and public perceptions of women’s role in adaptation to climate change.

7.5.5. Community Adaptation, Early Warning Systems and Resilient Infrastructure

Under NDC 3.0, climate literacy, inclusive dialogue platforms from an early age, and adaptation of infrastructure and early warning systems to the needs of women, children, persons with disabilities and local communities will be promoted through the following measures:

  • Environmental education and knowledge transfer: developing climate literacy from an early age;
  • Scaling up basin-level dialogue platforms using the successful model of the Syr Darya Basin Youth Forum and Basin Women’s Forum;
  • Priority adaptation of social infrastructure: upgrading key facilities (schools, health-care facilities, roads and water supply systems) exposed to natural hazards;
  • Establishing water management training schools: addressing irrigation water shortages through training in water-saving methods and technologies;
  • Inclusive design of emergency safe areas: creating permanent safe areas and evacuation camps that meet the specific needs of vulnerable groups;
  • Expanding the linguistic accessibility of early warnings: ensuring access to timely and important information while taking account of linguistic diversity within local communities;
  • Integrating maternal, child and reproductive health into climate adaptation in the health sector: prioritizing investment in rural health-care infrastructure to respond to the projected increase in climate-sensitive diseases by 2030, with mandatory consideration of maternal and reproductive health needs during extreme temperatures.

The analysis of the recommendations of Working Group No. 12 thus identifies four cross-cutting strategic conclusions recommended for implementation under NDC 3.0:

  • Adopting an integrated nexus approach: linking GESI considerations to programmes and budget decisions on water, energy, disaster risk reduction and infrastructure;
  • Introducing transparent financial monitoring and adapting internationally recognized gender-tagging approaches for climate projects to the national budget planning system;
  • Progressively expanding consultations to district and community levels, with local CSOs and women’s and youth groups participating from the early stages of subproject planning;
  • Strengthening institutional memory through e-learning to reduce risks associated with regional staff turnover.

7.5.6. Addressing the Needs and Rights of Children and Young People

Measures presented in this section that are not incorporated into existing government strategies, programmes and action plans are considered possible areas for further work rather than approved commitments. Their implementation will be clarified as relevant provisions are incorporated into government programme documents, with precise references to those documents.

Children and young people in Tajikistan are particularly vulnerable to the adverse impacts of climate change. Under UNICEF’s methodology for assessing climate risks to children, Tajikistan’s Children’s Climate Risk Index (CCRI) is 5.4 on a 10-point scale. Under that methodology, the sub-index reflecting children’s exposure to climate and environmental shocks is 6.7, indicating a relatively high degree of national vulnerability to the adverse impacts of climate change and the need for appropriate response measures.

In recent years, Tajikistan has made significant progress in modernizing social infrastructure and improving its resilience to climatic factors and natural disasters; advancing climate education and green knowledge; expanding access to water, sanitation and hygiene services for children and young people; improving vulnerable groups’ access to healthy and safe food; strengthening health systems with consideration of climate risks; and implementing large-scale climate information and education campaigns.

Tajikistan’s climate policy priorities are formulated with explicit consideration of the interests of children and young people. The country’s principal strategic development documents emphasize the importance of an environmentally sound and safe environment and access to quality, safe infrastructure for vulnerable population groups, including children and young people. Recognizing the scale of these challenges, the particular vulnerability of these groups to climate impacts, and their potential to drive comprehensive changes for long-term national development, the Government of Tajikistan is taking systematic measures to protect them while expanding their opportunities to participate actively in climate action.

Developing climate education is a priority of Tajikistan’s national policy. In the preparation of curricula and teacher-training programmes on climate education, it is envisaged that all subject standards will be reviewed by 2030 to reflect climate change, and that climate considerations will be integrated into the national education system.

Providing opportunities for systematic professional development in climate education for teachers at all levels is also an important component of national policy. It is therefore planned to train teachers in operating school education systems with due regard for climate risks and to develop a two-day distance-learning module for the National Institute for Teacher Training for this purpose.

To involve young people and develop environmental skills, it is envisaged that the list of occupations taught by educational institutions will be reviewed in light of climate considerations, green economy priorities, geographical barriers and equal access for women and men to new climate-related fields. This is expected to provide training in green occupations for up to 50,000 young people by 2030.

Considerable attention in Tajikistan is given to creating green and resilient infrastructure for children and young people. Under NDC 3.0 measures, it is envisaged that photovoltaic systems will be installed in 500 schools and 250 health-care facilities by 2030, and that solar thermal collectors with a combined capacity of at least 5 MW will be installed for heating and hot-water supply in educational and health-care facilities, thereby expanding access to clean energy for more than 500,000 pupils and teachers.

To provide children and young people with a safe and comfortable environment, Tajikistan will also pay close attention to introducing and enforcing public building standards that take account of climatic and environmental factors and energy-efficiency requirements. By 2030, energy efficiency is expected to improve in 500 schools and 250 health-care facilities through building envelope retrofits and heating, ventilation and air-conditioning measures, reducing energy consumption by at least 25–30% and substantially reducing coal, firewood and diesel consumption in social infrastructure facilities.

Strategic national climate policy priorities during implementation of NDC 3.0 will also include ensuring priority access to clean water and modern sanitation services for schools, preschools and medical centres. To this end, measures are envisaged to improve the climate resilience of water supply, sanitation and hygiene systems for at least 500 schools and 250 health-care facilities, and to introduce resilient and inclusive design standards (including early warning systems) for new schools.

A key medium-term national climate policy priority will be improving the safety and resilience of social infrastructure to natural disasters and climatic factors. To reduce disaster risks and enhance the climate resilience of social institutions, nature-based solutions are expected to be introduced in 500 schools and 250 health-care facilities by 2030, and resilient solutions will be mainstreamed into the design of all new construction facilities.

Considerable attention will also be given to developing disaster early warning systems, with the necessary equipment expected to cover 70% of existing schools by 2030.

Providing social protection and psychosocial support to children and families affected by climate-related disasters, as well as introducing a humanitarian cash transfer system for households with children, will play an important role in national climate policy. This will reduce their vulnerability to natural disasters by expanding a humanitarian cash transfer programme that is expected to cover up to 250,000 households and up to one million children by 2030.

To improve the readiness of the social sector to respond to heatwaves, appropriate response plans are expected to be developed and implemented in ten regions, covering approximately 800 health-care facilities. By 2030, it is envisaged that 100% of paediatric health centres will have adopted standard operating procedures for managing heat stress, including staff training, drills and patient information, and that up to 80% of schools and kindergartens will have adopted heat safety protocols.

To improve the safety of children with disabilities during emergencies, thematic workshops and awareness-raising activities on relevant issues are planned.

The capacity of Tajikistan’s health system under NDC 3.0 will be strengthened by integrating climate-related health risks to children and young people into national health strategies and cross-sectoral plans, and by developing, regularly updating and implementing national and regional action plans to protect children from the adverse impacts of climate change. By 2030, climate-related risks to children’s health and appropriate response measures are expected to be included in the national health strategy and intersectoral plans.

Particular attention is also planned for national research assessing the effects of climatic factors on the health and academic performance of school and university students and developing recommendations to reduce adverse climate impacts on education.

Tajikistan recognizes the importance of young people’s participation in the development and implementation of climate policy, emphasizing actions to equip children and young people with the knowledge, skills and behaviours needed to understand and strengthen the links between climate change and natural ecosystems and to access green occupations and new business opportunities.

To develop climate-related skills and engage children and young people in climate action, 500 eco-clubs are envisaged by 2030, actively engaging up to 500,000 students.

Broader coverage of children and young people by public climate change awareness initiatives is envisaged through at least five major national conferences each year, with information on their outcomes reaching an audience of at least one million people. Systematic support is also planned for the development and capacity-building of members of the Youth Network on Water and Climate across all regions, reaching at least 5,000 participants by 2030.

Practical engagement of Tajikistan’s young people in climate policy implementation by 2030 is also envisaged through systematic mini-grant support for at least 200 youth-led ecosystem restoration projects. National research capacity is also expected to be strengthened through at least five studies per year, supported by various funding sources, on climate change and related trends, involving regional universities and research centres and focusing on social inclusion.

Implementation of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 measures is also expected to ensure the integration of actions addressing the interests of children and young people into national strategic climate policy documents, with priority financing for these actions.

By 2030, it is also envisaged that agreed indicators relating to child well-being will be fully incorporated into national data systems (MICS, EMIS and HMIS). As part of NDC 3.0 implementation, UNICEF has also planned the national introduction of the CCRI-DRM system, with annual updates and management by a technical working group.

Gender equality and climate justice considerations, reflecting the interests of Tajikistan’s children and young people, will be addressed by integrating gender-responsive climate risk assessments into all stages of strategic climate document planning and incorporating gender and inclusion standards into sectoral climate change adaptation programmes and plans.

Climate action that safeguards the interests of Tajikistan’s children and young people will be financed from both domestic and external sources, including financial support mechanisms for projects involving young people’s scientific and community initiatives on climate and the environment, and expanded partnerships for mobilizing climate finance. The total financing required for Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 measures addressing the interests of children and young people is estimated at USD 1.975 billion.

7.5.8. Monitoring and Accountability

Progress on GESI commitments under NDC 3.0 is tracked through a core set of indicators disaggregated by sex, age and vulnerability, integrated into the monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) system and aligned with the UNFCCC Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF), national SDG reporting and commitments under the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW).

Reporting follows a bottom-up approach: the GESI and Climate Working Group collects and validates data annually through the dashboard; results are submitted to the National Development Council and integrated into national communications under the UNFCCC; and an independent gender audit of NDC implementation is conducted every two years, with findings published in the NDC Transparency Report.

The GESI indicators monitored under NDC 3.0 may also be used to prepare national reporting on implementation of the Beijing+30 Action Agenda in the priority area of climate justice, ensuring coherence between Tajikistan’s climate and gender accountability mechanisms.

Institutional accountability is ensured through annual GESI and climate reviews by the GESI Working Group, biennial gender audits of NDC 3.0 implementation and a publicly accessible feedback mechanism documenting how recommendations from civil society, academic institutions, women’s and youth groups, and the private sector have informed policy decisions.

NDC 3.0 commitment on gender equality and social inclusion: Tajikistan commits to pursuing a climate policy pathway that is gender-responsive, socially inclusive and grounded in social equity, recognizing women, children, young people and other vulnerable groups not only as particularly exposed to climate risks but also as indispensable partners in building the country’s long-term climate resilience.NDC 3.0 advances Tajikistan from gender awareness towards gender mainstreaming, and further towards a cross-cutting nexus approach and gender-transformative climate policy outcomes during implementation through 2030.

CHAPTER 8. FAIRNESS OF CLIMATE AMBITION

8.1. Fairness and Highest Possible Ambition

The principal concepts determining the nature and level of the Republic of Tajikistan’s climate commitments under the Paris Agreement and in the preparation of NDC 3.0 are the principles of equity and highest possible ambition, consideration of the outcomes of the first Global Stocktake (GST1), international and regional cooperation, and approaches to implementing Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.

The Republic of Tajikistan formulates its contributions from the perspective of international equity, recognition of the historical responsibility of major emitters and the country’s actual financial and technical capacities.

Equity is an important principle of the Paris Agreement. Article 2 provides for consideration of equity and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities when determining contributions. Each NDC should reflect the country’s highest possible ambition, in the light of its specific national circumstances.

8.1.1. Just Transition

The fairness of the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 is grounded in a combination of objective factors: the country’s historically negligible contribution to global greenhouse gas emissions, its high climate vulnerability and its limited economic capacities.

The Republic of Tajikistan is among the countries making the smallest contributions to global climate change: its total emissions account for less than 0.03% of the global total. Nevertheless, Tajikistan is experiencing some of the most severe consequences of climate change in Central Asia, including accelerated glacier melt, more frequent extreme hydrometeorological events, pasture degradation and declining crop yields.

The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, enshrined in the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement, requires the major historical emitters to assume commensurate responsibility for providing climate finance and technology transfer to vulnerable developing countries.

GDP per capita and the Human Development Index (0.685 in 2024) point to substantial macroeconomic and structural constraints on the country’s ability to implement climate measures without international support.

A justification of the fairness of the country’s contribution requires quantitative evidence translating qualitative arguments concerning historical responsibility and vulnerability into internationally comparable indicators. Compiling comparative indicators makes it possible to compare Tajikistan’s per capita and aggregate emissions, its share of cumulative global emissions and its level of economic development with global averages. This comparison supports the conclusion that Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 exceeds the level of ambition that would be expected in light of its historical contribution to climate change.

8.1.2. Rationale for the Level of Ambition

The Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 establishes ambitious yet realistic targets based on scenario modelling and an assessment of sectoral mitigation potential.

Under the Paris Agreement, each successive NDC is expected to represent a progression beyond the previous contribution. Tajikistan has consistently pursued this requirement, progressively raising its targets from the INDC to NDC 3.0.

A further indication of enhanced ambition is expanded greenhouse gas coverage: while the INDC and NDC 2.0 covered only CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, NDC 3.0 also encompasses HFCs, PFCs, SF₆ and NF₃.

CHAPTER 9. INTERNATIONAL COOPERATION MECHANISMS

9.1. International Cooperation

International cooperation is essential to the implementation of Tajikistan’s climate policy. As a mountainous country located in the centre of the Eurasian landmass, Tajikistan cannot address regional and global climate challenges unilaterally.

The country’s international climate cooperation therefore follows several interconnected tracks: regional cooperation, integrated water resources management, and mobilization of international technical and financial support for priority climate initiatives.

9.1.1. Regional Cooperation

Tajikistan engages in regional climate cooperation through international institutions. It actively draws on its leadership and partnership roles to advance the regional climate agenda.

From 2020 to 2023, the Republic of Tajikistan chaired the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS). During this chairmanship, the fourth Aral Sea Basin Programme (ASBP-4) was adopted and is being implemented, incorporating measures on climate adaptation, integrated water resources management and capacity-building for countries of the region.

Tajikistan also initiated the designation of 2025 as the International Year of Glaciers’ Preservation (United Nations General Assembly resolution A/RES/77/158 of 14 December 2022), reflecting the transboundary scale of glacier degradation and the importance of glacier monitoring for humanity, as well as the country’s leading role in advancing this initiative globally.

9.1.2. Integrated Water Resources Management and Energy Links

Hydropower development, better hydrometeorological forecasting and improved water resources management create opportunities to strengthen climate resilience and mutually beneficial regional cooperation. Relevant initiatives are implemented in accordance with existing international arrangements, national priorities and the need for coordination among the countries concerned.

Tajikistan is Central Asia’s “water tower”: around 60% of the runoff of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers is formed within its territory, supplying water resources to approximately 50 million people in the region. Climate change directly affects the flow regimes of these rivers through glacier melt and changes in precipitation.

Tajikistan’s glaciers (11,528 glaciers covering 7,624 km² according to the 2023 State Glacier Cadastre) provide water reserves of critical importance for irrigation and water supply throughout Central Asia. Projections indicate that under the RCP 4.5 scenario, Tajikistan could lose a further 40–60% of its glacier cover by 2100, significantly altering the seasonal distribution of runoff across the region.56

The systematic development of hydropower in the country, particularly the construction of the Rogun Hydropower Plant with a multi-year regulating reservoir, will enable more predictable management of water flows in the interests of all countries of the region. The Rogun Hydropower Plant will also make it possible to export 17–20 billion kWh of clean hydroelectricity annually, replacing coal-fired generation in neighbouring areas (Pakistan and Afghanistan). This project exemplifies practical implementation of the regional water–energy nexus in support of sustainable low-carbon development.

In the context of climate adaptation, Tajikistan is also developing regional early warning systems for extreme hydrological events. Glacier and river-flow monitoring data collected by Tajikhydromet are transmitted to regional data centres and used by partner countries to plan water management measures and respond to emergencies in a timely manner.

9.2. Approaches to Implementing Article 6 of the Paris Agreement

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement establishes a framework for international market and non-market cooperation to achieve climate goals.

Operational rules for all three mechanisms under Article 6 were adopted at COP26 in Glasgow (2021): cooperative approaches (Article 6.2), the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (Article 6.4) and non-market approaches (Article 6.8).

Article 6 mechanisms offer Tajikistan opportunities to attract international investment, enhance NDC ambition, transfer technologies and build capacity.

The Republic of Tajikistan considers voluntary cooperation under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement to be one means of attracting investment, transferring technologies and enhancing climate ambition. Participation in relevant mechanisms will comply with applicable CMA decisions, environmental integrity requirements and the avoidance of double counting, including corresponding adjustments where required. Decisions on participation and authorization will be taken in accordance with established national procedures.

9.2.1. Potential Cooperation Mechanisms

Tajikistan has considerable potential to participate in cooperative approaches under Article 6.2.

Priority areas for further assessment of cooperation opportunities under Article 6 mechanisms include renewable energy, energy efficiency, sustainable management of forests and other ecosystems, and waste management. 9.2.2. Environmental Integrity Requirements

Environmental integrity is a fundamental principle of Article 6 mechanisms. The Article 6 rules adopted in Glasgow (2021) and refined at subsequent COP sessions set stringent requirements to ensure that international transfers of mitigation outcomes do not result in a real increase in aggregate global emissions through double counting. Environmental integrity in Article 6 activities requires the following:

  • Additionality: activities must generate emission reductions that would not have occurred in the absence of the Article 6 mechanism.
  • Permanence: long-term projects, particularly nature-based projects, must ensure the durability of outcomes and include mechanisms to protect against reversals.
  • Corresponding adjustments: the quantity of ITMOs authorized for transfer must be reflected through corresponding adjustments in national accounting to prevent double counting and ensure that emission reductions are genuine.

Sustainable development: all activities under Article 6 must contribute to sustainable development in the host country.

Stakeholder participation: project implementation must provide for inclusive consultations with local communities and safeguard their rights.

To meet environmental integrity requirements, Tajikistan plans to develop a national methodology for establishing emission baselines in priority sectors, aligned with IPCC methodologies and Article 6 rules. A project-level MRV system compatible with the national GHG inventory system will be established.

For the LULUCF sector, participation in Article 6 mechanisms and REDD+ will be based on national assessments of forest carbon stocks and removals by forests, establishment of forest and State Forest Fund land reference levels, development of a forest carbon MRV system using forest inventory materials, remote sensing data and field measurements, and mechanisms to prevent double counting and ensure fair benefit-sharing among the state, forestry organizations and local communities. The Forestry Agency under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan will carry out this work in cooperation with the Committee for Environmental Protection, with support from development partners.

In line with international good-practice recommendations, managing the ITMO registry and corresponding adjustments in the country requires the establishment of a national GHG registry, or extension of its functions, compatible with the international Article 6 transaction registry administered by the UNFCCC secretariat.

9.2.3. Transparency in Accounting for Transfers of International Mitigation Outcomes

Transactions will be tracked through national registry systems or access to the relevant international Article 6 infrastructure.

To ensure transparent accounting, Tajikistan will implement a package of measures to strengthen the national MRV system: improving the national GHG inventory, establishing an ITMO registry, training specialists in the application of IPCC methodologies in the context of Article 6, and developing regulations on ITMO authorization and monitoring.

10.1. Trends in Climate Finance and Official Development Finance

Historically, the Republic of Tajikistan has been one of the principal recipients of international support in Central Asia. Total official development finance (ODF) to the Republic of Tajikistan declined over 2020–2023, from USD 778.0 million in 2020 to USD 601.2 million in 2023. The cumulative nominal decrease over this period was approximately 22.7%, reflecting both the completion of certain large-scale development assistance programmes and generally tighter external financing conditions.

This trend increases the importance of mobilizing additional resources to implement NDC 3.0, including dedicated climate finance; underscores the importance of grants and concessional instruments for financing climate priorities, particularly adaptation; and highlights the growing role of private capital and domestic resources in financing climate projects.

During the period under review, disbursements from all official donors fell from USD 715.4 million in 2020 to USD 528.1 million in 2023, a cumulative decline of approximately 26.2%. Multilateral organizations play a central role in official development support for Tajikistan: disbursements from this source amounted to USD 556.8 million in 2020, USD 396.4 million in 2021, USD 447.1 million in 2022 and USD 367.5 million in 2023, accounting for more than half of the country’s total official development finance.

Against declining multilateral funding, contributions from member countries of the Development Assistance Committee (DAC) increased steadily: their disbursements rose from USD 112.8 million in 2020 to USD 159.3 million in 2023. Conversely, financing from non-DAC donors fell from USD 45.9 million in 2020 to USD 1.2 million in 2023. European Union institutions provided between USD 25.3 million in 2020 and USD 65.2 million in 2023.

Actual disbursements through official development finance channels totalled USD 584.4 million in 2023. The principal disbursements that year went to energy (USD 92.6 million), health (USD 74.5 million), agriculture, forestry and fisheries (USD 57.2 million), transport and storage (USD 37.2 million), and water supply and sanitation (USD 36.3 million).

The Asian Development Bank (ADB) provided substantial disbursements: USD 159.4 million in 2020, USD 119.7 million in 2021 and USD 213.9 million in 2022. In 2023, the World Bank Group (principally through the International Development Association, IDA) was the largest source of official development finance, providing approximately USD 247–264 million. The continued leading role of concessional multilateral finance confirms its systemic importance for the country’s sustainable development and provides an institutional basis for expanding climate investment.

The Islamic Development Bank (IsDB) and Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) also played significant roles. 10.2. Financing Requirements for NDC 3.0 Measures

Implementation of the Republic of Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 measures is not possible without adequate financing, transfer of advanced technologies and capacity-building.

Articles 9, 10 and 11 of the Paris Agreement establish provisions on support by developed country Parties to developing countries through climate finance, technology transfer and capacity-building.

The Republic of Tajikistan considers its contribution in light of equity and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances. 10.2.1. Total Climate Finance Needs under the “With Measures” Scenario

The total financing requirement for key climate policies, measures and projects comprising Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 “With Measures” scenario over 2026–2035 is estimated at USD 20.0 billion.

This estimate comprises the financing needs of activities under key government programmes, national strategies and strategic investment projects included in the country’s government programmes, totalling USD 14.5 billion (72.5% of total financing needs), and more than 80 large climate investment projects being implemented or planned with development partner support, requiring a combined USD 5.5 billion (27.5% of the total).

A distinctive characteristic of the portfolio is the high concentration of climate investment needs in large infrastructure projects. Financing requirements for the largest energy projects (construction of the Rogun Hydropower Plant and the national component of the regional CASA-1000 project) amount to approximately USD 12.5 billion, or around 62% of total estimated climate finance needs under the “With Measures” scenario through 2035.

Other substantial needs arise in the water sector, including water supply, wastewater management and irrigation (approximately USD 1.9 billion), transport, agriculture, disaster risk reduction, forestry and waste management.

Under the “With Measures” scenario, mitigation projects account for USD 15.4 billion in NDC 3.0 financing needs, representing 76.8% of the total.

Key mitigation projects include construction of the Rogun Hydropower Plant and modernization of the Nurek Hydropower Plant, the Kayrakkum Hydropower Plant (USD 196.0 million) and the Golovnaya Hydropower Plant.

Substantial investment is also envisaged for other renewable energy sources under the sectoral programme and pilot solar generation projects.

Major investment projects are also planned to modernize Dushanbe’s combined heat and power plant and district heating system.

Approximately USD 0.7 billion is expected to be invested in transport decarbonization, principally urban electric transport, charging infrastructure, green highway networks and the Danghara–Guliston green corridor (USD 150.8 million).

Planned investment in measures under Tajikistan’s Green Economy Development Strategy and energy-efficiency projects in agriculture, industry and housing and communal services is approximately USD 0.5 billion.

10.2.3. Principal Climate Finance Mechanisms for Implementing NDC 3.0 Measures

Given the need to mobilize substantial financial resources for NDC 3.0 measures (approximately USD 20.0–22.5 billion), expanding the country’s access to a broad range of climate finance sources and mechanisms will play a central role in achieving Tajikistan’s national climate ambitions.

Tajikistan intends to expand the mobilization of international climate finance based on national priorities, substantiated needs and prepared investment proposals.

At COP29 (Baku, 2024), Parties to the UNFCCC adopted the New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) on climate finance—USD 300 billion per year by 2035 for developing countries, together with the mobilization of at least USD 1.3 trillion annually from all sources by that date. This decision provides a legal basis for Tajikistan to seek access to an appropriate share of international climate finance.

The Republic of Tajikistan seeks to expand access to international climate finance in line with its national needs and NDC priorities. The NCQG provides an international benchmark for scaling up support to developing countries.

To improve the effectiveness of climate resource mobilization, Tajikistan seeks to:

  • Strengthen the national climate finance coordination mechanism under the Committee for Environmental Protection, including the appointment of a dedicated climate finance adviser;
  • Develop and publish a registry of investment-ready climate projects with complete project documentation for the GCF, Adaptation Fund and international financial institutions;
  • Increase participation in the international climate process and relevant cooperation arrangements among developing countries, taking account of the interests of mountain countries and landlocked developing countries;
  • Ensure systematic reporting on support needed and received in accordance with ETF requirements and the modalities, procedures and guidelines (MPGs).

To address the financing gap in NDC 3.0 implementation, the Government has provided for several measures: a green taxonomy was developed and adopted in 2026; a pilot issuance of green bonds was successfully conducted in 2025; and access by national authorities to international climate mechanisms (GCF, Adaptation Fund and GEF) is being systematically expanded.

These decisions are consistent with IPCC recommendations and international good practices in climate finance for developing countries.

In the medium term, the following climate finance options will be particularly important for the country:

  • Loans from international financial institutions: concessional and investment loans from the World Bank, ADB, EBRD, European Investment Bank, IsDB, AIIB and other institutions should be considered a principal source of finance for capital-intensive projects in energy, water and transport;
  • Bilateral grants and technical assistance from countries such as Japan, Switzerland, the Republic of Korea, EU member states and Norway are particularly relevant for small and medium-sized projects concerning adaptation, disaster risk reduction, capacity-building, socially oriented measures and institutional development;
  • Vertical climate funds—including the GCF, Adaptation Fund, GEF, Climate Investment Funds and related programmes—are important sources of grant support and concessional loans for a wide range of mitigation and adaptation activities;
  • Public–private partnership mechanisms are appropriate for attracting private operators and investors to energy, urban water supply, wastewater treatment, waste management and other municipal and transport infrastructure projects;
  • Issuance of sovereign and corporate green, social and sustainability bonds to finance climate projects and attract institutional investors in domestic and international capital markets. The adoption of the national green taxonomy in 2026 has already established important prerequisites, and systematic work on ESG regulation is under way;
  • Energy service company mechanisms (ESCOs, super-ESCOs and revolving funds) are appropriate in the public and municipal sectors;
  • Carbon market mechanisms, including Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, are suitable for monetizing emission reductions from hydropower, renewable energy, forestry and energy-efficiency projects;
  • Direct private investment in renewable energy generation, electric transport, green entrepreneurship and agro-processing, including through risk-sharing instruments, guarantees and blended finance;
  • Private philanthropy: resources from charitable foundations (Autodesk, Michelin Corporate Foundation, Allen Family Foundation, Tomberg Family Foundation, Volvo Foundation and others) offer a niche but flexible funding source for pilot projects, education, youth and local climate initiatives.

At present, Tajikistan has access to resources from more than 40 climate funds and programmes, including multilateral and bilateral funds, corporate mechanisms and targeted programmes.

Alongside the GCF, GEF and Adaptation Fund, potentially relevant sources include the Special Climate Change Fund (SCCF), Climate Investment Funds (CIF), including the Clean Technology Fund (CTF, USD 5.8 billion), Pilot Program for Climate Resilience (PPCR, USD 1.2 billion), Scaling Up Renewable Energy Program (SREP, USD 720 million) and Forest Investment Program (FIP).

Funds administered by international financial institutions have significant potential. World Bank programmes include the Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery (GFDRR), the Energy Sector Management Assistance Program (ESMAP, USD 811.9 million) and the Carbon Initiative for Development (Ci-Dev, USD 250 million).

ADB administers the Japan Fund for Prosperous and Resilient Asia and the Pacific (USD 966 million), Leading Asia’s Private Infrastructure Fund (LEAP, USD 1.5 billion), Clean Energy Financing Partnership Facility (CEFPF), Asia-Pacific Climate Finance Fund, Canadian Climate Funds for the Private Sector (approximately USD 227 million), Climate Change Fund (USD 98 million), Water Resilience Trust Fund and other dedicated mechanisms.

The EBRD implements the Finance and Technology Transfer Centre for Climate Change (FINTECC) programme, co-financed by the EU and GEF.

Dedicated agricultural opportunities include IFAD’s Adaptation for Smallholder Agriculture Programme (ASAP, USD 500 million) and the Islamic Development Bank’s Lives and Livelihoods Fund (USD 2.5 billion).

Intensifying engagement with these funds, including the accreditation of national organizations in Tajikistan, is one of the key prerequisites for expanding the country’s access to climate finance to implement NDC 3.0 measures.

10.3. Regulatory and Legal Framework for Mobilizing Climate Finance

Regulatory and legal arrangements are crucial to establishing an enabling investment environment for green finance. The Government is implementing a range of measures to improve the legal framework for attracting climate investment.

The State Environmental Programme of the Republic of Tajikistan for 2023–2028, adopted in 2023, provides for drafting a law on the green economy, introducing environmental taxation, establishing green public procurement mechanisms and updating environmental standards in key sectors.

Principal instruments for planning climate finance mobilization include the Republic of Tajikistan’s GCF Country Programme for 2024–2027, which defines a national portfolio of priority project concepts for GCF financing and is to be updated through 2030; a consolidated list of priority investment projects compiled by the authorized state investment agency; and the Climate Finance Plan of the Republic of Tajikistan, approved by order of the Chairperson of the Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government on 20 January 2024 and prepared with ADB support. This Plan, presented in July 2024, estimates the country’s total climate finance needs through 2030 at USD 8.41 billion.62, 63

The Law of the Republic of Tajikistan “On Renewable Energy Sources” will provide a legal basis for licensing renewable energy activities and introducing green certificates to verify the renewable origin of energy.

The Agrifood System Development Programme through 2030 provides for subsidy mechanisms for climate-resilient agricultural technologies (drip irrigation, resilient varieties and crop insurance), thereby reducing financial risks for private investors in climate-resilient agriculture.

Full operation of market mechanisms under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement will require several measures, including ITMO accounting rules, registry requirements, and standards for additionality and the permanence of carbon credits.

10.4. Regional Aspects of Climate Finance

Tajikistan occupies a strategically important position in Central Asia’s water resources system, generating approximately 60% of the runoff of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers, which are transboundary water bodies. Climate change directly affects the glacier-fed flow regime of these rivers, creating transnational risks to regional water and food security.

Several of Tajikistan’s climate projects therefore have a clear regional dimension, helping to address systemic climate challenges in Central Asia:

  • Glacier and hydrological monitoring systems: monitoring data collected by the Committee for Environmental Protection and Tajikistan’s Hydrometeorological Service are critically important to all Central Asian countries;
  • The regulating capacity of the Rogun Hydropower Plant will help balance river flows and make water supplies more predictable in dry years;
  • Nature-based solutions in river headwaters: forest restoration and improved mountain pasture management reduce soil erosion and enhance ecosystems’ water-regulating functions, benefiting water quality for all users in the region.

Tajikistan’s leading role as a provider of water and climate services to Central Asia is reinforced by its initiatives in international fora, including its chairmanship of IFAS and its initiative for the International Year of Glaciers’ Preservation (2025), supported by the United Nations.

Tajikistan’s international leadership in advancing glacier preservation and cryosphere research is also reflected in United Nations General Assembly resolution A/RES/78/321, “Decade of Action for Cryospheric Sciences, 2025–2034”, adopted on 13 August 2024 on the joint initiative of Tajikistan and France, and United Nations Environment Assembly resolution UNEP/EA.7/Res.10, “Preservation of glaciers and the cryosphere, particularly in mountainous regions”, adopted on 12 December 2025 at UNEA-7 in Nairobi on the proposal of Tajikistan and Peru.64, 65

As regional cooperation mechanisms develop further, joint financing arrangements with neighbouring countries are planned for projects concerning integrated water resources management, regional early warning systems and adaptation of mountain ecosystems.

International good practices based on IPCC and World Meteorological Organization recommendations indicate that coordinated regional approaches are substantially more cost-effective than purely national solutions.

10.5. Monitoring of Climate Finance

Effective use of climate finance requires a robust monitoring and evaluation (M&E) system for financial flows, technology cooperation and capacity-building activities.

IPCC recommendations emphasize that highly climate-vulnerable countries with limited capacities need dedicated M&E tools covering both incoming support and its results at national level.

Tajikistan submits its national GHG inventory and information necessary to track progress in implementing and achieving its NDC in accordance with Article 13 of the Paris Agreement and applicable guidance. Information on financial support, technology transfer and capacity-building needed and received is submitted with regard to Article 13, paragraph 10, and national capacities.

The first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1, 2024) established the basic reporting architecture, and the second report (BTR2) is under preparation.

To strengthen national climate finance M&E capacity, training is planned for at least 30 climate finance specialists at the Committee for Environmental Protection and key ministries, with support from development partners.

10.6. Technology Transfer and Capacity-Building

Technological development is essential for achieving Tajikistan’s climate goals. Under Article 10 of the Paris Agreement, Parties share a long-term vision of the importance of fully realizing technology development and transfer to improve resilience to climate change and reduce emissions.

10.6.1. Technology Needs

Tajikistan’s Technology Needs Assessment (TNA), conducted with UNEP/CNCN support, identified priority technologies for mitigation and adaptation sectors. Priorities were selected on the basis of cost-effectiveness, feasibility in Tajikistan, financing availability and the greatest possible contribution to sustainable development (Table 22).

Renewable energy technologies, particularly solar photovoltaics and small hydropower, have the highest mitigation priority.

For adaptation, water conservation and efficient water resource use technologies are the highest priority, including drip irrigation, rehabilitation of main canals and modern pumping stations.

Nature-based solutions (restoration of degraded forests, improved pasture management and shelterbelts) have high potential for both adaptation and mitigation through carbon sequestration. According to IPCC recommendations, they could deliver up to 30% of the global mitigation potential required by 2030 at relatively low cost.

The energy service company (ESCO) model is of particular interest to Tajikistan: it enables energy-efficiency investments without upfront expenditure by end-users, with investments repaid through documented energy savings. Pilot projects implemented with UNDP/GEF support have demonstrated the feasibility of the ESCO model in the country’s public institutions and industrial enterprises.

Priority technology needs are aligned with key national programme documents (the Green Economy Development Strategy 2023–2037, Renewable Energy Development Programme 2023–2027 and others), and provide guidance for attracting technical assistance through the technology transfer mechanism and support from climate funds.

10.6.2. Technology Transfer

Implementation of Tajikistan’s climate commitments depends to a considerable extent on successful technology transfer from developed countries.

Article 10.6 of the Paris Agreement provides for support to developing countries concerning technology development and transfer, including access to technology on favourable terms.

At present, technology transfer to Tajikistan occurs primarily through investment projects supported by international financial institutions (ADB, World Bank and EBRD), technical assistance from UN agencies (UNDP, FAO, WMO, UNEP and others), GIZ and bilateral cooperation with a broad range of countries.

The country’s technological development priorities include strengthening the capabilities of national enterprises and scientific institutions, developing innovation infrastructure and mechanisms to disseminate climate technologies, and improving regulatory conditions for their deployment. International technical support and partnerships will help adapt technological solutions to mountainous and rural conditions.

Key barriers to technology transfer include: limited technical capacities among local firms to adopt new technologies; an underdeveloped national innovation ecosystem (patent base and start-up infrastructure); high transaction costs in adapting foreign technologies to the specific conditions of a mountainous country; and insufficient regulation of renewable energy and energy efficiency.

10.6.3. Institutional and Human Resource Development

Capacity-building is the third pillar of the means of implementation under the Paris Agreement (Article 11).

Tajikistan needs systematic strengthening of national institutional and human capacities to manage climate risks effectively, attract finance and implement technological solutions.

Institutional capacity-building priorities include strengthening the Committee for Environmental Protection as national climate policy coordinator; establishing an inter-agency climate coordination council under the Government; developing a clear MRV system aligned with UNFCCC ETF requirements; and integrating climate budgeting into public financial management.

The Programme of Socioeconomic Development of the Republic of Tajikistan for 2026–2030 identifies human capital development for the green economy as a priority, providing for the establishment of a network of regional centres of competence in climate-resilient development.

The State Environmental Programme of the Republic of Tajikistan for 2023–2028 provides for training at least 500 environment and climate specialists during its implementation period, including through international internships and courses at partner universities.

International good practices supported by IPCC recommendations highlight the need to integrate climate issues into school and university curricula, provide professional retraining for civil servants and establish regional knowledge-sharing mechanisms.

Tajikistan therefore requires systematic support for training specialists in climate data management (meteorology, glaciology and remote sensing); climate finance and green investment; low-carbon technologies (renewable energy, energy efficiency and nature-based solutions); and developing and implementing climate projects with support from a wide range of donors and international financial institutions.

CHAPTER 11. NDC 3.0 IMPLEMENTATION, MONITORING AND TRANSPARENCY

11.1. Institutional Architecture

Effective implementation of NDC 3.0 requires clearly assigned institutional roles, systematic progress monitoring and transparent reporting in accordance with the Republic of Tajikistan’s international commitments.

Article 13 of the Paris Agreement establishes the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF), under which Parties monitor and report on progress towards climate targets, adaptation support and the receipt of international climate finance.

The institutional framework for implementing NDC 3.0 is based on a multilevel coordination structure encompassing national, sectoral and subnational levels.

11.1.1. Coordination Mechanism

The Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan (CEP) is the authorized state body for climate change and serves as Tajikistan’s national focal point for the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement. CEP is responsible for preparing, updating and coordinating the implementation of NDCs and preparing National Communications, Biennial Transparency Reports and national greenhouse gas inventories.

NDC implementation is coordinated by CEP in cooperation with the Centre for the Implementation of the Paris Agreement, the Agency for Hydrometeorology and relevant government bodies within their established mandates. Further improvements in inter-agency cooperation will proceed in accordance with national decisions concerning NDC implementation arrangements.

To coordinate NDC 3.0 preparation, an Inter-Agency Working Group (IAWG) was established with representatives of 24 relevant ministries, agencies and national institutions. During document preparation, the IAWG held regular working sessions, facilitated the submission and review of sectoral data, discussed proposed measures and coordinated the positions of relevant government bodies.

To ensure consistent and coordinated NDC 3.0 implementation, the possibility of establishing a permanent Inter-Agency Climate Coordination Council (IACCC) is expected to be considered. The IACCC could serve as an inter-agency body coordinating implementation, monitoring and periodic assessment of NDC 3.0 progress.

The proposed membership of the IACCC would include representatives of government bodies and institutions involved in preparing NDC 3.0, including:

  • The Chairman of the Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan, serving as Chairperson of the IACCC;
  • First deputy heads of the Ministry of Economic Development and Trade, Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Energy and Water Resources, Ministry of Agriculture, Ministry of Transport, Ministry of Industry and New Technologies, Ministry of Health and Social Protection of the Population, and other relevant state bodies;
  • Heads or authorized representatives of the Agency for Hydrometeorology, Agency on Statistics, Committee of Emergency Situations and Civil Defence, Agency for Land Reclamation and Irrigation, Forestry Agency and other interested government agencies;
  • Representatives of the National Academy of Sciences of Tajikistan, private sector, civil society organizations and other stakeholders in an advisory capacity.

IACCC meetings are planned at least twice a year to review NDC 3.0 implementation, achievements, emerging issues and future priorities.

It is proposed that secretariat functions for the IACCC be assigned to the Centre for the Implementation of the Paris Agreement jointly with the Agency for Hydrometeorology of the Committee for Environmental Protection under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan.

11.1.2. Role of Sectoral Agencies

The effectiveness of NDC 3.0 implementation depends not only on structured commitments but also on a clear institutional allocation of responsibilities for planning, implementation, data collection and verification.

Each line ministry and agency is responsible for implementing climate measures in its sector, collecting and reporting data to CEP, and integrating NDC 3.0 climate targets into sectoral strategies and programmes.

The distribution of roles is set out in bilateral cooperation memoranda between CEP and sectoral agencies, whose preparation is envisaged under the NDC 3.0 Implementation Roadmap.

Each agency designates an authorized official responsible for liaison with CEP on NDC matters. The allocation of responsibilities builds on the principles established in Tajikistan’s BTR1 (2024) and is aligned with UNFCCC ETF requirements concerning national inventory and monitoring systems.

Formalizing institutional arrangements establishes CEP’s coordinating role, line ministries’ responsibility for sectoral measures and a mechanism for cooperation through climate focal points.

The principal responsible bodies are:

  • Ministry of Energy and Water Resources: implementing renewable energy and energy-efficiency projects;
  • Ministry of Agriculture: monitoring the implementation of LULUCF measures relating to agricultural land (arable land, pastures and perennial crops) and climate-resilient practices;66
  • Forestry Agency under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan: implementing and monitoring LULUCF measures concerning forests and State Forest Fund land; maintaining forest management and state forest resource records; conducting national forest monitoring; and assessing GHG removals by forests;
  • Agency for Land Reclamation and Irrigation under the Government of the Republic of Tajikistan: reporting on the modernization of pumping stations and irrigation indicators;
  • Ministry of Transport: reporting on the electrification of the vehicle fleet;
  • Ministry of Industry and New Technologies: implementing and monitoring emission reduction measures in the industrial processes and product use (IPPU) sector, and supplying official sectoral data and emissions-intensity indicators for major industrial enterprises (aluminium, cement and other carbon-intensive production).

This framework ensures clear lines of responsibility, reduces the risk of institutional gaps across governance levels, operationalizes the NDC 3.0 Implementation Roadmap and aligns with the national planning system. It integrates climate goals into sectoral strategies and supports continuity of governance in subsequent NDC update cycles (Table 23).

11.1.3. Regional Coordination of Climate Policy

Tajikistan has a two-tier system of subnational administration: the regional level (Sughd Region, Khatlon Region, GBAO and the Districts of Republican Subordination) and the jamoat level.

Local administrative authorities (hukumats) have extensive powers concerning land use, water management and implementation of state programmes locally.

Subnational authorities directly implement a significant proportion of NDC 3.0 adaptation measures. Disaster protection, rehabilitation of degraded land, settlement greening and rural water supply are implemented at hukumat and jamoat levels in coordination with sectoral agencies.

To strengthen the subnational dimension of NDC 3.0, the following measures are envisaged:

  • Developing subnational climate action plans for every region and Dushanbe, aligned with NDC 3.0 targets and local development plan priorities;
  • Training climate coordinators in local administrations (hukumats): officials responsible for liaison with the IACCC and CEP and for local monitoring;
  • Including climate indicators in monitoring systems for local budget expenditure and investment plans;
  • Establishing a digital climate monitoring platform with subnational modules enabling automatic collection and transmission of reporting data to CEP.

Special attention under these mechanisms will be given to GBAO, a mountainous autonomous region with particularly high climate vulnerability, including glacier degradation, landslides and low water availability. Specialized subnational monitoring mechanisms are envisaged for GBAO, involving the Pamir Biological Institute of the National Academy of Sciences.

11.2. Monitoring and Reporting

Tajikistan’s NDC 3.0 monitoring system has been developed in accordance with ETF requirements and international good practices reflected in IPCC guidance.

The M&E system covers monitoring of progress towards mitigation and adaptation targets and tracking climate finance, technology transfer and capacity-building.

11.2.1. Monitoring System

The Republic of Tajikistan is progressively developing its national monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) system for climate action. Improvements include harmonizing inter-agency data flows, strengthening national GHG inventories, developing measures and support tracking, and phased digital integration. Operational elements of the system, achievements and further support needs are reflected in national reporting.

The core components of the MRV system are:

  • National GHG inventory: prepared annually by CEP jointly with Tajikhydromet and line agencies in accordance with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (2019 Refinement);67
  • Mitigation measures tracking system: includes a database of completed and planned climate measures by sector, identifying responsible agencies, financing sources and results achieved;
  • Adaptation measures tracking system: integrates information on adaptation measures under the National Adaptation Strategy through 2030 and subnational climate plans;
  • Climate finance tracking system: maintained by the Ministry of Finance jointly with CEP to tag climate-related state budget expenditures and record incoming international support;
  • National forest and forest carbon monitoring system: being established by the Forestry Agency in cooperation with CEP and Tajikhydromet on the basis of forest management and inventory records, remote sensing data and field measurements, to assess forest cover, forest condition and GHG removals by forests for the GHG inventory and NDC 3.0 reporting.

11.2.2. Data Sources and Quality Control

Data quality control in the NDC 3.0 MRV system operates at three levels: primary data collection (departmental control), data consolidation at CEP (inter-agency verification) and external review (international review under BTR/ETF procedures).

At departmental level, each ministry and agency applies internal data quality procedures: scheduled audits of primary documentation, cross-checking against information from related agencies and retrospective time-series analysis. Quality control procedures for the GHG inventory were developed in accordance with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (2019 Refinement), using Tier 2 and Tier 3 where possible.

At the inter-agency verification level, CEP conducts annual data reconciliation sessions involving departmental climate focal points. Where discrepancies are identified, joint working meetings are held; in disputed cases, data from the Agency on Statistics are treated as authoritative.

External verification is carried out through the BTR Technical Expert Review (TER) under ETF procedures. Tajikistan underwent its first TER in 2024 and considered international experts’ recommendations to improve GHG inventory methodology, particularly for LULUCF and waste.

The quality of climate reporting depends not only on calculation methods but also on the reliability of data sources and the rigour of quality control procedures throughout processing. Systematizing sources and verification procedures allows the origin of each indicator to be traced, its reliability assessed and GHG inventory reproducibility ensured. Multilevel review, including inter-agency reconciliation coordinated by CEP and reliance on Agency on Statistics data in disputed cases, reduces the risk of errors and discrepancies.

11.3. Monitoring Progress in NDC 3.0 Implementation

11.3.1. Progress Tracking Methods

Progress in implementing NDC 3.0 is tracked through an integrated set of mechanisms ensuring consistent, comparable assessment of Tajikistan’s achievement of climate goals at national and international levels.

Progress in fulfilling NDC 3.0 commitments is assessed using three principal methodological approaches: comparing actual GHG emissions with target trajectories under the unconditional and conditional scenarios; monitoring the implementation of specific policies and projects; and evaluating adaptation targets through a system of indicators.

  • Approach 1—GHG target tracking. Annual national GHG inventory data are compared with target trajectories.
  • Approach 2—monitoring of measures. A Climate Measures Register records all sectoral actions and projects, indicating the responsible body, implementation status (planned/under implementation/completed), financing volume and actual results. Annual reports on implementation of the NDC Climate Finance Plan and sectoral programmes enable progress against schedules to be monitored.
  • Approach 3—assessment of adaptation progress. Given the challenges of quantitatively evaluating adaptation outcomes, a multicriteria assessment system is used, including quantitative and qualitative indicators (public climate awareness, quality of subnational climate plans). The methodology is aligned with UNFCCC framework decisions on the global goal on adaptation adopted at COP28.

11.3.2. Linkages to the Inventory of GHG Emissions and Removals

The national GHG inventory is the primary instrument for accounting for progress towards NDC 3.0 mitigation targets. It is compiled in accordance with the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (2019 Refinement) and covers all IPCC sectors: Energy, Industrial Processes and Product Use, Agriculture, LULUCF and Waste.

The links between the inventory and the NDC are as follows:

  • Inventory data for each year provide the basis for assessing the actual emissions trajectory against the NDC 3.0 target trajectory;
  • Inventory methodology is applied consistently to ensure comparability across multi-year time series;
  • Any inventory recalculations are documented and explained in the relevant BTR to ensure time-series transparency;
  • Inventory results are incorporated into CEP’s annual report on NDC 3.0 implementation, including analysis of deviations from the target trajectory.

A major inventory improvement priority is moving to Tier 2 for agriculture (livestock, rice and land management), where country-specific emission factors differ substantially from IPCC defaults.

Under the UNDP/GEF-supported project to improve the national GHG inventory system and prepare national climate reports (including the first Biennial Transparency Report and subsequent reporting, GEF ID 11496), country-specific emission factors for enteric fermentation in ruminants are to be updated to reflect national livestock production characteristics.

As part of the inventory, Tajikhydromet systematically monitors carbon sinks, particularly forests and mountain ecosystems. Forest biomass is monitored using remote sensing jointly with the CATCOS programme. The Forestry Agency supplies data on forests and State Forest Fund land (forest cover, timber and carbon stocks, areas of reforestation and natural regeneration, survival of established plantations) in accordance with its LULUCF responsibilities (Table 23).

11.3.3. Linkages to Biennial Transparency Reports

Biennial Transparency Reports are Tajikistan’s principal reporting instrument on NDC implementation under the Enhanced Transparency Framework. Tajikistan submitted its first Biennial Transparency Report to the UNFCCC in December 2024, including a national inventory of GHG emissions and removals for 1990–2022. Subsequent reports provide regular information on national emissions, progress in implementing and achieving the NDC, and other applicable information in accordance with CMA decisions.

The structural links between NDC 3.0 and the BTR system are as follows:

  • Each BTR includes a dedicated section on “Tracking Progress in Implementing and Achieving the NDC”, comparing actual inventory data and implemented measures against NDC targets;
  • Information on support (finance received, technology transfer and capacity-building) is included in the BTR in accordance with ETF guidance;
  • The UNFCCC’s technical expert review of the BTR assesses the adequacy of the national MRV system; TER findings inform improvements to monitoring arrangements;
  • BTR data feed into the Global Stocktake, a key mechanism for assessing collective progress under the Paris Agreement. The first GST concluded at COP28 (Dubai, 2023), and its outcomes informed the preparation of this NDC 3.0.

The regularity and predictability of BTR reporting are essential to the ambition-raising mechanism because they synchronize national accounting with international milestones under the Paris Agreement. Formalizing the schedule for BTRs and National Communications ensures institutional readiness for regular verification and embeds climate reporting in continuous governance processes.

NDC 3.0 thus establishes a comprehensive institutional coordination, monitoring and transparency framework intended to ensure effective implementation of the Republic of Tajikistan’s climate commitments.

Tajikistan intends to progressively strengthen national capacities for climate data, monitoring and reporting in accordance with IPCC guidance and UNFCCC ETF standards, while recognizing that a sustainable national MRV system will require systematic international technical and financial support from development partners.
